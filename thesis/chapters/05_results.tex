\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

At a high level, semantic entropy is competitive but not a universal winner.
The two semantic-entropy estimators are essentially tied at the top of the
sentence-length comparison; on short-phrase answers the leading methods are
nearly tied, and surface entropy leads under token-F1. Figure~\ref{fig:method-comparison}
puts the recorded methods side by side; the sections that follow show how the
ranking changes with the evaluation choices.

The results below compare uncertainty estimators under the settings named in
each table. Unless stated otherwise, each mean first averages the three
generation seeds within each of the nine dataset--model pairs and then
averages those nine values. A positive AUROC difference favors the first method
in the comparison. Intervals labeled \emph{units} resample those nine pairs;
intervals labeled \emph{hierarchical} also resample records within each cell.

Most values come from the measurement package described in Appendix~\ref{app:schema}
and stored in \texttt{measurements.csv}. The hierarchical intervals and the
out-of-distribution probe rows come from record-level analyses that cannot be
rerun from the exported table alone. Correctness labels come from token-F1 or
an LLM grader; no human labels were collected, and agreement between the two
LLM graders does not establish that either is correct.

Generation seeds also cause variation. Table~\ref{tab:seed-noise} gives the
AUROC range across the three seeds for the five entropy estimators. For
discrete semantic entropy, the mean range is 0.0245; this is the seed-variation
reference used in the figures, not a significance threshold. The same summary
gives a mean range of 0.0473 for P(True), which is not included in the table.

\section{Sentence-length answers}
\label{sec:sentence-length-results}

On sentence-length answers (\texttt{chat\_0shot}) under the Qwen judge at
$T=1.0$, likelihood-weighted semantic entropy reaches 0.758 and the discrete
variant 0.757 on the default entailment model, against 0.736 for the
clustering-free comparator (Table~\ref{tab:sentence-length}). Every comparator
used in the original study falls below both semantic estimators.

all 648 cells at $T=1.0$, likelihood-weighted minus discrete semantic entropy
averages $+0.0015$, the smallest average change in the four-step decomposition.

\section{Short-phrase answers}
\label{sec:short-phrase-results}

On short-phrase answers (\texttt{default\_5shot}) with the default entailment
model, the ordering depends on the correctness rule
(Table~\ref{tab:short-phrase}). Under token-F1, surface entropy leads
discrete semantic entropy by 0.0133. Under the Qwen judge the AUROC difference
is $+0.0005$ and under the Llama judge it is $+0.0027$, in both cases measured
as discrete semantic entropy minus surface entropy; both intervals include
zero.
Naive predictive entropy moves the same way: second-best under token-F1 at
0.789, and last of the three under either judge.

\input{includes/t_short_phrase.tex}

Appendix~\ref{app:complete-method-results} gives the absolute AUROC of all
eight recorded methods for both original prompting conditions, under all three
correctness rules, along with the added conditions and entailment models.

Mean answer length here is 11.2 characters. The semantic-versus-surface
differences under the LLM judges are below 0.003 AUROC, against the
seed-variation reference of 0.0245. Including naive predictive entropy, the three-method spread is
0.011 under the Qwen judge and 0.006 under the Llama judge.

\section{What reproduces from the original study}
\label{sec:reproduction}

Table~\ref{tab:reproduction} in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction-scorecard} compares selected findings from the original study
with the measurements here. The reimplementation follows the released
procedure, but the datasets, models, and grading rules differ. This tests
whether the reported patterns hold in the current setting, not whether an
exact repeat gives the same results.

These results broadly agree with the published patterns. The semantic entropy
probe comparison is only qualitative: Kossen et al. evaluate transfer to
held-out tasks, while the six comparisons here use in-distribution probes.
However, the P(True) model-size
trend does not reproduce, although two sizes from one model family provide only
a limited test. The answer-length difference is partly related to
the different token limits inherited from the released code. The
reimplementation also retains two defects from that code: token-F1 grading and
the TriviaQA split contamination documented in Appendix~\ref{app:dedup}.

The original study is often read as showing that grouping answers by meaning
produces the improvement. Its design does not isolate that step.
Section~\ref{sec:se-vs-surface} adds the required comparison.

\section{Separating the steps of the method}
\label{sec:se-vs-surface}

\subsection{The four steps}
\label{sec:ladder}

The comparison separates the method into four consecutive changes.
Naive predictive entropy reads sequence likelihoods without grouping. Naive
sample entropy counts raw strings. Surface entropy counts them after
normalization. Discrete semantic entropy groups the samples by meaning, and the
likelihood-weighted estimator weights those groups by likelihood.

The third step needs one qualification. Following the released code, the
clustering is run on the raw answers, while surface entropy counts the
normalized ones, so that step changes two things at once: how two answers are
judged to mean the same thing, and whether the text was cleaned up first.
Normalizing merges variants such as different capitalization and punctuation,
and the entailment model may group those variants differently when it sees the
raw text. The comparison therefore does not isolate semantic grouping
perfectly, and this study does not measure the direction of that effect on
AUROC.

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} give the change at each
step over all 24 comparisons.

\input{includes/t_ladder.tex}

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ladder.pdf}
\caption{What each step adds, measured against the step below it over all 24
  comparisons. The dashed line is the seed-variation reference, which is the AUROC range
  produced by rerunning the same configuration under a different sampling seed. Only the
  normalization step clears it.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

One step is clear. Normalizing the answer strings before counting them adds
$0.0398$ AUROC on average. The difference is positive and its interval excludes
zero in all 24 comparisons. It is also the only step whose AUROC difference
exceeds the seed-variation reference of 0.0245.

The other three steps are less consistent. Counting raw strings instead of
reading sequence log-likelihoods gives a positive AUROC difference in only 4 of
24 comparisons, while sequence-likelihood entropy performs better with an interval
that excludes zero in 8. The four positive differences are large enough to
outweigh the other twenty, giving the positive overall mean of $0.0067$;
that mean does not imply an improvement in most comparisons.
The claim that counting answer strings beats reading
log-likelihoods therefore holds only after the strings are normalized.
Grouping samples by meaning adds $0.0016$ AUROC over normalized-string counting
on average and is positive in 14 of 24 comparisons; seven intervals favor
normalized-string counting instead. Weighting the meaning groups by likelihood
adds $0.0015$ AUROC on average.

The rest of this section examines the third step, the one the method is named
after.

\subsection{The default entailment model under LLM grading}

Crossing the four prompting conditions with the two LLM correctness judges on
\texttt{deberta-v2-xlarge-mnli} gives eight comparisons, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:canonical-eight} with AUROC differences and intervals from the
hierarchical paired bootstrap. The differences run
from $-0.0020$ to $+0.0223$, with two-sided $p$ from 0.36 to 0.94. Not one of
the eight intervals excludes zero.

\input{includes/t_canonical_eight.tex}

\subsection{The resampling scheme changes the answer}

Table~\ref{tab:resampling-schemes} reports the same 24 comparisons under the
three schemes in Section~\ref{sec:statistics}. When only records are resampled,
18 intervals exclude zero and their mean width is 0.0153. When both units and
records are resampled, only 11 intervals exclude zero and their mean width is
0.0467, about three times wider. Seven apparent differences therefore
disappear. Of the eleven that remain, four favor semantic entropy and seven
favor surface entropy. Resampling units alone identifies the same eleven,
showing that variation between units causes the change.

\input{includes/t_resampling_schemes.tex}

\subsection{Pattern across all twenty-four comparisons}

Across the eleven hierarchical intervals that exclude zero, four favor
semantic entropy and seven favor surface entropy. The four that favor semantic
entropy all use the Qwen entailment model. The seven that favor surface
entropy all use token-F1 correctness labels. On the twelve comparisons that use
a released cross-encoder with an LLM correctness judge, no interval excludes
zero. Appendix~\ref{app:all-comparisons} gives the complete table, forest plot,
and comparison-level checks.

\section{Semantic entropy against the original's baselines}
\label{sec:paper-baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:paper-baselines} gives the AUROC difference between discrete
semantic entropy and each comparator used in the original study. Against
P(True) the mean difference is $+0.025$ over six comparisons, with a range of
$[+0.010, +0.042]$. Against the semantic
entropy probe it is $+0.051$, range $[+0.031, +0.080]$, and against the
accuracy probe $+0.049$, range $[+0.033, +0.084]$. Semantic entropy leads all
three in every comparison where each was measured.

\input{includes/t_paper_baselines.tex}

The semantic-entropy comparator uses the default entailment model for P(True)
and \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large} for the probes. These baseline contrasts
therefore do not hold semantic entropy's entailment model fixed. P(True)
itself does not use clustering.

Table~\ref{tab:ptrue-size} reports P(True) by generator size. The original
suggests the method ``seems to improve with model size''. Within the Llama
family the ordering here is flat or inverted: 0.711 for the 70B model against
0.726 for the 8B in \texttt{chat\_0shot}, and 0.819 against 0.826 in
\texttt{default\_5shot}. Two sizes in one family is a weak test of a trend the
original observes across three families.

\label{sec:clustering-sensitivity}

Surface entropy is 0.7364 under all three because it does not use an entailment

\section{Sampling temperature}
\label{sec:temperature-sensitivity}

The sweep under LLM grading appears in Table~\ref{tab:temperature} and
Figure~\ref{fig:temperature}. The labels for the original graded answers are
joined by \texttt{prompt\_id} and remain identical at all five temperatures.

\input{includes/t_temperature.tex}

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{temperature.pdf}
\caption{AUROC against sampling temperature, sentence-length answers, default
  entailment model, Qwen judge. The correctness labels are held fixed across the five
  temperatures, while the uncertainty samples change.}
\label{fig:temperature}
\end{figure}

Discrete semantic entropy falls from 0.757 at $T=1.0$ to 0.568 at $T=0.1$, and
surface entropy from 0.736 to 0.589. Naive predictive entropy, which reads
sequence likelihoods rather than spread across samples, moves from 0.694 to
0.644. At $T=0.1$ it leads both diversity-based estimators; at $T=1.0$ it
trails both. The crossing lies between $T=0.1$ and $T=0.3$ for surface entropy
and between $T=0.3$ and $T=0.5$ for discrete semantic entropy.

Appendix~\ref{app:complete-temperature-results} reports all five entropy
methods for both prompting conditions, both entailment models, and both LLM
graders. No token-F1 figure and no answer-length figure is quoted from a sweep
row, for the reason in Section~\ref{sec:temperature-sweep}.

\section{The correctness rule}
\label{sec:correctness-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:correctness} grades identical answers three ways. In
\texttt{chat\_0shot} the token-overlap rule scores the generators at 0.344 mean
accuracy against 0.623 under the Qwen judge and 0.677 under the Llama judge.
The semantic-minus-surface AUROC difference in that condition is $-0.0506$ under
token-F1, $+0.0204$ under the Qwen judge and $+0.0223$ under the Llama judge:
the same answers, the same scores, and a change of sign.

\input{includes/t_correctness.tex}

The gap narrows as answers shorten. In \texttt{default\_5shot}, at 11.2 mean
characters, token-F1 accuracy is 0.610 against 0.621 and 0.662 under the two
judges.

\paragraph{Cells where token-F1 labels almost nothing correct.}
Table~\ref{tab:degenerate-cells} lists the six cells of \texttt{chat\_0shot}
whose token-F1 accuracy falls below 0.05. Three are TriviaQA with
Llama-3.1-8B, at 0.0106, 0.0079 and 0.0079. That is three or four correct
answers out of 378. An AUROC based on only three or four correct examples is not a stable
measurement, and these cells carry much of the token-F1 AUROC difference in this
condition. Excluding the six cells outside the accuracy range $[0.05, 0.95]$,
which is two of the nine units, moves the Qwen-entailment-model token-F1 difference from
$-0.0658$ to $-0.0277$, while the default-entailment-model difference moves only from
$-0.0506$ to $-0.0485$. Dropping the TriviaQA and Llama-3.1-8B unit alone gives
$-0.0330$ and $-0.0440$. No LLM-graded short-answer cell falls below 0.36
accuracy; the lowest is 0.3625.
