\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

The main comparison asks how well semantic entropy detects incorrect answers,
how it ranks against other methods, and when it performs better. The two
semantic-entropy estimators are essentially tied at the top of the
sentence-length comparison. On short-phrase answers the leading methods are
nearly tied, and surface entropy leads under token-F1.
Figures~\ref{fig:method-comparison} and~\ref{fig:method-comparison-short}
compare all eight methods within each dataset and model.

The chapter first reports the headline comparisons, then separates answer
length from prompting and task effects. It next examines the estimator steps,
and finally tests the entailment model, temperature, and correctness rule.
This order distinguishes what the method achieves from the choices that change
the measurement.

The results below compare uncertainty estimators under the settings named in
each table. Unless stated otherwise, each mean first averages the three
generation seeds within each of the nine dataset--generator pairs (one dataset
and one generating model) and then averages those nine values. A \emph{cell} is
one dataset--generator--seed combination. A positive AUROC difference favors
the first method in the comparison. Intervals labeled \emph{units} resample
the nine pairs. Intervals labeled \emph{hierarchical} resample those pairs
and then resample question records within each selected cell. Section~\ref{sec:statistics}
defines these schemes and their assumptions.

Generation seeds also cause variation. Table~\ref{tab:seed-noise} gives the
AUROC range across the three seeds for the five entropy estimators and P(True). For
discrete semantic entropy, the mean range is 0.0245. This is the seed-variation
reference used in the figures, not a significance threshold. P(True) has a
larger mean range of 0.0473.

\section{Sentence-length answers}
\label{sec:sentence-length-results}

The sentence-length condition (\texttt{chat\_0shot}) asks for a brief, complete
sentence without worked examples (Section~\ref{sec:conditions}). Under the Qwen judge at
$T=1.0$, likelihood-weighted semantic entropy reaches 0.758 and the discrete
variant 0.757 on the default entailment model
(\texttt{deberta-v2-xlarge-mnli}), against 0.736 for surface entropy, which
counts distinct answers after normalizing them and never consults an
entailment model (Table~\ref{tab:sentence-length}).
Both semantic estimators outperform the measured baselines from
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\input{includes/t_sentence_length.tex}

The two semantic estimators differ by 0.0017, well below the seed range. Over
all 648 cells at $T=1.0$, likelihood-weighted minus discrete semantic entropy
averages $+0.0015$, the smallest average change in the four-step decomposition.

\section{Short-phrase answers}
\label{sec:short-phrase-results}

The short-phrase condition (\texttt{default\_5shot}) asks for the briefest
possible answer and supplies five worked examples. With the default entailment
model, the ordering depends on the correctness rule
(Table~\ref{tab:short-phrase}). Under token-F1, surface entropy leads
discrete semantic entropy by 0.0133. Under the Qwen judge the AUROC difference
is $+0.0005$ and under the Llama judge it is $+0.0027$, in both cases measured
as discrete semantic entropy minus surface entropy. Both intervals include
zero.
Naive predictive entropy moves the same way: second-best under token-F1 at
0.789, and last of the three under either judge.

Answers average 11.2 characters. Under the LLM judges, discrete semantic and
surface entropy differ by less than 0.003 AUROC. Even including naive predictive
entropy, the highest and lowest AUROCs differ by only 0.011 under the Qwen judge
and 0.006 under the Llama judge, compared with the seed-variation reference of
0.0245.

\section{Answer length}
\label{sec:answer-length-results}

Table~\ref{tab:answer-length} compares the four prompting conditions under the
Qwen correctness judge at $T=1.0$, using the default entailment model.
The sentence-length condition produces 255.2 characters on average, compared
with 11.2 in the short-phrase condition. Discrete semantic entropy's AUROC
advantage over naive predictive entropy is correspondingly larger: $+0.0628$
against $+0.0110$. The likelihood-weighted estimator gives the same ordering,
with advantages of $+0.0645$ and $+0.0110$.

\input{includes/t_answer_length.tex}

The two added conditions fill in the comparison. With the brevity instruction
and no examples (\texttt{default\_0shot}), answers average 69.8 characters and
the discrete-semantic-minus-naive difference is $+0.0326$. With the sentence
instruction and five examples (\texttt{chat\_5shot}), answers average only
11.4 characters and the difference is $+0.0094$. The condition names describe
the prompts. They do not guarantee the length of the generated answers.

Against surface entropy, the corresponding differences are smaller, from
$-0.0020$ to $+0.0204$. None of the four hierarchical intervals for this

of this length pattern and its relation to Farquhar et al.\ are discussed in

\section{Long-form biography answers}
\label{sec:longform-results}

The long-form biography evaluation asks each generator to write a biography paragraph rather
than a short answer. Each of the three generators is run with three seeds on
500 biography prompts. The Qwen judge assigns a single reference-consistency
verdict to one selected paragraph for each prompt.

For AUROC, each prompt contributes one uncertainty score and one correctness
label. The label is a whole-paragraph verdict against a Wikipedia introduction,
while semantic entropy is computed over ten sampled paragraphs. The evaluation
uses one correctness judge.

Table~\ref{tab:longform} shows higher recorded AUROC for meaning-based grouping
with the larger entailment models in this setting. Discrete semantic entropy
reaches 0.670 with the large DeBERTa entailment model and 0.708 with the Qwen
entailment model. Surface entropy and naive sample entropy remain near chance
at 0.497. These string-based methods do not use an entailment model, so their
results are unchanged across entailment settings. When all ten samples form
distinct string groups, each group has the same frequency and the string-based
entropy is constant. For both larger entailment models, the same higher score
appears across the three generators in Table~\ref{tab:longform-by-generator}
and Figure~\ref{fig:longform-comparison}. Every records-only interval for these
generator-level comparisons excludes zero. The two smaller entailment settings
behave differently. Exact matching places every differing paragraph in its own
group after normalization, so it equals surface entropy here. Raw-string
naive sample entropy differs slightly; both string baselines are nearly saturated. The base model
exceeds the string baselines for Mistral-7B, at 0.628 against 0.500, but falls
below them for both Llama generators, so it does not show the advantage
consistently.

\input{includes/t_longform.tex}

With the two larger entailment models, the semantic-grouping score is higher
for paragraphs rejected by the reference-based judge. Specific facts absent
from the reference are rejected even if they may be true elsewhere; vague
responses without a contradiction can pass. The verdict measures support
from that reference, not independently verified factual truth.

The largest recorded margin over surface entropy occurs in this biography
setting. Answer length is one possible explanation, but the task, prompts, and
grading target also differ from Section~\ref{sec:answer-length-results}. The pooled gap is $+0.174$
with large DeBERTa, with a records-only 95\,\% interval of $[+0.157,+0.190]$,
and $+0.212$ with Qwen, with an interval of $[+0.196,+0.228]$. These intervals
hold generators and seeds fixed and are narrower in scope than the
short-answer hierarchical intervals.

\section{Separating the steps of the method}
\label{sec:se-vs-surface}

\subsection{The four steps}
\label{sec:ladder}

The comparison separates the method into four consecutive changes.
Naive predictive entropy reads sequence likelihoods without grouping. Naive
sample entropy counts raw strings. Surface entropy counts them after
normalization. Discrete semantic entropy groups the samples by meaning, and the
likelihood-weighted estimator weights those groups by likelihood.

Following the released code, the
clustering is run on the raw answers, while surface entropy counts the
normalized ones, so that step changes two things at once: how two answers are
judged to mean the same thing, and whether the text was cleaned up first.
Normalizing merges variants such as different capitalization and punctuation,
and the entailment model may group those variants differently when it sees the
raw text. The comparison does not isolate semantic grouping perfectly. The
fixed-subset check in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} measures how often the
partition changes after normalization and how much the score varies.

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} give the change at each
step over all 24 configuration entries.

\input{includes/t_ladder.tex}

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ladder.pdf}
\caption{What each step adds, measured against the step below it over all 24
  configuration entries. The dashed line is the seed-variation reference, which is the AUROC range
  produced by rerunning the same configuration under a different sampling seed. Only the
  normalization step clears it.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

One step is clear. Normalizing the answer strings before counting them adds
$0.0398$ AUROC on average. The difference is positive and its interval excludes
zero in all 24 configuration entries. Because the two string scores do not
use entailment, these entries repeat 12 distinct condition--grader contrasts;
all 12 are positive. The mean weights the two original prompting conditions
three times each and the added conditions once each. Giving the 12 distinct
contrasts equal weight yields $+0.0372$ AUROC. Normalization is also the only step whose AUROC difference
exceeds the seed-variation reference of 0.0245.

The other three steps are less consistent. Counting raw strings instead of
reading sequence log-likelihoods gives a positive AUROC difference in only 4 of
24 comparisons, while sequence-likelihood entropy performs better with an interval
that excludes zero in 8. The four positive differences are large enough to
outweigh the other twenty, giving the positive overall mean of $0.0067$.
That mean does not imply an improvement in most comparisons.
The claim that counting answer strings beats reading
log-likelihoods therefore holds only after the strings are normalized.
Grouping samples by meaning adds $0.0016$ AUROC over \mbox{normalized-string} counting
on average and is positive in 14 of 24 comparisons, while seven intervals favor
normalized-string counting instead. Weighting the meaning groups by likelihood
adds $0.0015$ AUROC on average.

\subsection{The resampling scheme changes the answer}

Table~\ref{tab:resampling-schemes} reports the same 24 comparisons under the
three schemes in Section~\ref{sec:statistics}. When only records are resampled,
18 intervals exclude zero and their mean width is 0.0153. When both units and
records are resampled, only 11 intervals exclude zero and their mean width is
0.0467, about three times wider. Seven apparent differences therefore
disappear. Of the eleven that remain, four favor semantic entropy and seven
favor surface entropy. Resampling units alone identifies the same eleven,
showing that variation between units causes the change.

\input{includes/t_resampling_schemes.tex}

\subsection{Pattern across all twenty-four comparisons}

Across the eleven hierarchical intervals that exclude zero, four favor
semantic entropy and seven favor surface entropy. The four that favor semantic
entropy all use the Qwen entailment model. The seven that favor surface
entropy all use token-F1 correctness labels. On the twelve comparisons that use
a released cross-encoder with an LLM correctness judge, no interval excludes
zero. Appendix~\ref{app:all-comparisons} gives all 24 paired differences and
intervals.

\section{Sampling temperature}
\label{sec:temperature-sensitivity}

The sweep under LLM grading appears in Table~\ref{tab:temperature} and
Figure~\ref{fig:temperature}. The answers being graded and the decisions about
whether they are correct remain the same at all five temperatures. Only the
samples used to estimate uncertainty change.

\input{includes/t_temperature.tex}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{temperature.pdf}

  decisions stay fixed while the samples used to estimate uncertainty change.}

Discrete semantic entropy falls from 0.757 at $T=1.0$ to 0.568 at $T=0.1$, and

and between $T=0.3$ and $T=0.5$ for discrete semantic entropy.

\section{Changing the grader changes the ranking}
\label{sec:grading-results}

The method ranking reverses without generating a single new answer. For
sentence-length answers with the default entailment model, surface entropy
leads discrete semantic entropy by 0.0506 AUROC under token-F1. With Qwen or
Llama grading, semantic entropy instead leads by 0.0204 or 0.0223. The answers
and uncertainty scores are identical. Only the decisions about which answers
count as correct change. Both LLM judges give the same mean ordering, although
their confidence intervals include zero (Table~\ref{tab:all-slices}).

The graders also give very different estimates of answer accuracy. Token-F1
accepts 34.4\,\% of the sentence-length answers, compared with 62.3\,\% under
Qwen and 67.7\,\% under Llama. For short phrases, the estimates are much closer:
61.0\,\%, 62.1\,\%, and 66.2\,\%, respectively. The grading choice therefore
matters most in the condition where answers contain more than a brief phrase.
Section~\ref{sec:correctness} defines the three grading rules.

An extreme case occurs on TriviaQA with Llama-3.1-8B. Across the three
sentence-length runs, token-F1 accepts only three or four of 378 answers per
run. AUROC then has very few correct answers against which to compare the
incorrect ones. Yet these extreme cases do not fully explain the ranking:
excluding all six runs with token-F1 accuracy below 5\,\% leaves surface
entropy's advantage at 0.0485 AUROC with the default entailment model, compared
with 0.0506 before exclusion.

The result is a change in how the detectors are evaluated, even though neither
detector's scores change. Appendix~\ref{sec:correctness-comparison} gives the
accuracy comparisons and exclusion checks in full.

\section{Summary of comparisons}
\label{sec:secondary-results-overview}

The comparisons answer two different questions. The original baseline tests
rank complete estimators, while the decomposition examines which steps add
value. The entailment-model and grading checks change that ranking on identical
answers; the temperature sweep changes the samples used to estimate uncertainty.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} explains what these distinctions imply for the
value of semantic grouping.
