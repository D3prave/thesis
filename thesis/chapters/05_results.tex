\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports what was measured. Interpretation waits for
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}. Every table here except the reproduction scorecard
of Table~\ref{tab:reproduction}, which is written out by hand, is generated from
the measurement package by one script. Two sets of rows are the exception to
that: the hierarchical intervals and the out-of-distribution probe figures are
quoted from two record-level scripts that the published package cannot rerun.

\section{How to read the numbers}
\label{sec:how-to-read}

Three conventions apply throughout, and each changes what a margin means. A
fourth, on the two rejection-accuracy metrics, is set out in
Section~\ref{sec:aurac-disagreement}.

\paragraph{Nine units, not twenty-seven cells.} An aggregate figure is the mean
over the nine independent (dataset, model) units of the mean over that unit's
three seeds. Intervals labelled \emph{units} resample those nine units, 2{,}000
times. Intervals labelled \emph{hierarchical} resample the units and then the
records inside each drawn cell.

\paragraph{The backend is always named.} Discrete semantic entropy ranges from
0.751 to 0.823 on the same set of generations, depending only on which model
judges entailment. A semantic-entropy number without a backend attached is not
well defined, and none is quoted here.

\paragraph{The noise floor.} Table~\ref{tab:seed-noise} gives the AUROC range
across the three run seeds: 0.024 to 0.030 for the entropy estimators, 0.047
for P(True). Where a single figure is quoted below, it is $0.025$, the range for
discrete semantic entropy itself, which is the estimator the comparisons are
about. A smaller unpaired margin is inside the variation from resampling alone.

\section{Decomposing the method: what each step is worth}
\label{sec:se-vs-surface}

\subsection{The four steps}
\label{sec:ladder}

Semantic entropy differs from the crudest string baseline in four steps, and
each can be isolated because neighbouring steps differ in essentially one way.
Naive predictive entropy reads sequence likelihoods without grouping. Naive
sample entropy counts raw strings. Surface entropy counts them after
normalization. Discrete semantic entropy groups the samples by meaning, and the
full estimator weights those groups by likelihood.

The third step needs one qualification. Following the released code, the
clustering is run on the raw answers, while surface entropy counts the
normalized ones. So that step changes two things at the same time: how two
answers are judged to mean the same thing, and whether the text was cleaned up
first.

It is worth saying which way that cuts. Cleaning up an answer merges variants
like different capitalization and punctuation. If the entailment model does not
also merge them, then the clustering step gets \emph{less} credit than it
deserves, not more. The imperfection can therefore only understate the value of
that step, never manufacture one. In practice the model does merge most of these
variants, but the comparison is not perfectly clean, and the margins below should
be read with that in mind.
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} give what each step adds
over all 24 slices.

\input{includes/t_ladder.tex}

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ladder.pdf}
\caption{What each step adds, measured against the step below it over all 24
  slices. The dashed line is the seed noise floor, which is the AUROC range
  produced by rerunning the same configuration under a different sampling seed. Only the
  normalization step clears it.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

One step is unambiguous. Normalizing the answer strings before counting them is
worth $+0.0398$ AUROC, positive in all 24 slices and resolved in all 24, and it
is the only step whose margin exceeds the seed noise floor of 0.025.

The other three are not. Counting raw strings instead of reading sequence
log-likelihoods is positive in only 4 slices of 24 and resolves against raw
counting in 8. So the familiar claim that counting answer strings beats
reading log-likelihoods holds only for \emph{normalized} strings. Grouping the
samples by meaning adds $+0.0016$ over counting normalized strings, positive in
14 of 24 and resolved against in 7. Weighting the resulting groups by likelihood adds $+0.0015$.

The rest of this section examines the third step, the one the method is named
after.

\subsection{The resampling scheme changes the answer}

Table~\ref{tab:resampling-schemes} reports the same 24 slices under the three
schemes of Section~\ref{sec:statistics}. Resampling records alone resolves 18
of 24 slices at a mean interval width of 0.0153. Resampling both units and

\subsection{All twenty-four slices}

Table~\ref{tab:all-slices} and Figure~\ref{fig:margin-forest} give every slice.
The eleven resolved ones are not scattered. All four favoring semantic entropy
are Qwen-backend slices: $+0.0867$ and $+0.0872$ in \texttt{chat\_0shot} under
the two judges, and $+0.0119$ and $+0.0139$ in \texttt{default\_5shot}. All
seven favoring surface entropy are token-F1 slices, across all four conditions
and all three backends.

The last two of those four are smaller than the 0.025 seed floor, which was
given earlier as the scale for judging a margin. That is not a contradiction.
The floor describes how much a \emph{single} method's AUROC moves when the
sampling seed changes. A paired test compares two methods on the same generated
answers, so most of that movement affects both of them equally and cancels out
when one is subtracted from the other. The floor is the right check for a
single number on its own; the interval is the right check for a difference
between two methods. Both of these margins are small, and neither carries an
argument by itself.

\input{includes/t_all_slices.tex}

\section{The long-form arm}
\label{sec:longform-results}

Table~\ref{tab:longform} gives the nine long-form cells, 500 biographies each,
under claim-level grading by the Qwen judge, with mean claim accuracy 0.375.

\input{includes/t_longform.tex}

Surface entropy scores 0.4965 and naive sample entropy 0.4973 under every
backend. These are not backend-invariant by coincidence, and they are not
measurements of a weak detector. Ten sampled biographies are never
string-identical, so every sample forms its own cluster, the score is constant
within a cell, and the AUROC is chance up to ties. No comparison against them
is informative here.

What the arm does support is a comparison between backends. Across the three
entailment backends the spread is 0.184 AUROC: 0.5245 under
\texttt{nli-deberta-v3-base}, 0.6703 under \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large} and
0.7084 under \texttt{Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct}. Exact match sits at 0.4965, which
widens the range to 0.212, but it is the degenerate baseline just described and
is not a fourth reading of the same choice. The canonical backend was never
applied to this arm.

The 0.5245 for \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-base} is a result about this study's
clustering rule, not about that model's ability to judge paragraph entailment.
As Section~\ref{sec:longform-design} records, this study sets
\texttt{strict\_entailment} in every arm while the original's paragraph result
uses the relaxed default, and a strict rule applied with a lower-recall model
over-splits.

\section{Probes in and out of distribution}
\label{sec:probe-results}

Table~\ref{tab:probes} gives the two hidden-state probes on
\texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large}, the backend whose clustering supplied the
semantic entropy probe's training target, in and out of distribution.

In distribution, on sentence-length answers, the accuracy probe leads the
semantic entropy probe by 0.046 and 0.042 under the two judges, though under
token-F1 the ordering reverses. On short-phrase answers the semantic entropy
probe leads under both judges.

\input{includes/t_probes.tex}

Out of distribution, meaning trained on the pooled prompts of the other two
short-answer tasks and scored on the held-out one, the accuracy probe reaches
0.625 on sentence-length answers and the semantic entropy probe 0.615.
On short-phrase answers the ordering reverses, at 0.642 against 0.668.
Comparing each probe against its own in-distribution value under the same
grader, the four losses are 0.094 and 0.061 on sentence-length answers and
0.105 and 0.078 on short phrases. The mean is 0.084 and the range 0.061 to
0.105. Under the grader the out-of-distribution pass used, the accuracy
probe's advantage of 0.042 in the first condition falls to 0.010.

Two limits apply. Both probes are trained and evaluated on disjoint question
sets in both regimes, so these are held-out figures throughout. But the
out-of-distribution pass ran under one grader per condition. It used the Llama
judge for sentence-length answers and token-F1 for short phrases. So the two
out-of-distribution rows are not graded alike and should not be compared with
each other directly.
