\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports what the experiments found. It is written to be
readable without prior knowledge of the method: every table and figure
is introduced in plain words before any numbers are discussed, and a set
of worked, by-hand examples (Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits}) shows what
the detector actually does on individual questions before the
aggregate scores are presented.

\section{How to read the numbers}
\label{sec:how-to-read}

The task in every experiment is the same. The language model answers a
question; sometimes it is right and sometimes it is wrong. A
\emph{detector} looks at the answer (or at several sampled answers) and
outputs a single number that is meant to be high when the answer is
unreliable. A good detector gives higher numbers to the wrong answers
than to the right ones, so that a user could decline the riskiest
answers and keep the safe ones.

Two summary measures are used throughout.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{AUROC} (area under the ROC curve). Pick one wrong answer
    and one right answer at random; the AUROC is the probability that
    the detector scored the wrong one higher. \textbf{0.5 means the
    detector is useless} (no better than a coin flip), and \textbf{1.0
    means it is perfect}. Values around 0.7--0.9 indicate moderate-to-strong
    ranking in this study; practical usefulness depends on the operating
    threshold and the relative costs of missed and false alerts.
    Because AUROC only depends on the \emph{ranking} of scores, the unit
    of the entropy (nats vs.\ bits) does not affect it.
  \item \textbf{AURAC} (area under the rejection--accuracy curve). If the
    answers the detector is most worried about are discarded, the
    accuracy of what remains should go up. AURAC summarizes how quickly
    accuracy improves as flagged answers are discarded; higher is better.
    Unlike AUROC, it also depends on how accurate the model was to begin
    with, so AURAC values are only comparable within the same task and
    model.
\end{itemize}

A small worked example makes both metrics concrete. Suppose a detector
scores five answers, of which three are correct (C) and two are wrong
(W), as follows:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Answer & \#1 & \#2 & \#3 & \#4 & \#5 \\
\midrule
Detector score & 0.10 & 0.20 & 0.25 & 0.30 & 0.90 \\
Actually & C & C & W & C & W \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{AUROC by hand.} Form every (wrong, correct) pair; there are
$2 \times 3 = 6$. The wrong answer gets the higher score in five of the
six pairs; the only failure is the pair (\#3, \#4), where the wrong
answer \#3 ($0.25$) scores below the correct answer \#4 ($0.30$). So
AUROC $= 5/6 \approx 0.83$: a good but imperfect ranking.
\emph{AURAC by hand.} Start from the raw accuracy, $3/5 = 0.60$. Reject
the highest-scoring answer (\#5, wrong): accuracy of the rest rises to
$3/4 = 0.75$. Reject the next (\#4, correct): accuracy dips to
$2/3 \approx 0.67$, because the detector discarded a good answer. Reject
the next (\#3, wrong): accuracy reaches $2/2 = 1.00$. Rejecting \#2
leaves the correct answer \#1, so the final non-empty retained set also has
accuracy $1.00$. The rejection--accuracy curve is therefore the sequence
$0.60, 0.75, 0.67, 1.00, 1.00$. Under the implementation's stepwise
definition, AURAC is the mean over these five non-empty retention levels,
$0.803$. Every table in this chapter uses these two metrics, and
for both of them \emph{higher is better}; bold entries mark the best
value in each column (or row, where the caption says so).

Throughout, the positive class is the \emph{incorrect} answer, so
``higher score $=$ more likely wrong''. All headline numbers are
averaged over three independent runs (random seeds 42, 43, 44); where a
spread is shown it is the standard deviation across those three runs.
The detectors compared are the ones defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Because their names are easy to mix
up, Table~\ref{tab:detector-key} lists every detector name that appears
in the result tables, with a one-line reminder of what it does and
where it is defined. The naming convention is simple: ``surface'' and
``naive'' scores look only at answer strings, ``semantic'' scores group
answers by meaning first, and the parenthetical says what is counted or
weighted.

\section{The headline picture}
\label{sec:headline}

Figure~\ref{fig:regime-split} summarizes the main result of the thesis, and
the rest of the chapter works through it in detail. It compares two
detectors across the four tasks and three models: the \emph{cheap}
surface score, which just counts how many of the sampled answers are
different strings, and the \emph{meaning-aware} semantic entropy, which
first groups answers that mean the same thing and then measures the
spread over meanings.

The pattern is the same for all three models, and it has three parts. On
short answers the two bars are nearly equal, so the expensive meaning-based
machinery yields little measurable improvement over simply noticing that the
model wrote different strings. On whole-paragraph biographies the cheap
baseline drops to the chance line while the meaning-aware score survives ---
but it drops there because it is pinned at its ceiling and has no variance
left, which makes it a degenerate opponent rather than a beaten one
(Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}). The third group is the one that settles the
question. Scoring the same biographies one claim at a time gives the surface
baseline short strings to work with again, so it ranks usefully rather than
sitting at its ceiling --- and the meaning-aware score still leads it, in every
cell. \emph{Meaning-based clustering is close to redundant on short answers,
and earns its cost on long-form text at a granularity where the comparison is
fair.} The remainder of the chapter establishes this quantitatively and
explains why it happens.

\begin{figure*}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{regime_split.png}
\caption{Surface-form versus meaning-aware detection across tasks
  (AUROC, higher is better; the dashed line is the ``coin-flip''
  level of 0.5). On the three short-answer tasks the two scores are
  almost the same height: the cheap string-counting baseline is already
  as good as meaning-aware clustering. On whole-paragraph biographies the

  is therefore a diagnostic rather than a fair contest
  (Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}). Scored one claim at a time, the surface

  leads it by $0.07$ to $0.11$ AUROC in every cell
  (Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}). That is the one regime in this study
  where meaning-based clustering beats a baseline that is genuinely competing.
  Each pair of bars is a comparison within one group; no bar is compared
  across groups. The three regimes are graded differently --- against
  reference answers, an LLM judge, and published human labels respectively ---
  so their absolute heights are not commensurable.}
\label{fig:regime-split}
\end{figure*}

\section{Worked examples: what the detector sees}
\label{sec:hand-audits}

Before the aggregate tables, it helps to look at three real records from
the recorded runs (canonical configuration, temperature $1.0$). Each one
shows the ten sampled answers, how the meaning-based clustering grouped
them, and the resulting scores. These by-hand audits make concrete what
the averages later summarize. Appendix~\ref{app:gallery}
extends them with further recorded cluster examples, including the
characteristic failure cases (over-merges, under-merges, and a
confidently wrong answer that clustering makes \emph{worse}).

\subsection{Example 1: meaning-based grouping lowers a falsely high
score}
\label{sec:audit-merge}

\textbf{Question (TriviaQA, Llama-3.1-8B):} ``According to Rudyard
Kipling what were the `two imposters' to meet and treat the same day?''
\textbf{Gold answer:} \emph{Triumph and Disaster}. The ten sampled
answers were:

\begin{quote}\small
\emph{Success and Failure.} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Triumph and Disaster}
$\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Triumph and defeat.} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Triumph and
disaster.} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Triumph and defeat.} $\;\cdot\;$
\emph{Success and Failure} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Success and Failure.}
$\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Triumph and Disaster} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Tyrant and a
dreamer.} $\;\cdot\;$ \emph{Success and Failure.}
\end{quote}

The surface baseline sees many \emph{different strings} (``Triumph and
Disaster'', ``Triumph and defeat.'', ``Success and Failure'', and so on)
and therefore reports high uncertainty: surface entropy $1.28$ nats. The
meaning-based step asks, for each pair, whether the two phrases say the
same thing in context; it judges the various ``triumph/defeat'' and
``success/failure'' phrasings to be mutually entailing and collapses
nine of the ten samples into a single meaning, leaving only the odd
``Tyrant and a dreamer'' on its own. The semantic entropy is then only
$0.33$ nats, a much more honest reflection of the fact that the model
basically settled on one idea. The answer was graded correct. This is
the mechanism by which clustering is \emph{supposed} to help: it removes
uncertainty that is only about wording.

\subsection{Example 2: a confident wrong answer the method cannot
catch}
\label{sec:audit-artifact}

\textbf{Question (TriviaQA, Llama-3.1-8B):} ``Who was the director of the
CIA from 1976--81?'' \textbf{Gold answer:} \emph{George Bush}. All ten
samples gave the same answer, \emph{Stansfield Turner} (differing only
in a trailing period). Because every sample
agrees, the spread over meanings is zero (semantic entropy $0.00$), so
the detector reports maximum confidence, yet the answer is graded
incorrect.

This single record illustrates two important limits at once. First, it
is a genuine \emph{confident systematic error}: the model is wrong in the
same way every time, and no consistency-based detector can flag it,
because there is no inconsistency to measure. Second, it is partly a
\emph{labeling artifact}: Stansfield Turner did head the CIA for most of
1977--1981, so the ``wrong'' label here reflects an ambiguous gold answer
as much as a model error. Both effects, the blind spot for confident
errors and the noise in automatic correctness labels, recur in the
aggregate numbers and are returned to in the discussion
(Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}).

\subsection{Example 3: why the surface baseline fails on long-form
biographies}
\label{sec:audit-bio}

\textbf{Question (Biography, Mistral-7B):} ``Tell me a bio of Edmond
Pourchot.'' The model produced ten multi-sentence paragraphs. One began:
\emph{``Edmond Pourchot, born on March 2, 1896, in Gap, France, was a
prominent figure in the field of French aviation\ldots''} (Edmond
Pourchot was in fact a seventeenth-century French philosopher; the
paragraph is fabricated.) All ten paragraphs were different strings, so
the surface baseline reports the \emph{maximum possible} value every
time: $\ln 10 = 2.30$ nats. Across the whole biography set the average
surface entropy is $2.303$, pinned at the ceiling, which is exactly
why it carries no information and lands at the chance line in
Figure~\ref{fig:regime-split}: a score that is always maximal cannot rank
anything.

Meaning-based clustering avoids this failure. It groups the paragraphs by
what they claim rather than by their exact wording, so its score varies
from record to record (average semantic entropy $1.73$, not pinned at the
ceiling). For this fabricated biography the ten paragraphs genuinely
disagree, so the semantic entropy is high and the answer is correctly
flagged as risky. This is the long-form mechanism behind the headline
figure.

Three things stand out on short answers. First, all of the
sampling-based detectors work: AUROC sits between roughly $0.67$ and
$0.90$ across the three datasets and both smaller and larger models, and
the three count-based scores that carry the comparison stay above $0.69$,
consistent with the original semantic entropy paper under the matched
protocol. Second, the normalized \emph{surface} baseline is the
strongest of the three count-based scores in eight of the nine
cells---every NQ-Open and SVAMP cell and both larger TriviaQA
models---and discrete semantic entropy matches it closely but never
exceeds it. On NQ-Open, for instance, surface reaches $0.799$, $0.827$
and $0.755$ against the meaning-aware $0.794$, $0.799$ and $0.725$; only
with Mistral-7B on TriviaQA does either of the other two count-based
scores edge past surface, and there it is the raw-string naive estimator
($0.784$ versus $0.781$), not the meaning-aware one. The
probability-weighted naive estimator is higher still in that cell
($0.821$), which is the long-form pattern of
Section~\ref{sec:bio-results} showing up early. Third, discrete semantic entropy tracks the surface
baseline throughout: normalizing the answer strings already performs
almost all of the useful grouping, so adding an NLI-based
meaning-clustering step changes the ranking little. The reason, made
concrete in Example~\ref{sec:audit-merge}, is that on short answers most
equivalent answers are already identical once normalized; meaning-based
entailment then has little left to add.

\section{Significance: does meaning-based clustering beat the surface
baseline?}
\label{sec:significance}

Because the surface and semantic scores are so close on short answers, the
question is whether the difference between them is real or just noise.
Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface} reports paired bootstrap
comparisons: for each task and model it asks whether a given detector
beats the surface baseline by more than sampling noise would explain.

On the short-answer tasks the result is clear: in none of the nine
cells does question-conditioned discrete semantic entropy beat the
surface baseline. Every mean difference is negative or within noise of
zero. The only Holm-significant cell runs \emph{against} the meaning-aware
score: on NQ-Open with Llama-3.1-8B the surface baseline leads by $0.028$
AUROC (mean over seeds, $95\%$ CI $[-0.047, -0.009]$, Holm $p = 0.026$). The
70B model on the same dataset is a near-miss in the same direction
($-0.030$, $p = 0.058$). The largest positive seed-level
movement is $+0.002$ for SVAMP with Mistral-7B (seed 44); its three-seed mean
is $-0.003$ and is nowhere near significant. Once the
answer strings are normalized, adding an NLI clustering step therefore
does not improve short-answer hallucination detection, and on the dataset
where the two differ most it makes detection slightly worse. This is a
negative result: on short answers the meaning-clustering step gains nothing
over a normalized-string baseline. Whether it is worth its cost anywhere is a
question for the long-form setting, which the following sections examine.

\paragraph{Why the null holds.} A negative result is easier to trust when the
reason for it can be shown, and here the reason is simple. AUROC depends only on the
\emph{order} a score imposes on prompts, so if two scores order prompts
alike they must score alike. Table~\ref{tab:rank-agreement} measures that
agreement directly on the locked family, where both scores are recorded for
the same prompts. The mean Spearman correlation across the nine cells is
$0.898$, ranging from $0.745$ (TriviaQA with the 70B model) to $0.989$
(SVAMP with the 70B model). More informative than the correlation is what
drives it: on $57.6$ to $95.3\,\%$ of prompts the two scores are
\emph{numerically identical}. Once the answer strings are normalized, the
entailment step usually finds nothing left to merge, so the meaning-aware
estimator returns exactly what the surface estimator returned. On SVAMP,
where answers are integers, this happens on $85$ to $95\,\%$ of prompts.
The nine cells of
Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface} are therefore not a case of two
different detectors performing similarly; they are largely the same detector
evaluated twice, and the null result is what that predicts.

\paragraph{How small a difference could have been detected?} A null result
carries weight only in proportion to the precision behind it, so it is worth
saying what this design could and could not have seen.
Table~\ref{tab:equivalence-bound} reports two quantities per cell, both from
the same bootstrap that produced Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface}. The
first is the central $90\,\%$ interval, whose upper limit is the largest
advantage for the semantic score that the data remain consistent with. The
second is the minimum detectable effect: the smallest true difference the
cell would detect at $80\,\%$ power.

Read one-sided, the bound is reasonably tight. An advantage for
meaning-based clustering larger than $+0.026$ AUROC is excluded in every
cell, and larger than $+0.011$ in eight of the nine; in three cells the
interval excludes zero altogether or reaches it only at its upper limit. So the claim supported here is not merely
that no advantage was observed, but that an advantage large enough to justify
the cost of pairwise entailment would very likely have been seen if it were
there.

The precision is not uniform, and one cell is genuinely weak. Minimum
detectable effects run from $0.012$ (SVAMP with the 70B model) to $0.062$
(TriviaQA with the 70B model); seven of the nine are at or below $0.03$. The
TriviaQA/70B cell is the exception and should not be read as evidence of
absence --- its interval runs from $-0.047$ to $+0.026$, wide enough to
accommodate a difference that would matter. Note also that this is a post-hoc precision
analysis: no equivalence margin was preregistered, so these are reported as
bounds rather than as a test declaring equivalence against a threshold chosen
after the fact.

The picture changes on long-form text. There the same normalized-string
baseline collapses toward chance, so meaning-aware scoring is what
remains useful rather than redundant. The claim-level long-form results
are reported in Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}, and
Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity} shows how sharply that long-form
outcome depends on the model used to judge equivalence---the opposite of
the short-answer setting, where the backend barely matters.

\section{Clustering backend sensitivity}
\label{sec:clustering-sensitivity}

On short answers the backend effect is modest. Across the nine short-answer
cells the four backends span at most $0.057$ AUROC, and the plain exact-match
grouping is the best of the four in seven of the nine cells: once the answer
strings are normalized there is very little for a stronger equivalence judge
to add. This is the same finding as
Section~\ref{sec:significance}, seen from the other side.

On biographies the choice largely determines the result
(Figure~\ref{fig:bio-clustering-sensitivity}). Exact-match clustering carries
no information there, sitting at chance in all three cells ($0.500 / 0.498 /
0.480$), because repeated paragraphs are extremely rare and nearly every
sample therefore forms its own group. The DeBERTa-v3-base backend reaches only
$0.605$ for Mistral-7B and falls \emph{below} chance for both Llama
generators ($0.467$ and $0.471$); the larger DeBERTa-v3-large recovers a
usable signal in every cell ($0.734 / 0.590 / 0.648$); and the Qwen-72B judge
is stronger again ($0.755 / 0.678 / 0.695$). The same samples therefore yield
anything from anti-predictive to
moderately useful behavior depending only on how equivalence is judged. On
long-form inputs it is the capacity of the entailment model, not merely its
presence, that determines whether a signal appears at all --- and a backend
that mis-merges paragraph-length pairs does not simply add noise, it can
invert the ranking. This is the long-form counterpart to the short-answer
negative result: when clustering matters at all, \emph{which} clusterer is
used matters a great deal, which is also why the following section treats the
whole-paragraph numbers as a diagnostic rather than as a detector benchmark.

\section{Long-form biography results}
\label{sec:bio-results}

The biography task is the controlled long-form case: the same
sample-and-cluster pipeline, but the model now writes a paragraph rather
than a phrase. Table~\ref{tab:auroc-bio} lists every detector for all
three generators.

These numbers are best read as a result about \emph{where the method
applies} rather than as a ranking of detectors. Semantic entropy asks whether
repeated samples agree with one another. Two independently generated
biographies of the same person rarely agree in the sense the method can
measure --- they differ in wording, in which facts they include, and in what
order --- so the score tends towards its maximum and has little room left to
discriminate. Two measurements below show how far that goes here. The
constructive counterpart is Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}: asking one
question per claim and re-sampling short answers restores a setting in which
agreement is measurable, and the long-form conclusion of this thesis rests on
that claim-level result rather than on the numbers in this section.

\thisfloatsetup{capposition=above}
\centering

  (mean $\pm$ std over seeds 42/43/44), canonical DeBERTa-v3-large
  clustering. The two count-based surface
  scores (naive \emph{sample} entropy and surface entropy) sit near chance
  ($\approx 0.50$) because long paragraphs almost never repeat as identical
  strings. Best value per model column in bold (higher is better).}
\label{tab:auroc-bio}
\input{includes/generated_bio_wholepara.tex}
\end{table}

The contrast with short answers is large. The two count-based surface
scores, naive \emph{sample} entropy and surface entropy, sit at
$\approx 0.50$, approximately chance, as Example~\ref{sec:audit-bio}
predicted: long paragraphs almost never repeat as identical strings, so these
scores are nearly pinned at their maximum and have little ranking information. Measured
across the biography set the mean surface entropy is $2.303$ nats for
Mistral-7B, which to three decimals \emph{is} the ceiling $\ln 10 =
2.3026$. A score with no variance cannot rank anything, and this is the
clearest long-form result of the section.

Escaping string identity is necessary but not sufficient. KLE, which
compares meanings softly through sentence embeddings rather than hard
clusters, is at best marginally above chance ($0.540$, Mistral-7B with the
RoBERTa-NLI encoder) and falls \emph{below} chance for both Llama generators
($0.392$--$0.449$): its kernels do not cope with paragraph-length inputs.
Hard NLI clustering does better --- $0.734 / 0.590 / 0.648$ for
DeBERTa-v3-large, $0.755 / 0.678 / 0.695$ with the Qwen judge --- but the
next paragraph shows why those values should not be read as a working
long-form detector.

\paragraph{Whole-paragraph entropy is partly saturated.}
Table~\ref{tab:longform-saturation} measures how often the score is pinned at
its ceiling. Under the canonical backend, discrete semantic entropy takes its
maximum value on $31.8\,\%$ of Mistral-7B records, $15.6\,\%$ for
Llama-3.1-8B and $9.0\,\%$ for Llama-3.1-70B, with $5.3$ to $7.3$ of the ten
samples landing in distinct clusters.
\input{includes/generated_longform_saturation_note.tex}
Under the
weaker DeBERTa-v3-base backend the biography figures rise to
$35.2$--$72.3\,\%$. So on long text a large minority of records carry an
identical, maximal score and contribute nothing to the ranking; the detector
is working only on the remainder. The relationship is not a simple one ---
the Qwen judge saturates more than DeBERTa-v3-large on two of the three
generators yet ranks better, so saturation limits headroom without by itself
fixing the ordering --- but the weakest backend is both the most saturated
and the worst-ranking, and the short-versus-long gap is an order of
magnitude.

\paragraph{Mistral's paragraphs are frequently unfinished.} A second, independent
defect affects the same table. Of the ten sampled paragraphs per prompt,
$27.5\,\%$ of Mistral-7B samples end on a word character rather than on
sentence-terminal punctuation, i.e.\ the generation ran into the decoding cap
mid-sentence; the corresponding rates for Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.1-70B are
$0.3\,\%$ and $0.7\,\%$. Truncated paragraphs are textually distinctive for
reasons that have nothing to do with factual content, so they add
cluster boundaries the meaning of the text does not warrant, and they do so
for one generator far more than the others.

\paragraph{What the table still supports.} Two detectors that need no
clustering at all are the strongest here. Probability-weighted naive entropy,
which keeps each distinct paragraph but weights it by the model's own
sequence probability, is the best score on the two smaller models ($0.769$
for both Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B), and the self-evaluation baseline
\mbox{P(True)} is the best on the largest ($0.835$). Neither is affected by
the saturation above, because neither counts clusters. The lesson is that on
long text the model's \emph{own probability signal} carries much of the
confabulation information, while the hard-clustering step recovers it only
partially and only when the equivalence judge is strong enough.

\paragraph{What this suggests in general.} Taken together, the two
measurements point to the granularity of the scored unit, rather than to
meaning-based detection as such, as the limiting factor on long-form text.
The suggestion they support is that a consistency-based detector works best
at a granularity at which independent samples can plausibly agree, and that
this is worth checking from the samples themselves before interpreting an
AUROC. Two quantities suffice: how often the score sits at its ceiling, and
how many distinct clusters the samples form. Compared like for like under
the canonical backend, on short answers those are $0.0$--$4.8\,\%$ and
$1.3$--$4.4$ clusters, and on whole paragraphs $9.0$--$31.8\,\%$ and
$5.3$--$7.3$ clusters; across all three backends the whole-paragraph
figures span $3.2$--$72.3\,\%$ and $2.7$--$9.5$ clusters
(Table~\ref{tab:longform-saturation}). Read only through AUROC, the second case
looks like a weak detector and invites a stronger entailment model or more
samples; read through these two quantities, it looks instead like a unit that
is too large for the test being applied. The claim-level evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}, where each regenerated answer is short
and the ceiling is far away, is the version of the experiment that avoids the
problem. This is one long-form task and three generators, so the point is
offered as an interpretation of these results rather than as an established
general law.

\section{Sampling-temperature sensitivity}
\label{sec:temperature-sensitivity}

Every result so far used the model's default sampling temperature. Since
sampling-based detectors depend on getting \emph{diverse} samples, the
decoding temperature ought to matter, and it does. This is one of the more
practically useful findings in the thesis, because it says which detector to
reach for under which decoding settings. Each temperature is a separate sealed
instance of the same protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol-provenance}),
differing from the others in the sampling temperature and in nothing else, so
the comparison across a row is internally consistent: what changes from
column to column is the decoding temperature and nothing besides. The
$T{=}1.0$ column is computed on the same evaluation records as the tables
above and reproduces them exactly in seven of the nine cells; the two that
differ do so by $0.005$ and $0.001$, because the sweep scores the full
evaluation set while the primary tables use the feature-present subset
(Section~\ref{sec:budgets-and-exclusions}).
Table~\ref{tab:temp-sweep} and
Figure~\ref{fig:temp-sweep} sweep the temperature from $0.1$ (nearly
deterministic) to $1.0$; the table reports the sweep for the largest
generator, and Figure~\ref{fig:temp-sweep} shows the same pattern for
all three.

At low temperature the model repeats nearly the same answer every time,
so there is little diversity for a sampling-based method to work with and
semantic entropy is weak (TriviaQA/70B: AUROC $0.52$ at $T{=}0.1$). As
temperature rises the samples spread out and the same method becomes
strong ($0.75$ at $T{=}1.0$). \mbox{P(True)}, which reads one greedy
self-evaluation and never looks at the samples, barely moves: across the nine
cells it changes by $+0.031$ on average and declines in only one of them.

The consequence is a closing gap rather than a symmetric exchange. At
$T{=}0.1$ the single-pass baseline is ahead in eight of the nine cells, by as
much as $0.329$ AUROC; by $T{=}1.0$ that lead has shrunk in every cell, and
in five of them it has reversed. Where the ranking ends up follows model
scale rather than task, and it does so without exception: at $T{=}1.0$
semantic entropy is ahead in all six cells of the two smaller generators and
behind in all three cells of Llama-3.1-70B. Five of those six are crossings;
the sixth, SVAMP with Mistral-7B, is the one cell where semantic entropy
already leads at $T{=}0.1$. The crossing also arrives earlier for weaker
generators --- by $T{=}0.3$ for Mistral-7B on NQ-Open, and not until between
$0.5$ and $0.7$ for Llama-3.1-8B on TriviaQA.

The practical lesson is that the right detector depends on both the decoding
temperature and the generator. Under near-greedy decoding the cheap
single-pass score is the better choice in eight of the nine cells. How warm
the decoder must run before sampling-based entropy overtakes it grows with
model strength, and for the largest model tested it never does within the
measured range. Biography was generated at the canonical temperature only, so
this trade-off is established on the short-answer tasks alone.

\paragraph{Is this a property of the decoding temperature or of the seeds?}
The distinction matters, because a trend that only appeared under one random
draw would say nothing about how these detectors behave in general. Three
things separate the two explanations here.

First, the effect is expected on mechanistic grounds, independently of any
seed. Sampling-based entropy consumes disagreement among the samples, and
temperature is precisely the control on how much disagreement there is to
consume. P(True) reads a separate greedy self-evaluation and does not consume
the sampled diversity, so a strong movement on the entropy side alongside a
comparatively flat P(True) curve is consistent with how the two constructions
work.

Second, the effect is far larger than the seed variation on the entropy side.
Every point in Table~\ref{tab:temp-sweep} and Figure~\ref{fig:temp-sweep} is
a mean over the same three seeds, and the seed standard deviation across the
short-answer sweep cells averages $0.007$. Semantic entropy rises
\emph{monotonically in all nine} model--dataset cells, by $0.178$ to $0.279$
AUROC, which is between $22$ and $51$ times the seed spread of the cell it
occurs in (median $32$). A change that large, and that consistent in shape,
cannot be an accident of which seeds were drawn.

Third, and it qualifies the picture: the movement is
almost entirely on the entropy side. \mbox{P(True)} changes by $-0.023$ to
$+0.076$ across the same sweep, a median of about three times its seed
spread, and where it moves it more often rises than falls: it ends higher at
$T{=}1.0$ than at $T{=}0.1$ in eight of the
nine cells. The closing gap is therefore carried by the entropy family
climbing, not by the single-pass baseline falling away beneath it. Any
account of this as two detectors moving in opposite directions would
overstate what the sweep shows.

None of this makes the behavior universal. Three seeds, three datasets and
three generators cannot establish that, and the sweep is one run set. What
the evidence supports is narrower and still useful: within this grid the
temperature dependence is systematic rather than seed-specific, it holds in
every cell measured, and it follows from what each detector family consumes
rather than from the particular random draws.

\section{Claim-level long-form detection: FactualBio}
\label{sec:factualbio-results}

The whole-paragraph biography score asks ``is this paragraph reliable?''
A finer question is ``which individual claims in the paragraph are
reliable?'' The FactualBio extension answers the finer question by
applying the same meaning-based idea one claim at a time, following the
procedure shown in panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:method-schematic}. It starts
from the $150$ published biography claims and their \emph{human} factuality
labels, so no automatic judge stands between the method and the ground
truth. For each claim it (i)~turns the claim into questions
(``When was this person born?''), using one fixed question set shared across
all three generators, (ii)~re-samples fresh answers to each question from the
generator under test, and (iii)~groups those answers by meaning and computes
the semantic entropy; a high value flags the claim as a likely
confabulation. Because
each regenerated answer is a short, question-conditioned string, this is the
short-answer regime applied to long-form content, and the ceiling effects of
Section~\ref{sec:bio-results} do not arise. Table~\ref{tab:factualbio}
reports the result.

Claim-level detection works for every generator, and it degrades with model
size: AUROC falls monotonically from $0.748$ (Mistral-7B) through $0.724$
(Llama-3.1-8B) to $0.701$ (Llama-3.1-70B), with seed standard deviations of
$0.017$ or less. The decline is real and monotonic but modest --- $0.047$
AUROC across a tenfold difference in parameter count --- and the largest
model still detects well above chance. AURAC behaves the same way and is
almost flat ($0.847 / 0.843 / 0.838$), which is what one expects when the
underlying claim accuracy is similar across generators.

\paragraph{Does meaning-clustering help here?} The same records
answer this directly, because the surface baseline can be recomputed on them
by replacing entailment with normalized exact match and changing nothing
else. It does not collapse the way it does on whole paragraphs: the
regenerated answers are short strings, so exact-match grouping still ranks
usefully, at AUROC $0.642 / 0.650 / 0.619$. Meaning-based clustering is
nevertheless ahead in every cell, by $0.07$ to $0.11$ AUROC and $0.03$ to
$0.06$ AURAC. On AUROC that gap is at least five times the seed standard
deviation of either arm in every cell; on AURAC it is at least three times,
the narrowest case being Llama-3.1-70B. This is the mirror image of the short-answer result of
Section~\ref{sec:significance}, and it is the cleaner of the two long-form
comparisons: the gain is measured against a baseline that works rather than
against one pinned at its ceiling, and it is measured against human labels.
The comparison is exploratory rather than preregistered
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}), and three generators on one claim set
cannot establish a general law, but within this grid the meaning-clustering
step is worth its cost at claim granularity, which it never is on short
answers.

The whole-paragraph observation of Section~\ref{sec:bio-results} does not
reproduce this monotonic ordering: its values are $0.734 / 0.590 / 0.648$.
The claim-level decline is nevertheless consistent with the mechanism
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:scale-factualbio}: semantic entropy measures
inconsistency, so repeatedly regenerating the same wrong detail would leave
less disagreement to detect. This mechanism is an interpretation, not a
directly measured cause. The human labels provide the measurement the method
should be judged against, and
against them the erosion with scale is a gentle slope rather than a collapse:
at the largest size the signal is weaker, not absent.
