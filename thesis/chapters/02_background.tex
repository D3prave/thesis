\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} used ``hallucination'' as a cover term. This
chapter makes it precise, then builds the two ideas the method rests on: why
uncertainty is hard to measure over free text, and how entailment can stand in
for sameness of meaning. It ends with the gap the three questions address.

\section{Hallucinations and confabulations}
\label{sec:hallucination-confabulation}

``Hallucination'' covers several failures. A model can be unfaithful to a
source, make unsupported claims, run out of knowledge, reason badly, or guess
because the benchmark rewards guessing over admitting
ignorance~\cite{ji_hallucination_survey_2023,%
huang_llm_hallucination_survey_2023,%
maynez_faithfulness_factuality_2020,%
kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025,%
augenstein_factuality_challenges_2024}. These have different causes and need
different fixes, so no single detector handles all of them.

The semantic entropy paper~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} isolates one:
\emph{confabulations}, fluent answers that are both wrong and arbitrary.
``Arbitrary'' means the answer changes with things that should not matter, such
as the random seed used while decoding. That is the target throughout this
thesis, and the restriction is strict rather than incidental. Some models are
wrong the same way every time. That happens when the training data contains a
misconception, when the problem needs reasoning the model cannot do, or when
the benchmark discourages abstention. Such a model gives stable answers, and no
detector built on sampling inconsistency can see
it~\cite{simhi_choke_2025,ma_semantic_energy_2025}.

The reason is short, and it bounds everything that follows. The method reads
one quantity: how much the answers to a question disagree with each other when
the question is asked repeatedly. An error that survives resampling produces no
disagreement, so it produces no signal.

The same framing gives the expectation the thesis tests. If the model knows the
answer, repeated samples should vary in wording but keep the meaning. If it is
confabulating, they should split across incompatible meanings. Measuring
uncertainty over meanings rather than over strings should therefore be the
better signal~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\section{Uncertainty in free-form generation}
\label{sec:uncertainty-freeform}

When a model picks from a short fixed list, say ``cat'', ``dog'' or ``horse'',
measuring its uncertainty is easy. It assigns a probability to each option, and
those probabilities already say how sure it is. A language model has no such
list. It produces whole sentences, and the same fact can be written many ways
(``Paris'', ``Paris, France'', ``the capital is Paris''), while two sentences
that look almost alike can mean different things. Counting how often the exact
words differ is therefore a poor guide to whether the model is unsure. Spread
the model's probability across ten paraphrases of one fact and the token-level
numbers look uncertain, even though the model said the same thing ten times.
Sequence-level entropy can mislead for the same reason, which is the starting
point of work on uncertainty in natural language
generation~\cite{baan_uncertainty_nlg_2023} and semantic
uncertainty~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023}.

Three levels of variation are worth keeping apart:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{lexical variation}: different words for the same thing
    (``Paris'' vs.\ ``Paris, France'');
  \item \emph{semantic variation}: different claims
    (``Paris'' vs.\ ``Lyon'');
  \item \emph{factual correctness}: whether the chosen claim is right.
\end{enumerate}

Semantic entropy works at the second level. It estimates uncertainty over
meanings, not over truth. Deciding whether an answer is correct is a separate
step that compares it against a reference or a
judge~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. That separation matters for reading
this thesis, because the two steps can be varied independently, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows that changing the second changes the apparent
ranking of methods that differ only in the first.

Recent surveys~\cite{shorinwa_uq_llm_survey_2024,%
liu_uq_confidence_calibration_survey_2025} place this family within uncertainty
quantification for language models, alongside calibration, verbalized
confidence and internal-state methods, and benchmarks now compare many such
estimators side by side~\cite{lm_polygraph_vashurin_2025}.

\section{Confidence, calibration, and abstention}
\label{sec:calibration-abstention}

The point of estimating uncertainty is not only to attach a number to an
answer. It is to decide what happens next: answer, abstain, look something up,
ask a person, or run a more expensive check.

A related idea is \emph{calibration}. A model is well calibrated when its
stated confidence matches how often it is right: among answers given with
90\,\% confidence, about 90\,\% should be correct. Calibration and accuracy are
different properties and a model can have one without the other. The view here
needs neither. Selective prediction only needs risky answers to be \emph{ranked}
above safe ones, which is why rejection-based evaluation is the natural lens.
Accuracy on its own is not enough, because many benchmarks reward a lucky guess
over an honest admission of uncertainty~\cite{kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025}.
Rejection-accuracy curves and their area summary fit the practical question
better. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} still finds that the summary this
literature reports behaves in a way that makes it harder to use, and
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics} returns to it.

Semantic entropy has also been used as a training signal: Tjandra et
al.~\cite{tjandra_abstain_semantic_entropy_2024} fine-tune models on it to
encourage abstention. That is a different use of the same quantity and not what
this thesis does. Here the score is only ever computed on a finished answer
from a model that is left alone.

Several related methods attack the same problem without grouping by meaning.
P(True)~\cite{kadavath_language_models_know_2022} asks the model whether its own
answer is correct, testing what the model can say about itself rather than how
much its samples vary. SelfCheckGPT~\cite{manakul_selfcheckgpt_2023} samples
several responses and checks them against each other, without any database or
access to token probabilities. Semantic entropy is closest to the second kind,
but unlike it, semantic entropy groups the samples into meaning-clusters before
computing an
entropy~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.
These methods form a spectrum. P(True) reads only the model's own verdict, and
SelfCheckGPT compares samples. Semantic entropy sits between them and adds one
step none of the others perform: deciding when two answers mean the same thing.
What that step is worth is what this study measures.

A third family reads the model's \emph{hidden states} instead of its words. The
observation behind it is that internal activations often carry information
about reliability that the output text does not. Burns et
al.~\cite{burns_latent_knowledge_2023} find a truth-like direction in activation
space without any labels; Azaria and
Mitchell~\cite{azaria_internal_state_lying_2023} train a small classifier on
hidden states to tell true statements from false ones; and
INSIDE~\cite{chen_inside_2024} scores hallucinations from the geometry of
sentence embeddings taken from inside the model. The appeal is cost: one forward pass instead of $M$ samples and $M^2$
entailment comparisons. Semantic Entropy
Probes~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024} join the two families by
training such a probe to predict semantic entropy itself. The expensive signal
is then paid for once during training and read cheaply afterwards. Both that
probe and a correctness-trained variant appear in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, and
Sections~\ref{sec:sep-method} and~\ref{sec:other-estimators} define them.

\section{Semantic equivalence and entailment}
\label{sec:semantic-equivalence}

The working step in semantic entropy is deciding when two sampled answers count
as the same answer. It uses semantic equivalence rather than string equality:
in Farquhar et al.'s formulation~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, two
answers belong together when they entail one another in the context of the
question. That is an approximation to sameness of meaning, not a definition of
it.

Natural Language Inference (NLI) is the standard tool. An NLI model reads two
sentences and labels the relation between them as \emph{entailment} (the first
implies the second), \emph{contradiction}, or \emph{neutral}. Checking both
directions then stands in for ``these two answers mean the same thing''. Two
kinds of model do this here. The first is a \emph{cross-encoder} fine-tuned on
standard inference data such as MultiNLI~\cite{williams_multinli_2018}: the
model the released code loads is a DeBERTa model~\cite{he_deberta_2021} of that
kind, and a current DeBERTa-v3
cross-encoder~\cite{he_debertav3_2021,%
sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_large_2022,%
sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_base_2022} is used beside it.
``Cross-encoder'' means both sentences go into the model together, so it can
compare them directly; the alternative, a \emph{bi-encoder}, encodes each
sentence separately and compares afterwards. The cross-encoder is more accurate
for this kind of judgment but slower, because every pair has to be run through
it. The second kind is a general instruction-tuned language model asked the
same question in words. That role is filled by
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct~\cite{yang_qwen25_2024}, standing in for the proprietary
judge the original uses on sentence-length answers.
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy} gives the exact rule and the full list.

Two properties of this approximation matter later. It is only as good as the
model making the judgment, and the models available differ by two orders of
magnitude in size. And the relation is symmetric by construction but not
transitive: nothing forces $a \equiv b$ and $b \equiv c$ to imply $a \equiv c$.
What a grouping procedure does when that fails is a design decision, and
Section~\ref{sec:clustering} shows the released code resolves it in a way that
makes the result depend on the order the samples arrive in.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

Semantic entropy is reported to detect confabulations better than the methods
it is compared against, and that result has been taken up
widely~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024,kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,%
kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024}. Three gaps remain, one for each question
this thesis asks.

\paragraph{The evaluation has not been repeated on current open models.} The
original uses proprietary judges and a generation of models that has since been
superseded, and its released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024} differs from its prose in
several small ways that only show up if you read the code. Whether the reported
advantage survives a faithful reimplementation on open models, with every
difference recorded, is the first question.

\paragraph{No baseline isolates any single step.} This is the substantive gap.
The comparators in this literature are of three kinds: entropy over sequence
log-likelihoods, self-evaluation in the style of
P(True)~\cite{kadavath_language_models_know_2022}, and hidden-state
probes~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024}. Each differs from semantic entropy in more than one way at once. They differ in
what quantity is read, in how many model calls are made, and in whether any
grouping happens at all. The surveys and benchmarks that follow inherit the
same set~\cite{shorinwa_uq_llm_survey_2024,%
liu_uq_confidence_calibration_survey_2025,lm_polygraph_vashurin_2025}. A
comparison of that shape can show the whole procedure beats the alternatives.
It cannot show which part of the procedure earns the margin, and the result is
still usually read as evidence for the grouping step in particular.

The missing comparator is the obvious one: draw the same samples, normalize
them, count the distinct strings, and take the entropy of those counts. It
needs no entailment model and no second network, and it isolates the step at
issue about as closely as the released code allows.

Supplying it also makes a fuller answer possible. Semantic entropy differs from
the crudest string baseline in four steps, and comparing each against the one
below it gives the value of every step rather than only the last. Which step
earns the advantage, tested with a procedure that respects how the evaluation
grid is actually built, is the second question.

\paragraph{The parts treated as implementation details have not been varied.}
Three choices sit inside every reported number and are described in this
literature as configuration: which entailment model decides that two answers
mean the same thing, which rule decides whether an answer was correct, and what
temperature the samples were drawn at. Each is a free parameter a reader is
invited to assume does not matter much, and nothing establishes that. Recent
work reports that semantic entropy weakens as one-sentence outputs get
longer~\cite{nguyen_beyond_semantic_entropy_2025} and includes isolated
ablations of temperature, but no study varies these three on one fixed set of
generations, which is the only way to attribute a change to the factor rather
than to the data. Seed-to-seed variability is not reported either, so a reader
has no scale for judging whether a margin is large. How far these choices move
the answer, next to the difference between the methods themselves, is the third
question.

\paragraph{}
One smaller point shapes Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design}. The original
reports two prompting conditions, one giving sentence-length answers and one
giving short phrases, and they differ at the same time in the instruction given
to the model and in the number of worked examples before the question. Any
difference between them could come from either factor or from their
interaction. Collecting the two missing combinations costs little and separates
them.

These questions fix the scope to three short-answer tasks and one long-form
task. The short-answer tasks are open-domain factual recall with
TriviaQA~\cite{joshi_triviaqa_2017} and NQ-Open~\cite{lee_nq_open_2019}, and
arithmetic word problems with SVAMP~\cite{patel_svamp_2021}. The long-form task
is a FactScore-style biography task~\cite{min_factscore_2023}, which supplies
the regime where counting strings cannot work at all. The dataset rationale, the prompt
construction and the reproduced question selection are in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} and Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

Taken together, the literature places semantic entropy between cheap surface
signals and expensive fact-checking. Testing it fairly means keeping
generation, grouping, scoring and evaluation separate, so each can be varied
while the others are held fixed. Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy} defines the
method in those terms, and Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} builds the
design around them.
