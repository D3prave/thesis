\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{ch:experimental_design}

This chapter specifies the experimental design used to evaluate whether
semantic entropy provides a better hallucination and abstention signal
than surface-form uncertainty. The design is split into two phases.
Phase~1 establishes an exact-match clustering baseline that is small
enough to debug locally before being run at evaluation scale. Phase~2
repeats the same measurement plan with NLI clustering on NHR@FAU
resources. Chapter~5 then covers execution, storage, and reproducibility
on the cluster.

\section{Datasets}

The thesis uses two datasets: TriviaQA and SVAMP. TriviaQA provides
open-domain question answering with short factual answers, e.g.\
\textit{``What is the capital of France?''} \cite{joshi_triviaqa_2017}.
SVAMP provides short math word problems for which answer normalization
and semantic equivalence matter at the level of numbers rather than
phrasings, e.g.\ \textit{``Mia has 3 apples. She buys 4 more. How many
apples does she have now?''}~\cite{patel_svamp_2021}. These two
datasets are small enough to support repeated sampling while still
exercising two distinct failure modes --- factual recall and structured
arithmetic reasoning. Example records are reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:experiment-details}.

The original semantic entropy evaluation~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}
reports results across five datasets: TriviaQA, SQuAD, BioASQ, NQ-Open,
and SVAMP. Of these, three (BioASQ, NQ-Open, TriviaQA) are open-domain
factual question-answering tasks that differ primarily in domain rather
than in task structure, and TriviaQA is the most widely used and the
primary reference dataset in the original experiments. SQuAD is a
reading-comprehension dataset whose original benchmark design includes
source passages; Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} make it context-free by withholding
those passages, which still leaves a different dataset origin and
prompt assumption from open-domain recall tasks. The two-dataset scope
used here therefore covers the two qualitatively distinct task types
--- open-domain factual recall and short-answer arithmetic --- within a
compute budget appropriate for a bachelor-thesis pilot. Expanding to
NQ-Open or BioASQ remains a straightforward extension because both
share the same short free-text answer format and normalization
approach as TriviaQA. SQuAD, BioASQ, and NQ-Open are not used here
\cite{rajpurkar_squad_2016,tsatsaronis_bioasq_2015,lee_nq_open_2019};
TruthfulQA is likewise omitted because the design isolates semantic
clustering on short-answer tasks rather than broader
truthfulness~\cite{lin_truthfulqa_2022}.

\section{Models and the two phases}

The two language models used as generators in both phases are
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\cite{jiang_mistral_2023} and
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct~\cite{grattafiori_llama3_2024}. They span the
same small/large open-weights pairing that Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} use within the LLaMA-2 family
(LLaMA-2-Chat-7B and LLaMA-2-Chat-70B), but both substitutions are
deliberately upward: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 reports a substantial
improvement over LLaMA-2-Chat-7B on standard knowledge-recall and
reasoning benchmarks~\cite{jiang_mistral_2023}, sitting in
approximately the open-weights analogue of the GPT-3.5 capability
band that Farquhar et al.\ assume for their judge model, and
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct sits above LLaMA-2-Chat-70B across MMLU,
TriviaQA, and GSM8K~\cite{grattafiori_llama3_2024}. Choosing newer
open instruction-tuned models rather than reproducing the LLaMA-2
generation thus moves the experiments to a regime that is closer to
how current chat models are actually deployed, while keeping the
seven-billion-vs-seventy-billion contrast that motivates Phase~1 and
Phase~2 in the first place. The NLI judge in Phase~2 is a local
DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder rather than the proprietary GPT-3.5 used by
Farquhar et al., so the whole pipeline runs without dependence on a
closed API; a posthoc rerun in which this cross-encoder is replaced
with a large open instruction-tuned judge is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}. The methodological implications of these
substitutions are discussed in Section~8.5.

\subsection{Phase 1: exact-match (surface-form) baseline}

Phase~1 records the surface-form baseline. For each prompt, $M=10$
answers are sampled, lightly normalized, and clustered by exact-match
on the normalized string. This is \emph{not} the bidirectional-NLI
clustering rule used by Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; it just collapses whitespace,
prefix and capitalization variation. Phase~1 therefore acts both as a
sanity check on the full pipeline --- generation, normalization,
clustering, entropy scoring, correctness labeling, JSONL output ---
and as the surface-form reference against which the Phase~2 numbers are
compared. Throughout the thesis, the Phase~1 entropy score is
reported as a surface-form baseline and \emph{not} described as
semantic entropy. The Slurm job identifiers are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:runs}.

\subsection{Phase 2: NLI clustering}

Phase~2 keeps the same model--dataset grid, prompt subset, decoding
settings, and seed, but writes a separate output record in which the
exact-match clustering step is replaced with the bidirectional-NLI rule
from Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Correctness labels are
recomputed for the Phase~2 records. The NLI backend used is a
Sentence-Transformers DeBERTa-v3-base cross-encoder fine-tuned on SNLI
and MultiNLI~\cite{he_debertav3_2021,williams_multinli_2018,%
sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_base_2022}. This is the
configuration that corresponds most directly to the original
semantic-entropy method.

Phase~2 introduces no new research question; it tests whether the
ranking behavior seen in Phase~1 survives when exact-match clustering is
replaced by NLI-defined semantic clustering under the same evaluation
grid. Two posthoc extensions are run on top of the Phase~2 outputs (a
larger NLI backend and Kernel Language Entropy); they are described
next.

\section{Sampling settings}

All Phase~1 and Phase~2 runs use $M=10$ samples per prompt, temperature
$T=0.7$, top-$p=0.95$, and a short maximum answer length appropriate
for sentence-length question answering. The sample count $M=10$ matches
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; the temperature and
top-$p$ values do not. Farquhar et al.\ use $T=1.0$, top-$p=0.9$, and
$\textit{top-k}=50$. The thesis uses $T=0.7$ and top-$p=0.95$ because
those are the default decoding settings for current
instruction-tuned chat models including Mistral-7B-Instruct and the
Llama-3.1 instruct family, so they correspond to the regime in which
these models are normally deployed. We fix one configuration across
both phases and both models rather than running a temperature sweep:
the goal is to compare clustering rules and judges under a single
sampling regime, not to characterize the temperature sensitivity of
semantic entropy. The implications of this choice are revisited in
Section~8.5. The meaning of $M$, $T$, and top-$p$ is explained in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Each run logs its decoding
configuration into the JSONL record.

\section{Baselines and methods}

The minimum baseline is surface-form uncertainty: the naive entropy
over distinct normalized strings. It addresses a single question --- does
semantic clustering add value beyond observing that sampled outputs
differ lexically?

The core method is discrete semantic entropy with bidirectional NLI
clustering. SelfCheckGPT-style sampling consistency and P(True) are
related sampling-based baselines~\cite{manakul_selfcheckgpt_2023,%
kadavath_language_models_know_2022} but were not included in the
recorded runs.

\section{Posthoc extensions}

Following the Phase~2 main runs, two posthoc extensions were completed
and are reported in Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}.

\textbf{Kernel Language Entropy (KLE).} KLE is a continuous,
kernel-based alternative to discrete semantic entropy. Instead of
grouping answers into hard clusters and counting them, it embeds each
answer as a vector using a sentence-embedding model and measures how
spread out the resulting embedding cloud is: a tight cloud means low
semantic uncertainty, a diffuse cloud means high uncertainty. The
mathematical definition is given in Chapter~\ref{ch:extensions}. KLE
was applied to all four Phase~2 JSONL output sets using the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence
encoder~\cite{nikitin_kernel_language_entropy_2024,%
sentence_transformers_all_minilm_l6_v2_2022}.

\textbf{DeBERTa-v3-large reclustering.} The Phase~2 NLI clustering step
was re-run with the larger DeBERTa-v3-large NLI cross-encoder to test
whether the
70B/TriviaQA regression observed under the base model disappeared with
a stronger NLI backend~\cite{sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_large_2022}.

\section{Output schema}

Each recorded run writes JSONL --- one JSON object per line --- with
exactly one record per prompt. The schema below makes generation,
clustering, scoring, and evaluation auditable while leaving extensions
free to add method-specific fields:

\begin{verbatim}
{
  "run_id": "string",
  "phase": "phase1|phase2",
  "cluster": "local|tinygpu|alex|fritz",
  "prompt_id": "string",
  "dataset": "triviaqa|svamp|...",
  "split": "validation",
  "prompt": "string",
  "reference_answers": ["string"],
  "model": "string",
  "model_tier": "7b_8b|70b_plus",
  "decoding": {
    "num_samples": 10,
    "temperature": 0.7,
    "top_p": 0.95,
    "max_new_tokens": 64,
    "seed": 42
  },
  "sampled_answers": ["string"],
  "normalized_answers": ["string"],
  "semantic_clusters": ["integer_per_sample"],
  "cluster_representatives": ["string"],
  "scores": {
    "naive_sample_entropy": 0.0,
    "discrete_semantic_entropy": 0.0
  },
  "entailment_backend": "string",
  "correctness_label": "boolean"
}
\end{verbatim}

In Phase~1 \texttt{naive\_sample\_entropy} and
\texttt{discrete\_semantic\_entropy} coincide by construction --- both
count distinct normalized strings --- so the schema contains a single
\texttt{naive\_sample\_entropy} score and uses it as the surface-form
baseline. Larger raw generations, model caches, and full intermediate
outputs are described by the storage and reproducibility policy in
Chapter~5.

\section{Validation checks}

Every recorded run is checked against the following structural
requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item every prompt has exactly $M$ sampled answers;
  \item every sampled answer has a normalized form;
  \item every answer receives a semantic cluster identifier, and the
    cluster list contains one identifier per sampled answer;
  \item entropy scores are finite and non-negative;
  \item correctness labels are present for all evaluated prompts;
  \item decoding settings, model names, clustering backend, dataset
    split, and random seeds are recorded.
\end{enumerate}

A manual audit on a handful of records additionally checks that the
clusters look reasonable; the audit is reported in Section~6.4.

\section{Evaluation metrics}

The main evaluation framework is \emph{selective prediction}. The idea
is to use the uncertainty score as a ranking signal: examples with the
highest uncertainty scores are flagged as ``probably wrong'' and would
in deployment be rejected, deferred to a human, or routed to a more
expensive verification step. The thesis reports three quantities.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{AUROC} (area under the receiver operating characteristic
        curve). Treating ``incorrect'' as the positive class, AUROC is
        the probability that a randomly chosen incorrect record
        receives a higher uncertainty score than a randomly chosen
        correct record. An AUROC of 0.5 corresponds to random ranking;
        1.0 is perfect ranking.
  \item \emph{Rejection-accuracy curve}. For each rejection fraction
        $f\in[0,1]$, the curve shows the accuracy obtained on the
        remaining $1-f$ fraction of records after rejecting the $f$
        fraction with the highest uncertainty. A useful uncertainty
        signal makes the curve rise above the flat ``raw accuracy''
        baseline.
  \item \emph{AURAC} (area under the rejection-accuracy curve). A
        single-number summary of the rejection-accuracy curve.
\end{itemize}

Raw answer accuracy is reported only as context; it is not sufficient
on its own, because the practical goal is to decide when a model
should answer, abstain, or route the prompt to a more expensive
verification procedure~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.
