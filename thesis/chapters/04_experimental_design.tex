\chapter{Experimental Design and Implementation}
\label{ch:experimental_design}

This chapter specifies the experimental design used to evaluate whether
semantic entropy provides a better hallucination and abstention signal
than surface-form uncertainty. The design separates surface-form
baselines from an NLI-clustered replication condition. The baseline is
small enough to debug locally before being run at evaluation scale. The
replication condition repeats the same measurement plan with NLI
clustering on NHR@FAU resources. The final section
(Section~\ref{sec:hpc-execution}) covers how the runs were implemented
and executed on the cluster, together with the storage and
reproducibility policy.

\section{Datasets}

The final evaluation grid uses four datasets: TriviaQA~\cite{joshi_triviaqa_2017}, NQ-Open~\cite{lee_nq_open_2019}, SVAMP~\cite{patel_svamp_2021}, and a FactScore-style biography generation task~\cite{min_factscore_2023}. TriviaQA provides open-domain question answering with short factual answers, e.g.\ \textit{``What is the capital of France?''}. NQ-Open provides open-domain questions derived from real search queries. It shares TriviaQA's short free-text answer format and normalization approach but is markedly harder for all generators evaluated here. SVAMP provides short math word problems for which answer normalization and semantic equivalence matter at the level of numbers rather than phrasings, e.g.\ \textit{``Mia has 3 apples. She buys 4 more. How many apples does she have now?''}. The biography task prompts the model with \textit{``Tell me a bio of \{entity\}.''} and elicits a paragraph rather than a short answer. Following the FactScore setup~\cite{min_factscore_2023}, the entities come from English Wikipedia category listings, including Nobel laureates, heads of state and government, major scientific-award winners, inventors, and novelists. Each entity's reference is the pinned lead section of its article. The evaluated prompt sets are fixed once and shared by every model, seed, and condition: 500 prompts each for TriviaQA, NQ-Open, and the biography task, and 300 for SVAMP. The short-answer prompts are drawn from the datasets' validation splits. The biography prompts are drawn from the Wikipedia category listings described above. The three short-answer datasets are small enough to support repeated sampling while exercising distinct failure modes: trivia-style factual recall, search-query factual recall, and structured arithmetic reasoning. The biography task adds a qualitatively different regime in which each sample is a free-form paragraph rather than a normalizable token string. This long-form regime is the setting in which surface-form and meaning-level uncertainty are most likely to diverge. After normalization, the ten samples for a biography prompt are almost always ten distinct strings, so exact-match clustering can no longer collapse them. Representative records from each dataset are shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-samples}, and further example records are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:experiment-details}.

The original semantic entropy evaluation~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}
reports results across five short-answer datasets: TriviaQA, SQuAD,
BioASQ, NQ-Open, and SVAMP. The grid used here covers three of these
(TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP), spanning open-domain factual recall and
short-answer arithmetic, together with the long-form biography
setting that the original work also evaluates and that is precisely
where the surface-form baseline is expected to break down.
SQuAD~\cite{rajpurkar_squad_2016} is omitted because its
reading-comprehension origin assumes source passages that Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} have to withhold artificially;
BioASQ~\cite{tsatsaronis_bioasq_2015} is omitted as a third
open-domain recall task that differs from TriviaQA and NQ-Open
primarily in domain; and TruthfulQA~\cite{lin_truthfulqa_2022} is
omitted because the design isolates semantic clustering rather than
broader truthfulness. Beyond the whole-paragraph biography evaluation, a claim-level long-form
extension (FactualBio) scores individual factual claims rather than whole
paragraphs. It starts from the $150$ published FactualBio claims together
with their \emph{human} factuality labels, so no automatic judge stands
between the method and the ground truth, and it generates no new
biographies. Claims and labels come from the code release accompanying
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}
(\texttt{jlko/long\_hallucinations}, release v.1.0.0, commit
\texttt{957b806}), pinned by file hash at import time; the importer requires
21 biographies, 150 claim annotations and the published 105 correct / 45
false split before it writes anything. Each claim carries six frozen questions, and three answers are
regenerated per question from the model under test. The question set is
hash-locked and shared across all three generators, which makes the model
comparison paired at the cost of not tailoring questions per model; that
fixed-question adaptation is disclosed rather than treated as neutral. Two
further deviations from the released FactualBio code are disclosed for the
same reason: the clustering here is question-conditioned rather than
answer-only, and it uses \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-large} in place
of the original \texttt{microsoft/deberta-v2-xlarge-mnli}. This is therefore a
fixed-claim, question-conditioned FactualBio adaptation using the released
claim labels, not a direct replication. Results are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}.

\section{Models and the replication condition}
\label{sec:models-replication}

The study generates answers with three open language models of different
sizes: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\cite{jiang_mistral_2023},
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct~\cite{grattafiori_llama3_2024}. Using three sizes
lets the experiments ask how the detectors behave as the model gets
bigger. Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} evaluate
LLaMA-2 Chat at 7B, 13B and 70B, Falcon Instruct at 7B and 40B, and
Mistral Instruct at 7B, and report that semantic entropy is stable across
those families. The grid here is narrower in families but spans the same
range of scales, and it deliberately retains one point of overlap: Mistral
7B Instruct appears in both, here at the later v0.3 revision. The two Llama
models replace the LLaMA-2 pair with a current within-family contrast, which
is the only pair in this grid for which a scale comparison is
within-architecture. Every model--dataset cell is decoded under three seeds
(42, 43, 44), and all headline results are reported as cross-seed means with
seed standard deviations.

The entailment judges are local DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoders (two sizes, base
and large, introduced below). Farquhar et al.\ operationalize entailment
either with an NLI model, DeBERTa-Large-MNLI, or with a general-purpose LLM
such as GPT-3.5; this study takes the first of those two options throughout,
so the choice is a narrowing of their protocol rather than a departure from
it.
Using a local model rather than a closed API matters for several
reasons: the model version is fixed and publicly available, so results
are fully reproducible without requiring access to any external service;
there are no per-call costs or rate limits; and no prompt or response
data leaves the local system.
The whole pipeline therefore runs without dependence on a closed API;
a posthoc rerun in which this cross-encoder is replaced
with a large open instruction-tuned judge is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}. The methodological
implications of these substitutions are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison-farquhar}.

\paragraph{Surface-form baselines.}
The baseline records two surface-form scores. For each prompt, $M=10$
answers are sampled, lightly normalized, and clustered by exact-match
on the normalized string, as defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Entropy is computed over these
normalized exact-match clusters and, as a deliberately naive companion,
over the raw unnormalized strings. The baseline therefore acts both as a
sanity check on the full pipeline (generation, normalization,
clustering, entropy scoring, correctness labeling, JSONL output)
and as the surface-form reference against which the NLI numbers are
compared. Because exact-match clustering ignores paraphrases, the
exact-match entropy score is reported as a surface-form baseline rather
than as semantic entropy. The corresponding run artifacts are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:runs}.

\paragraph{NLI-clustered replication condition.}
The replication condition keeps the same model--dataset grid, prompt subset, decoding
settings, and seed, but writes a separate output record in which the
exact-match clustering step is replaced with the bidirectional-NLI rule
from Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Correctness labels are
recomputed for the NLI-clustered records. The NLI backend used is a
Sentence-Transformers DeBERTa-v3-base
cross-encoder~\cite{sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_base_2022},
a DeBERTa-v3 model~\cite{he_debertav3_2021} fine-tuned on SNLI and
MultiNLI~\cite{williams_multinli_2018}. This is the lightest
configuration faithful to the original semantic-entropy method, and it
is the backend used for the full-grid generation runs.

Two NLI cross-encoders therefore appear in the results. The replication
condition above is run end-to-end with DeBERTa-v3-base. For the headline
entropy numbers reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, every saved sample
set is additionally re-clustered \emph{post hoc} with the larger
DeBERTa-v3-large cross-encoder, which is treated as the canonical
backend. Because re-clustering reuses the same saved samples, any
difference between the two backends reflects the equivalence judge alone.
Throughout, a table or figure that reports ``canonical'' clustering
refers to DeBERTa-v3-large; the base-backend numbers appear as one column
of the clustering-sensitivity analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}).

The replication condition introduces no new research question; it tests whether
the ranking behavior seen in the baseline survives when exact-match clustering
is replaced by NLI-defined semantic clustering under the same evaluation grid.
Its outputs are then reused rather than regenerated. The saved samples are
re-clustered under a larger NLI backend and an LLM judge, re-scored with Kernel
Language Entropy, and used as the training target for the semantic-entropy
probe. Those checks, the supervised and self-evaluation baselines, and the
sampling-temperature sweep are set out in
Section~\ref{sec:baselines-and-analyses}.

\paragraph{The biography task under the same comparison.}
The biography task is not a separate study but a fourth dataset evaluated
under exactly the same baseline-versus-semantic comparison. The baseline
clusters its samples by exact match and the replication condition by
semantic clustering, with the only
differences being mechanical: the maximum answer length is raised to
accommodate paragraph-length generations, and because free-form text has
no dataset-specific normalization, the exact-match baseline is
applied to the raw sampled strings. The semantic-clustering step is run
both with the whole-response NLI cross-encoder and with the open
instruction-tuned judge, since paragraph-length pairs are exactly the
case where the two semantic-equivalence backends are most likely to
disagree.

\paragraph{Correctness labeling for long-form generations.}
For TriviaQA and NQ-Open, the low-temperature most-likely answer is marked
correct when its maximum SQuAD token-F1 against the reference answers exceeds
$0.5$; SVAMP uses exact match after numeric normalization. This short-answer
protocol is not available for
paragraph-length biographies, which have no single short ground-truth
string. The biography task therefore grades correctness with the open
instruction-tuned judge
(Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct~\cite{yang_qwen25_2024}) against the first
paragraph of the entity's English Wikipedia article. Because AUROC and
AURAC need one correctness bit per prompt, the recorded label grades the
representative response from the modal cluster, not all ten sampled
paragraphs independently; this assumes that the most frequently expressed
paragraph is the one whose reliability the detector is being asked to
predict. The judge is asked whether that representative
biography is factually consistent with the reference, and the binary
verdict becomes the correctness label used for AUROC and AURAC. The
reference revision is pinned per record so the label is reproducible.
This protocol matches the long-form correctness setup of Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. One caveat is specific to the
long-form task. These correctness labels are produced by the
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct judge, and that same model is also one of the
clustering backends (Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}); in that
sensitivity variant the clusterer and the grader coincide, so a
systematic judge error could affect a detector score and its label in a
correlated way. The canonical headline numbers avoid this coincidence,
because their clustering uses the independent DeBERTa-v3-large
cross-encoder rather than the grading model. Varying the clusterer while
holding the label fixed (Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity})
therefore also serves as a partial control; this and the more general
limitation of a judge standing in for ground truth are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-limitations}.

\section{Sampling settings}
\label{sec:sampling-settings}

The final grid uses the paper-faithful sampling configuration of
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}: $M=10$ samples per
prompt at temperature $T=1.0$ with nucleus top-$p=0.9$ and
top-$k=50$, applied uniformly to every generation stage. Per-sequence
log-probabilities are captured for each sample and length-normalized,
which is what enables the probability-weighted estimators (naive
entropy and full semantic entropy) alongside the count-based ones. The maximum answer length is short for the short-answer datasets,
appropriate for sentence-length question answering, and is raised for the
biography task so that paragraph-length generations can complete. The exact
ceilings, and the truncation actually observed under them, are given in
Section~\ref{sec:budgets-and-exclusions}.

Correctness labeling on the short-answer datasets also follows the
original protocol: in addition to the ten high-temperature samples,
each prompt receives one low-temperature ($T=0.1$) most-likely answer,
which is counted correct when its SQuAD token-F1 against any reference
answer exceeds 0.5 (exact match for SVAMP's numeric answers). This
single label propagates to every estimator and posthoc backend on that
record, so all methods share one accuracy definition. The biography
task instead grades correctness with the long-form judge, as described
above.

During pipeline development an earlier configuration was used, the
instruction-tuned models' default deployment settings ($T=0.7$,
top-$p=0.95$) with string-comparison correctness labels, to debug the
pipeline before the evaluation grid was locked. All headline numbers in
this thesis come from the paper-faithful configuration above. The
meaning of $M$, $T$, and top-$p$ is explained in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Each run logs its decoding
configuration into the JSONL record.

\section{Baselines, replication method, and additional analyses}
\label{sec:baselines-and-analyses}

The surface-form baselines are entropy over raw strings and entropy over
distinct normalized exact-match strings. They address a single question:
does semantic clustering add value beyond observing that sampled outputs
differ lexically? The replication methods are discrete semantic entropy
with bidirectional NLI clustering and, using the captured sequence
log-probabilities, the paper's probability-weighted naive entropy and
full semantic entropy (the estimator taxonomy is laid out in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}).

Beyond the entropy family, the final grid records three supervised or
self-evaluation baselines.

\emph{P(True)}~\cite{kadavath_language_models_know_2022} asks the generator
to judge its own most-likely answer. The grading prompt first shows a few
solved true/false examples, then the actual question; the probability the
model puts on the token ``True'' becomes the uncertainty score
$1-P(\mathrm{True})$.

\emph{Embedding regression} is a logistic probe on the final hidden state,
trained to predict incorrectness. It is reported in-distribution, and
out-of-distribution by training on the pooled prompts of the other two
short-answer tasks and scoring on the held-out one.

The third is the \emph{semantic-entropy probe} of
Section~\ref{sec:sep-method}.
SelfCheckGPT~\cite{manakul_selfcheckgpt_2023} is a related
sampling-based baseline but was not included in the recorded runs.

Each saved sample set is additionally re-clustered posthoc under
multiple semantic backends (DeBERTa-v3-base and DeBERTa-v3-large
cross-encoders plus a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
LLM judge) and re-scored with KLE under two sentence encoders
(all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and nli-roberta-large). These checks reuse the saved
answer samples, so any metric change comes from the semantic judge or
entropy estimator rather than from new generations. The KLE method is
defined in Section~\ref{sec:kle}; clustering sensitivity is
reported in Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}.

\section{Output schema}

Each recorded run writes JSONL (one JSON object per line) with
exactly one record per prompt. Every record stores the prompt and reference answers; the model and its
full decoding configuration, including the seed; the ten sampled answers with
their normalized forms and length-normalized sequence log-probabilities; the
low-temperature most-likely answer used for accuracy labeling; the cluster
assignment of every sample with one representative string per cluster; all
entropy scores; the clustering backend; and the correctness label.
This makes generation, clustering, scoring, and evaluation auditable
from a single line of JSONL, while leaving extensions free to add
method-specific fields (KLE, probe, and P(True) scores; a greedy-response
hidden state and its feature metadata in canonical SEP collection runs).
The metric-relevant schema and canonical SEP feature shape are reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:schema}.

In the exact-match baseline artifacts, \texttt{surface\_entropy} and
\texttt{discrete\_semantic\_entropy} coincide by construction, because
both count clusters induced by normalized exact-match strings.
\texttt{naive\_sample\_entropy} is kept as a separate raw-string
baseline, where unnormalized spelling, casing, punctuation, and
whitespace variants remain distinct. The SEP collection runs use the
exact-match clustering backend (so \texttt{discrete\_semantic\_entropy}
again equals \texttt{surface\_entropy} on those records) and additionally
write one greedy-response \texttt{sep\_hidden\_state} with
\texttt{sep\_feature\_metadata} per record; held-out evaluation splits also
carry a \texttt{probe\_uncertainty} score in $[0,1]$. The legacy
\texttt{hidden\_states} matrix is reserved for the separately named
sample-level accuracy-probe path.
The serialized \texttt{phase} field remains as a legacy condition label
so recorded outputs stay traceable; this thesis instead uses the labels
baseline, replication condition, and additional analysis.
Larger raw generations, model caches, and full intermediate outputs
are described by the storage and reproducibility policy in
Section~\ref{sec:hpc-execution}.

\section{Validation checks}
\label{sec:validation-checks}

Every recorded run is checked against the following structural
requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item every prompt has exactly $M$ sampled answers;
  \item every sampled answer has a normalized form;
  \item every answer receives a semantic cluster identifier, and the
    cluster list contains one identifier per sampled answer;
  \item entropy scores are finite and non-negative;
  \item correctness labels are present for all evaluated prompts;
  \item decoding settings, model names, clustering backend, dataset
    split, and random seeds are recorded.
\end{enumerate}

A manual audit on a handful of records additionally checks that the
clusters look reasonable; worked examples of such audits are shown in
Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits}.

\section{Decoding budgets, truncation, and excluded prompts}
\label{sec:budgets-and-exclusions}

Three properties of the generation step affect how the results should be
read. All three are measured rather than assumed, and all three turn out to be
small; they are recorded here so that later chapters can refer to a number
instead of an assurance.

\paragraph{Two decoding budgets.} The ten stochastic samples and the single
greedy response used as the probe feature have separate token ceilings. The
stochastic ceiling is per dataset ($64$ tokens for TriviaQA, $128$ for NQ-Open
and SVAMP, $256$ for biography), while the greedy feature response is fixed at
$256$ tokens everywhere. Keeping the two apart matters: an earlier version of
the pipeline reused the stochastic ceiling for the greedy response and cut it
short.

\paragraph{Truncation.} A sample that hits its ceiling before the model emits
an end-of-sequence token is truncated, which can distort the entropy computed
over it. The protocol rejects any collection shard above $1\,\%$; no shard
reaches it, the worst being $0.86\,\%$. Pooled by dataset the rates are
$0.38\,\%$ for TriviaQA, $0.39\,\%$ for NQ-Open and $0.07\,\%$ for SVAMP.

\paragraph{Excluded prompts.} On a few prompts greedy decoding enters a
repetition loop and never terminates within the feature budget; a diagnostic
at a $2048$-token ceiling confirmed these are genuine loops, so a larger cap
would not rescue them. Such a prompt keeps its ten stochastic samples in the
entropy analysis but carries no probe feature, and is skipped by the probes.
This affects $33$ of the $11\,700$ prompts in the evaluation grid
($0.28\,\%$). Because the greedy response is decoded deterministically, the
affected prompts are identical across all three seeds, which is what makes the
exclusion a property of the prompt rather than of the draw.

So that the primary short-answer table compares like with like, every method
in it is scored on the same $99.72\,\%$ of prompts for which all methods
produce a score. The confirmatory semantic-versus-surface comparison is the
exception: neither score needs the greedy feature, so both are defined on all
$11\,700$ prompts and that comparison is paired on the full set.

\paragraph{Empty samples from Mistral-7B.} One further artifact is recorded
because it damaged an earlier set of runs. The answer-extraction step strips
stop markers that include Mistral's own chat-template tokens, so a sample
opening with a new conversational turn was cut to the empty string, which then
formed its own cluster and inflated the entropy. In the sealed runs it is
effectively gone: no cell exceeds one empty sample in $5\,000$
($\le 0.02\,\%$), and the two Llama generators sit at $0.00\,\%$. The effect
would in any case attenuate rather than inflate measured performance, so no
Mistral number in this thesis is flattered by it.

\section{Protocol provenance}
\label{sec:protocol-provenance}

Every number reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} comes from one family of
runs. A frozen master protocol fixes the prompt sets, the model and tokenizer
revisions, the decoding budgets, the sampling parameters, the seeds, the
train/eval splits and the code commit. One instance of that protocol is derived
per sampling temperature, so the five temperatures differ in exactly one
declared parameter and in nothing else. No result in this thesis mixes
protocols.

The binding is mechanical rather than declarative. Before any artifact is accepted, every reported metric is recomputed from the per-record files and each artifact is fixed by content hash. A cell whose recorded metadata disagrees with the manifest in any field, including clustering backend, revision pin, split role, or decoding budget, is rejected outright rather than kept with a note. Every artifact behind a number in this thesis is bound this way, including the supporting analyses as well as the headline ones. The manifest digests are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}.


What still distinguishes the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} from one
another is statistical, not procedural. The semantic-versus-surface comparison
is preregistered, and its significance is assessed against a fixed bootstrap
over prompt clusters; the remaining comparisons are descriptive, and are read
for direction and magnitude. That distinction is stated where it applies rather
than attached to every caption.

One figure (Figure~\ref{fig:regime-split}) places short-answer and biography results side by side because the short-versus-long contrast is the central claim of this thesis. The two biography groups are shaded and separated by a divider there, and comparisons are made only between the two bars of one group. The three regimes are graded differently: short answers against reference answers, whole paragraphs by an LLM judge, and individual claims against published human labels. Their absolute AUROC values are therefore not commensurable.

One further difference concerns the biography experiment and cannot be

\section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation-metrics}

The main evaluation framework is \emph{selective prediction}. The idea
is to use the uncertainty score as a ranking signal: examples with the
highest uncertainty scores are flagged as ``probably wrong'' and would
in deployment be rejected, deferred to a human, or routed to a more
expensive verification step. The thesis reports three quantities.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{AUROC} (area under the receiver operating characteristic
        curve). Treating ``incorrect'' as the positive class, AUROC is
        the probability that a randomly chosen incorrect record
        receives a higher uncertainty score than a randomly chosen
        correct record. An AUROC of 0.5 corresponds to random ranking;
        1.0 is perfect ranking. Values around 0.7--0.9 indicate
        moderate-to-strong ranking in this study; practical usefulness
        depends on the operating threshold and the relative costs of
        missed and false alerts. Because AUROC depends only on the
        \emph{ranking} of scores, the unit of the entropy (nats
        vs.\ bits) does not affect it.
  \item \emph{Rejection-accuracy curve}. For each rejection fraction
        $f\in[0,1]$, the curve shows the accuracy obtained on the
        remaining $1-f$ fraction of records after rejecting the $f$
        fraction with the highest uncertainty. A useful uncertainty
        signal makes the curve rise above the flat ``raw accuracy''
        baseline.
  \item \emph{AURAC} (area under the rejection-accuracy curve). A
        single-number summary of the rejection-accuracy curve. Unlike
        AUROC it depends on how accurate the model was to begin with,
        so AURAC values are comparable only within the same task and
        model.
\end{itemize}

A small worked example makes both concrete. Suppose a detector scores five
answers, of which three are correct (C) and two are wrong (W):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Answer & \#1 & \#2 & \#3 & \#4 & \#5 \\
\midrule
Detector score & 0.10 & 0.20 & 0.25 & 0.30 & 0.90 \\
Actually & C & C & W & C & W \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{AUROC by hand.} Form every (wrong, correct) pair; there are
$2 \times 3 = 6$. The wrong answer gets the higher score in five of the six
pairs; the only failure is the pair (\#3, \#4), where the wrong answer \#3
($0.25$) scores below the correct answer \#4 ($0.30$). So
AUROC $= 5/6 \approx 0.83$: a good but imperfect ranking.
\emph{AURAC by hand.} Start from the raw accuracy, $3/5 = 0.60$. Reject the
highest-scoring answer (\#5, wrong): accuracy of the rest rises to
$3/4 = 0.75$. Reject the next (\#4, correct): accuracy dips to
$2/3 \approx 0.67$, because the detector discarded a good answer. Reject the
next (\#3, wrong): accuracy reaches $2/2 = 1.00$. Rejecting \#2 leaves the
correct answer \#1, so the final non-empty retained set also has accuracy
$1.00$. The rejection--accuracy curve is therefore the sequence
$0.60, 0.75, 0.67, 1.00, 1.00$, and under the implementation's stepwise
definition AURAC is the mean over these five non-empty retention levels,
$0.803$. For both metrics, \emph{higher is better}.

Raw answer accuracy is reported only as context; it is not sufficient
on its own, because the practical goal is to decide when a model
should answer, abstain, or route the prompt to a more expensive
verification procedure, as in the selective-prediction framing of
semantic entropy~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

Two kinds of uncertainty are reported for these metrics, and they are
kept strictly separate. The \emph{cross-seed spread} (the $\pm$ values
in the result tables) is the standard deviation of a metric over the
three decoding seeds; with only three seeds it indicates stability
rather than statistical significance.

Significance claims instead use a \emph{prompt-cluster paired bootstrap}.
Prompts are resampled with replacement $10\,000$ times; each resample is
applied identically to all three seeds, and the per-seed AUROC difference
between the two detectors is recomputed and averaged over seeds. Both
detectors are always scored on exactly the same records within a seed, so
the comparison is paired and far more sensitive than comparing two
independent averages. A difference is reported as significant when the
resulting $95\,\%$ interval excludes zero after Holm correction across the
family of tests. Holm's step-down procedure~\cite{holm_simple_1979} is what
makes that family-wise rather than per-cell: the nine $p$-values are ordered,
the smallest is tested against $\alpha/9$, the next against $\alpha/8$, and so
on until one fails, after which the remainder are retained. Nine independent
chances at $\alpha = 0.05$ would otherwise yield at least one spurious
``significant'' cell about a third of the time. Holm is used in preference to
Bonferroni because it guarantees the same family-wise error rate while
rejecting at least as often.

\paragraph{What the interval is an interval over.} The resampling unit is
the prompt, not the seed. The estimand is therefore the mean per-seed AUROC
difference \emph{conditional on seeds 42, 43 and 44}, not a statement about
the population of decoding seeds. This is a measured design decision rather
than a convenience: the between-seed standard deviation of the primary
comparison is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the bootstrap
interval half-width in every cell, so prompt sampling, not seed sampling, is
what limits precision at $N=500$ (or $300$ for SVAMP). Additional seeds
would shrink the smaller component only, and a seed-variance component
estimated from three seeds would in any case be badly underpowered. The
per-seed differences are reported alongside every interval so the seed
spread stays visible.

\paragraph{Confirmatory versus exploratory.} Exactly one comparison is
preregistered and confirmatory: question-conditioned discrete semantic
entropy against the normalized surface baseline across the nine short-answer
task--model cells, with Holm correction over that nine-member family. Every
other comparison reported in this thesis, including clustering backends,
probes, \mbox{P(True)}, KLE, temperature, and both long-form analyses, is
exploratory and is described as such. The distinction matters because it
prevents the negative headline result from being read as the outcome of a
search across many comparisons for the most striking one.


\section{Implementation and HPC execution}
\label{sec:hpc-execution}

This section records how the design above was implemented and run. The
work used the NHR@FAU computing center (Nationales Höchstleistungsrechnen
an der Friedrich-Alexander-Universität): its GPU cluster \emph{Alex}
(NVIDIA A100 nodes) for all generation and GPU-heavy steps, and its CPU
cluster \emph{Fritz} for the lighter posthoc reclustering and KLE scoring
that needs no GPU. The scope of these results matters for reading them correctly. The entropy
baselines and the NLI replication are the primary runs. The larger judges,
KLE and the probes are posthoc checks computed on the \emph{same} saved
samples, so a change in their scores reflects the judge or the estimator,
not a new set of generations.

\paragraph{Code and libraries.} All code, configuration, and batch
scripts are in a small public Python package
(\texttt{semantic\_entropy}), installed with \texttt{uv} and targeting
Python~3.11+. Generation uses vLLM for fast sampling; the probe-collection
runs instead use the HuggingFace \texttt{transformers} path, because vLLM
does not expose the hidden states a probe needs. NLI clustering uses a
DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder, KLE uses sentence-transformer encoders, and the
small logistic-regression probes use scikit-learn. The pipeline reads a
processed dataset, samples $M$ answers per prompt, normalizes them,
clusters them with the configured backend, computes the entropy scores,
attaches a correctness label, and writes the JSONL schema above; the
same script serves both the baseline and the replication, with the
clustering backend chosen by a command-line flag.

\paragraph{What ran where.} Generation and NLI clustering ran on Alex
(a single A100 for the 7B/8B models; four A100-80GB GPUs with tensor
parallelism for the 70B model, with the NLI cross-encoder on CPU so the
GPUs stayed free for generation). The DeBERTa-v3-large reclustering and
KLE rescoring ran on Fritz; the Qwen2.5-72B judge, being itself a 72B
model, ran on Alex. All GPU work went through the Slurm batch scheduler.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and auditing.} Every run writes a
\texttt{job\_meta.json} recording the Slurm job id, host, model, dataset,
full decoding parameters, seed, backend, and input path: enough to
reproduce or audit it later. Source code, configs, small result tables,
and final figures are version-controlled; bulky artifacts (model caches,
raw generations, hidden states) live in separate project storage. Analysis is deliberately separable from the runs: every reported number is
recomputed from the saved per-record JSONL by a single aggregation step, and
an independent audit checks those values against the reported tables. That
separation is also what makes the worked examples of
Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits} possible.
