\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{ch:experimental_design}

This chapter specifies the experimental design used to evaluate whether
semantic entropy provides a better hallucination and abstention signal
than surface-form uncertainty. The design is split into two phases.
Phase~1 establishes an exact-match clustering baseline that is small
enough to debug locally before being run at evaluation scale. Phase~2
repeats the same measurement plan with NLI clustering on NHR@FAU
resources. Chapter~\ref{ch:hpc} then covers execution, storage, and
reproducibility on the cluster.

\section{Datasets}

The thesis uses three datasets: TriviaQA~\cite{joshi_triviaqa_2017},
SVAMP~\cite{patel_svamp_2021}, and a FactScore-style biography
generation task~\cite{min_factscore_2023}. TriviaQA provides
open-domain question answering with short factual answers, e.g.\
\textit{``What is the capital of France?''}.
SVAMP provides short math word problems for which answer normalization
and semantic equivalence matter at the level of numbers rather than
phrasings, e.g.\ \textit{``Mia has 3 apples. She buys 4 more. How many
apples does she have now?''}. The biography task prompts the model with
\textit{``Tell me a bio of \{entity\}.''} for a held-out set of person
entities and elicits a multi-sentence paragraph rather than a short
answer. The first two datasets are small enough to support repeated
sampling while exercising two distinct short-answer failure modes ---
factual recall and structured arithmetic reasoning --- and the
biography task adds a third, qualitatively different regime in which
each sample is a free-form paragraph rather than a normalizable token
string. This long-form regime is the setting in which surface-form and
meaning-level uncertainty are most likely to diverge, because after
normalization the ten samples for a biography prompt are almost always
ten distinct strings, so exact-match clustering can no longer collapse
them. Example records are reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:experiment-details}.

The original semantic entropy evaluation~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}
reports results across five datasets: TriviaQA, SQuAD, BioASQ, NQ-Open,
and SVAMP. Of these, three (BioASQ, NQ-Open, TriviaQA) are open-domain
factual question-answering tasks that differ primarily in domain rather
than in task structure, and TriviaQA is the most widely used and the
primary reference dataset in the original experiments. SQuAD is a
reading-comprehension dataset whose original benchmark design includes
source passages; Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} make it context-free by withholding
those passages, which still leaves a different dataset origin and
prompt assumption from open-domain recall tasks. The scope
used here therefore covers two qualitatively distinct short-answer task
types --- open-domain factual recall and short-answer arithmetic ---
together with one long-form generation task, biography, that probes the
regime the original five short-answer datasets do not reach. Adding the
biography task rather than a fourth short-answer benchmark is
deliberate: the original semantic-entropy work~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}
also evaluates a long-form biography setting, and it is precisely where
the surface-form baseline is expected to break down, so it is the most
informative single addition for testing whether meaning-level clustering
earns its cost. Expanding to NQ-Open or BioASQ remains a straightforward
extension because both share the same short free-text answer format and
normalization approach as TriviaQA. SQuAD~\cite{rajpurkar_squad_2016},
BioASQ~\cite{tsatsaronis_bioasq_2015}, and
NQ-Open~\cite{lee_nq_open_2019} are not used here;
TruthfulQA~\cite{lin_truthfulqa_2022} is likewise omitted because the
design isolates semantic
clustering on short-answer tasks rather than broader
truthfulness.

\section{Models and the two phases}

The two language models used as generators in both phases are
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\cite{jiang_mistral_2023} and
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct~\cite{grattafiori_llama3_2024}. They span the
same small/large open-weights pairing that Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} use within the LLaMA-2 family
(LLaMA-2-Chat-7B and LLaMA-2-Chat-70B), but both substitutions are
deliberately upward: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 reports a substantial
improvement over LLaMA-2-Chat-7B on standard knowledge-recall and
reasoning benchmarks~\cite{jiang_mistral_2023}, sitting in
approximately the open-weights analogue of the GPT-3.5 capability
band that Farquhar et al.\ assume for their judge model, and
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct sits above LLaMA-2-Chat-70B across MMLU,
TriviaQA, and GSM8K~\cite{grattafiori_llama3_2024}. Choosing newer
open instruction-tuned models rather than reproducing the LLaMA-2
generation thus moves the experiments to a regime that is closer to
how current chat models are actually deployed, while keeping the
seven-billion-vs-seventy-billion contrast that motivates Phase~1 and
Phase~2 in the first place. The NLI judge in Phase~2 is a local DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder rather
than the proprietary GPT-3.5 used by Farquhar et al.
Using a local model rather than a closed API matters for several
reasons: the model version is fixed and publicly available, so results
are fully reproducible without requiring access to any external service;
there are no per-call costs or rate limits; and no prompt or response
data leaves the local system.
The whole pipeline therefore runs without dependence on a closed API;
a posthoc rerun in which this cross-encoder is replaced
with a large open instruction-tuned judge is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}. The methodological implications of these
substitutions are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:comparison-farquhar}.

\paragraph{Phase~1: exact-match surface-form baseline.}
Phase~1 records the surface-form baseline. For each prompt, $M=10$
answers are sampled, lightly normalized, and clustered by exact-match
on the normalized string, as defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Phase~1 therefore acts both as a
sanity check on the full pipeline --- generation, normalization,
clustering, entropy scoring, correctness labeling, JSONL output ---
and as the surface-form reference against which the Phase~2 numbers are
compared. Because exact-match clustering ignores paraphrases, the
Phase~1 entropy score is reported as a surface-form baseline rather
than as semantic entropy. The corresponding run artifacts are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:runs}.

\paragraph{Phase~2: NLI clustering.}
Phase~2 keeps the same model--dataset grid, prompt subset, decoding
settings, and seed, but writes a separate output record in which the
exact-match clustering step is replaced with the bidirectional-NLI rule
from Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Correctness labels are
recomputed for the Phase~2 records. The NLI backend used is a
Sentence-Transformers DeBERTa-v3-base
cross-encoder~\cite{sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_base_2022},
a DeBERTa-v3 model~\cite{he_debertav3_2021} fine-tuned on SNLI and
MultiNLI~\cite{williams_multinli_2018}. This is the
configuration that corresponds most directly to the original
semantic-entropy method.

Phase~2 introduces no new research question; it tests whether the
ranking behavior seen in Phase~1 survives when exact-match clustering is
replaced by NLI-defined semantic clustering under the same evaluation
grid. Three posthoc checks are run on top of the Phase~2 outputs (a
larger NLI backend, an LLM-as-judge backend, and Kernel Language
Entropy); they are described next.

\paragraph{The biography task under the same two phases.}
The biography task is not a separate study but a third dataset evaluated
under exactly the same Phase~1 and Phase~2 design. Phase~1 clusters its
samples by exact-match and Phase~2 by semantic clustering, with the only
differences being mechanical: the maximum answer length is raised to
accommodate paragraph-length generations, and because free-form text has
no dataset-specific normalization, the Phase~1 exact-match baseline is
applied to the raw sampled strings. The semantic-clustering step is run
both with the whole-response NLI cross-encoder and with the open
instruction-tuned judge, since paragraph-length pairs are exactly the
case where the two semantic-equivalence backends are most likely to
disagree.

\paragraph{Correctness labeling for long-form generations.}
For TriviaQA and SVAMP a generation is marked correct by normalized
string comparison against the reference answer, the same exact-string
protocol used to cluster Phase~1. This is not available for
paragraph-length biographies, which have no single short ground-truth
string. The biography task therefore grades correctness with the open
instruction-tuned judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) against the first
paragraph of the entity's English Wikipedia article: the judge is asked
whether the generated biography is factually consistent with the
reference, and the binary verdict becomes the correctness label used for
AUROC and AURAC. The reference revision is pinned per record so the
label is reproducible. This protocol matches the long-form correctness
setup of Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; its main
limitation, the judge standing in for ground truth, is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-limitations}.

\section{Sampling settings}
\label{sec:sampling-settings}

All Phase~1 and Phase~2 runs use $M=10$ samples per prompt, temperature
$T=0.7$, and top-$p=0.95$. The maximum answer length is short for the
two short-answer datasets, appropriate for sentence-length question
answering, and is raised for the biography task so that paragraph-length
generations are not truncated. The sample count $M=10$ matches
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; the temperature and
top-$p$ values do not. Farquhar et al.\ use $T=1.0$, top-$p=0.9$, and
$\textit{top-k}=50$. The thesis uses $T=0.7$ and top-$p=0.95$ because
those are the default decoding settings for current
instruction-tuned chat models including Mistral-7B-Instruct and the
Llama-3.1 instruct family, so they correspond to the regime in which
these models are normally deployed. We fix one configuration across
both phases and both models rather than running a temperature sweep:
the goal is to compare clustering rules and judges under a single
sampling regime, not to characterize the temperature sensitivity of
semantic entropy. The implications of this choice are revisited in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison-farquhar}. The meaning of $M$, $T$, and
top-$p$ is explained in Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}. Each run logs its decoding
configuration into the JSONL record.

\section{Baselines, methods, and posthoc checks}

The surface-form baseline is entropy over distinct normalized strings.
It addresses a single question: does semantic clustering add value
beyond observing that sampled outputs differ lexically? The core method
is discrete semantic entropy with bidirectional NLI clustering.
SelfCheckGPT~\cite{manakul_selfcheckgpt_2023} and
P(True)~\cite{kadavath_language_models_know_2022} are related
sampling-based baselines but were not included in the recorded runs.

Following the Phase~2 main runs, three posthoc check families were
completed: KLE with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and nli-roberta-large sentence
encoders, DeBERTa-v3-large reclustering, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
reclustering. These checks reuse
the saved Phase~2 answer samples, so any metric change comes from the
semantic judge or entropy estimator rather than from new generations.
The KLE method is defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:extensions}; all posthoc
results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}.

\section{Output schema}

Each recorded run writes JSONL --- one JSON object per line --- with
exactly one record per prompt. The schema below makes generation,
clustering, scoring, and evaluation auditable while leaving extensions
free to add method-specific fields:

\begin{verbatim}
{
  "run_id": "string",
  "phase": "phase1|phase2",
  "cluster": "local|alex",
  "prompt_id": "string",
  "dataset": "triviaqa|svamp|...",
  "split": "validation|train|test",
  "prompt": "string",
  "reference_answers": ["string"],
  "model": "string",
  "model_tier": "7b_8b|70b_plus",
  "decoding": {
    "num_samples": 10,
    "temperature": 0.7,
    "top_p": 0.95,
    "max_new_tokens": 64,   // dataset-specific
    "seed": 42
  },
  "sampled_answers": ["string"],
  "normalized_answers": ["string"],
  "semantic_clusters": ["integer_per_sample"],
  "cluster_representatives": ["string"],
  "scores": {
    "naive_sample_entropy": 0.0,
    "surface_entropy": 0.0,
    "discrete_semantic_entropy": 0.0
    // optional posthoc/extension fields:
    // "kle": 0.0,
    // "probe_uncertainty": 0.0
  },
  // optional (SEP collection runs only):
  // "hidden_states": [[float, ...], ...],
  // shape: (num_samples, hidden_dim)
  "entailment_backend":
    "exact-match|nli-deberta-v3-base|nli-deberta-v3-large|"
    "llm-judge-qwen2.5-72b-instruct",
  "correctness_label": "boolean"
}
\end{verbatim}

In Phase~1 \texttt{surface\_entropy} and
\texttt{discrete\_semantic\_entropy} coincide by construction, because
both count clusters induced by normalized exact-match strings.
\texttt{naive\_sample\_entropy} is kept as a separate raw-string
baseline, where unnormalized spelling, casing, punctuation, and
whitespace variants remain distinct. The SEP collection runs use the
exact-match clustering backend (so \texttt{discrete\_semantic\_entropy}
again equals \texttt{surface\_entropy} on those records) and additionally
write a \texttt{hidden\_states} array per record; held-out evaluation
splits also carry a \texttt{probe\_uncertainty} score in $[0,1]$.
Larger raw generations, model caches, and full intermediate outputs
are described by the storage and reproducibility policy in
Chapter~\ref{ch:hpc}.

\section{Validation checks}
\label{sec:validation-checks}

Every recorded run is checked against the following structural
requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item every prompt has exactly $M$ sampled answers;
  \item every sampled answer has a normalized form;
  \item every answer receives a semantic cluster identifier, and the
    cluster list contains one identifier per sampled answer;
  \item entropy scores are finite and non-negative;
  \item correctness labels are present for all evaluated prompts;
  \item decoding settings, model names, clustering backend, dataset
    split, and random seeds are recorded.
\end{enumerate}

A manual audit on a handful of records additionally checks that the
clusters look reasonable; the audit is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:cluster-audit}.

\section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation-metrics}

The main evaluation framework is \emph{selective prediction}. The idea
is to use the uncertainty score as a ranking signal: examples with the
highest uncertainty scores are flagged as ``probably wrong'' and would
in deployment be rejected, deferred to a human, or routed to a more
expensive verification step. The thesis reports three quantities.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{AUROC} (area under the receiver operating characteristic
        curve). Treating ``incorrect'' as the positive class, AUROC is
        the probability that a randomly chosen incorrect record
        receives a higher uncertainty score than a randomly chosen
        correct record. An AUROC of 0.5 corresponds to random ranking;
        1.0 is perfect ranking.
  \item \emph{Rejection-accuracy curve}. For each rejection fraction
        $f\in[0,1]$, the curve shows the accuracy obtained on the
        remaining $1-f$ fraction of records after rejecting the $f$
        fraction with the highest uncertainty. A useful uncertainty
        signal makes the curve rise above the flat ``raw accuracy''
        baseline.
  \item \emph{AURAC} (area under the rejection-accuracy curve). A
        single-number summary of the rejection-accuracy curve.
\end{itemize}

Raw answer accuracy is reported only as context; it is not sufficient
on its own, because the practical goal is to decide when a model
should answer, abstain, or route the prompt to a more expensive
verification procedure, as in the selective-prediction framing of
semantic entropy~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.
