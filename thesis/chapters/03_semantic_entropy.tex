\chapter{Semantic Entropy}

This chapter defines the method that the thesis implements first: discrete
semantic entropy over sampled answers. The aim is to preserve the central
idea of semantic entropy --- that uncertainty should be measured over
meanings rather than over surface strings --- while keeping the first
reproduction small enough to verify
\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. The
discussion stays at the method level. Chapter 4 turns the method into an
experiment plan, and Chapter 7 keeps probability-weighted variants, kernel
methods, and probe-based approximations outside the main baseline until the
discrete pipeline is stable.

\section{Sampling Setup}

For each question or prompt \(x\), the model is sampled \(M\) times under a
fixed decoding configuration. The resulting answers are
\[
  Y(x) = \{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_M\}.
\]
The choice of \(M\) controls the trade-off between statistical resolution
and compute cost: larger values can reveal more semantic alternatives, but
they also require more generation and more pairwise semantic comparisons.
Chapter 4 fixes the pilot values used for debugging and for evaluation.

The method separates generation from evaluation. The generation step
produces candidate answers; the uncertainty step groups those answers by
meaning and assigns an entropy score; the correctness step labels whether
the evaluated answer record is correct for the benchmark. This separation
matters because semantic entropy estimates uncertainty over semantic
alternatives, not factual truth directly
\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\section{Semantic Clustering by Entailment}

The core operation is a semantic equivalence test between two answers.
Exact string matching is too strict, because two paraphrases can express
the same answer. Token-level similarity is also unreliable, because small
wording changes can alter the fact being asserted. Following the semantic
entropy framing, two answers belong to the same cluster when they are
equivalent in the context of the prompt \cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,
semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

The baseline approximates equivalence with bidirectional entailment. For
answers \(y_i\) and \(y_j\), the method asks whether \(y_i\) entails
\(y_j\) and whether \(y_j\) entails \(y_i\), conditioned on the prompt
\(x\). If both directions hold, the answers are treated as the same
semantic answer. Natural Language Inference is a practical backend for this
decision because it produces the entailment, contradiction, and neutral
labels needed by the clustering rule \cite{williams_multinli_2018}. A
DeBERTa-v3 NLI cross-encoder is the preferred first local backend, because
it is practical for a pilot while avoiding dependence on a proprietary judge
\cite{he_debertav3_2021,sentence_transformers_nli_deberta_v3_base_2022}.

This rule is an approximation rather than a definition of truth. It may
split answers that differ only in harmless detail, and it may merge answers
that hide important factual differences. These errors are recorded as part
of the method's limitations, especially for numeric answers, partial
answers, and answers whose meaning depends on omitted context.

\section{Discrete Semantic Entropy}

After clustering, let \(C(x)=\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}\) be the set of semantic
clusters observed among the \(M\) sampled answers, where \(1 \le K \le M\),
and let \(n_k\) denote the number of sampled answers assigned to cluster
\(c_k\). The empirical probability of cluster \(c_k\) is
\[
  \hat{p}_k = \frac{n_k}{M}.
\]
The discrete semantic entropy score is then
\[
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)
  = - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{p}_k \log \hat{p}_k .
\]
A low score indicates that the samples mostly agree on one semantic
answer. A high score indicates that the samples spread across several
meanings, which the method treats as evidence of semantic uncertainty.
Only observed clusters enter the sum, and the choice of logarithm base
affects only the scale, not the ordering. The thesis reports this
cluster-frequency entropy first; more exact semantic-entropy variants that
use model probabilities remain out of scope until the discrete version and
its failure cases are understood
\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024,kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}.

The principal baseline is naive entropy, or diversity, over answer
strings. This baseline isolates the question of whether semantic clustering
adds value beyond observing that sampled outputs differ lexically.
SelfCheckGPT-style consistency and P(True) are useful later comparisons,
but they should not delay the first discrete semantic entropy
implementation \cite{manakul_selfcheckgpt_2023,
kadavath_language_models_know_2022}.

\section{Worked Example}

The following example makes the pipeline concrete. Consider the factual
question $x = $ ``What is the capital of Australia?'' and suppose the model
produces $M=5$ sampled answers under temperature $T=0.7$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\hline
Index & Sampled answer $y_i$ \\
\hline
1 & \emph{Canberra} \\
2 & \emph{Canberra is the capital city of Australia} \\
3 & \emph{Sydney} \\
4 & \emph{Melbourne} \\
5 & \emph{Canberra} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: Bidirectional entailment.}
The NLI model is applied to each ordered pair $(y_i, y_j)$ conditioned on
$x$. Table~\ref{tab:entailment_example} shows the resulting binary
entailment decisions; a cell is 1 if the row answer entails the column
answer, 0 otherwise.

\begin{table}[h]
\thisfloatsetup{capposition=above}
\centering
\caption{Pairwise entailment matrix for the worked example.
         A cell $(i,j)=1$ means $y_i$ entails $y_j$ given $x$.}
\label{tab:entailment_example}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccccc}
       & $y_1$ & $y_2$ & $y_3$ & $y_4$ & $y_5$ \\\hline
$y_1$ &  --   &   1   &   0   &   0   &   1   \\
$y_2$ &   1   &  --   &   0   &   0   &   1   \\
$y_3$ &   0   &   0   &  --   &   0   &   0   \\
$y_4$ &   0   &   0   &   0   &  --   &   0   \\
$y_5$ &   1   &   1   &   0   &   0   &  --   \\
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: Semantic clustering.}
Two answers belong to the same cluster iff both off-diagonal cells are 1
(bidirectional entailment). Reading from Table~\ref{tab:entailment_example}:
$y_1 \sim y_2$, $y_1 \sim y_5$, and $y_2 \sim y_5$, while $y_3$ and $y_4$
each stand alone. This yields $K=3$ clusters:
\[
  c_1 = \{y_1, y_2, y_5\},\quad
  c_2 = \{y_3\},\quad
  c_3 = \{y_4\}.
\]

\noindent
\textbf{Step 3: Empirical probabilities.}
\[
  \hat{p}_1 = \tfrac{3}{5} = 0.60,\quad
  \hat{p}_2 = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.20,\quad
  \hat{p}_3 = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.20.
\]

\noindent
\textbf{Step 4: Discrete semantic entropy.}
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)
  &= -\bigl(0.60\ln 0.60 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20\bigr) \\
  &= -(-0.306 - 0.322 - 0.322) \\
  &\approx 0.95 \text{ nats.}
\end{align*}

\noindent
\textbf{Step 5: Lexical baseline for comparison.}
The surface-form baseline treats each distinct string as a separate outcome.
With strings $\{$\emph{Canberra}$\times 2$, \emph{Canberra is the capital
city of Australia}$\times 1$, \emph{Sydney}$\times 1$,
\emph{Melbourne}$\times 1\}$ the lexical entropy is:
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{lex}}(x)
  &= -\bigl(0.40\ln 0.40 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20\bigr) \\
  &\approx 1.33 \text{ nats.}
\end{align*}

The lexical score is higher (1.33 vs.\ 0.95) because it treats the
paraphrase \emph{``Canberra is the capital city of Australia''} as a
different answer from \emph{``Canberra''}, inflating the apparent
uncertainty. After semantic clustering, the correct answer class
($c_1$, Canberra) accounts for 60\,\% of samples, and the entropy is
correspondingly lower and better calibrated to the actual semantic
situation. The model is genuinely somewhat uncertain --- two samples
confabulated wrong cities --- but less so than the lexical score implies.

\section{Method Contract}

The method contract is intentionally minimal:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item receive a benchmark prompt and \(M\) sampled answers under a fixed
    decoding configuration;
  \item normalize only obvious formatting differences, such as surrounding
    whitespace and simple answer prefixes;
  \item cluster answers using bidirectional entailment;
  \item compute naive answer-diversity entropy and discrete semantic
    entropy;
  \item attach a correctness label and evaluate whether uncertainty
    predicts incorrect answers.
\end{enumerate}

Chapter 4 defines the concrete JSONL schema, datasets, model tiers, and
validation checks that instantiate this contract.

\section{Evaluation Use}

The entropy score is used as a ranking signal for selective prediction. If
semantic entropy is informative, examples with higher entropy should be
more likely to be incorrect or unsafe to answer directly. The concrete
evaluation metrics are specified in Chapter 4. At the method level, the
relevant point is that uncertainty is useful only when it helps decide
whether a model should answer, abstain, or route the prompt to a more
expensive verification process \cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024,
kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025}.

\section{Scope of Later Extensions}

Several extensions are relevant but lie outside the first implementation.
Kernel Language Entropy, Semantic Energy, Semantic Volume, adaptive
Bayesian semantic entropy, and Semantic Entropy Probes each change either
the entropy estimator, the representation of semantic similarity, the
sampling budget, or the required model access
\cite{nikitin_kernel_language_entropy_2024,ma_semantic_energy_2025,
li_semantic_volume_2025,sun_adaptive_bayesian_semantic_entropy_2026,
kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024}. Chapter 7 treats these as Phase 2
extensions. The first research contribution remains deliberately
conservative: implement the discrete version cleanly, document where
entailment clustering succeeds and where it fails, and only then decide
whether a given extension is worth running.
