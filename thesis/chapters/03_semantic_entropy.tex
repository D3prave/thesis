\chapter{Semantic Entropy and Related Estimators}
\label{ch:semantic_entropy}

This chapter defines the main method used in the thesis: discrete
semantic entropy over sampled answers. The aim is to preserve the
central idea of semantic entropy from
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} --- that uncertainty
should be measured over meanings rather than over surface strings ---
while keeping the implementation small enough to verify. This
meaning-level framing follows Kuhn et
al.~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023} and Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} then turns the method into an
experimental design. The later sections of this chapter
(Sections~\ref{sec:kle}--\ref{sec:other-estimators}) define the related
estimators that the experiments compare against discrete semantic
entropy: Kernel Language Entropy, Semantic Entropy Probes, P(True), and
embedding regression.

\section{Entropy as a measure of uncertainty}

Before describing the algorithm, it helps to recall what the word
``entropy'' means here. For a discrete probability distribution
$p_1,\ldots,p_K$ over $K$ outcomes, the Shannon entropy is
\[
  H(p) \;=\; -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log p_k .
\]
This quantity is the average amount of information that an outcome
carries, measured in nats when $\log$ is the natural logarithm. Its
intuition is more useful here: $H(p)$ is small when the distribution
puts almost all its mass on a single outcome (the next outcome is
``predictable''), and large when the mass spreads out over many
outcomes (we are very uncertain which outcome will come next). The
maximum value for $K$ outcomes is $\log K$, achieved by the uniform
distribution. The base of the logarithm changes the unit but not the
ordering of distributions, so the choice between $\ln$, $\log_2$ and
$\log_{10}$ does not affect ranking metrics such as AUROC. The thesis
uses natural log throughout.

For semantic entropy we use the same formula, but the ``outcomes'' are
not raw strings: they are groups of strings that are considered to mean
the same thing. The entropy is then a measure of \emph{semantic}
uncertainty: how spread out the meanings produced by the model are for
a single prompt.

\section{The method, step by step}

At a high level, the method we use proceeds in the following three
computational steps. Each step is then explained in detail in the rest
of the chapter, and a fully worked numerical example is given in
Section~\ref{sec:worked_example}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sampling.} For a prompt $x$, draw $M$ candidate answers
        $y_1,\ldots,y_M$ from the language model under a fixed
        decoding configuration (temperature, nucleus-sampling
        threshold top-$p$, and maximum token length held constant
        across all $M$ draws; the concrete values are given in the
        Sampling Setup section below).
  \item \textbf{Clustering.} Group the $M$ answers into $K$ clusters
        $c_1,\ldots,c_K$, where two answers are placed in the same
        cluster if they are considered equivalent in the context of
        $x$.
  \item \textbf{Entropy scoring.} Compute the empirical probability
        $\hat{p}_k=n_k/M$ of each cluster and the discrete entropy
        $\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)=-\sum_k \hat{p}_k\log\hat{p}_k$.
\end{enumerate}

An optional fourth step --- attaching a correctness label and using
$\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)$ as a selective-prediction signal ---
belongs to the \emph{evaluation} of the method rather than to the
method itself, and is treated separately in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design}.

Figure~\ref{fig:method-schematic} reproduces the original illustration of
the method. Panel~(a) makes the central point on a short factual
question: when the sampled answers are counted as raw strings,
paraphrases such as ``Paris'' and ``It's Paris'' are treated as different
answers and the naive entropy looks misleadingly high; after the answers
are grouped by meaning, the same samples give a low semantic entropy.
Panel~(b) shows the long-form version used later in this thesis
(FactualBio), where a generated biography is split into individual factual
claims, each claim is re-asked as a question, and the regenerated answers
are grouped by meaning to score how likely that claim is to be a
confabulation.

\section{Sampling setup}

For each prompt $x$ the model is sampled $M$ times under a fixed
decoding configuration. The sampled answers are
\[
  Y(x) = \{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_M\}.
\]
Concretely, the language model defines a conditional distribution
$p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ over output token sequences, and sampling means
drawing token sequences from this distribution rather than taking the
single most-likely sequence. The randomness is controlled by two
parameters:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{temperature} $T$. The model's token logits are divided by
        $T$ before applying the softmax. $T=0$ collapses sampling to
        the deterministic ``greedy'' choice and produces zero
        diversity, while larger $T$ flattens the distribution and
        produces more diverse samples.
  \item \emph{nucleus sampling} (top-$p$). At each token step only the
        smallest set of tokens whose probability mass adds to at
        least $p$ is kept; the rest are discarded.
\end{itemize}

The choice of $M$ controls a trade-off between statistical resolution
and compute cost: larger $M$ can reveal more semantic alternatives but
also requires more generation and (in the NLI condition) more pairwise
semantic comparisons. The concrete values of $M$, $T$, and top-$p$ used
in the recorded runs are part of the experimental design in
Section~\ref{sec:sampling-settings}.

\section{Clustering by equivalence}
\label{sec:clustering}

The crucial design choice is the equivalence relation used in the
clustering step. We use two:

\paragraph{Normalized exact match.} The simplest possible relation:
$y_i \sim_{\mathrm{exact}} y_j$ iff their normalized strings are
identical, where ``normalization'' means lowercasing, stripping
whitespace and obvious answer prefixes such as ``the answer is''. This
is a \emph{surface-form} grouping, not a semantic one. As emphasized in
the introduction, the exact-match baseline is therefore not true semantic
entropy in Farquhar et
al.'s sense~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; it just
collapses spelling and formatting variation. Throughout the thesis,
exact-match results will be labeled as either ``surface entropy''
(when the underlying distinct strings are counted) or as
``exact-match-clustered entropy''.

\paragraph{Bidirectional NLI.} Rather than use string
identity, we ask whether two answers entail each other in the context
of the prompt. For answers $y_i$ and $y_j$ the method asks whether
$y_i$ entails $y_j$ \emph{and} whether $y_j$ entails $y_i$, both
conditioned on $x$. If both directions hold, the answers are treated as
the same semantic answer:
\[
  y_i \sim_{\mathrm{sem}} y_j
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  e(y_i,y_j\mid x)=1 \ \text{and}\ e(y_j,y_i\mid x)=1.
\]
This bidirectional-entailment rule is the practical approximation of
``meaning equivalence'' proposed by Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}; it is an approximation rather
than a definition, because NLI judgments made by a small cross-encoder
can be noisy. In particular it may split answers that differ only in
harmless detail and merge answers that hide important factual
differences. The empirical impact of these errors is examined through
the worked examples in Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits} and the discussion
in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}. The backends used to compute
$e(\cdot,\cdot\mid x)$ are DeBERTa NLI cross-encoders (v3-base,
v3-large, and v2-xlarge-MNLI) and a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct LLM judge;
DeBERTa-v3-large is the canonical backend for the headline results,
and the sensitivity to this choice is quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}.

The grouping uses the same bidirectional-entailment criterion as Farquhar
et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, but computes the groups in a
slightly different (and equivalent in intent) way. Rather than scanning
the answers one at a time and comparing each to a single cluster
representative, the implementation here tests \emph{every} pair of
sampled answers for mutual entailment and then forms the
\emph{connected groups} of the resulting graph: any two answers that
entail each other in both directions are placed together, and this
merging is transitive --- if answer~A matches answer~B and B matches~C,
then A, B and C all end up in one group, even if A and C were never a
direct match. Algorithm~\ref{alg:clustering} states this procedure
exactly as implemented, and the worked example in
Section~\ref{sec:worked_example} shows it on a small case, reading the
groups directly off the pairwise entailment matrix.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Bidirectional-entailment clustering by connected components, as
  implemented in this thesis
  (\texttt{semantic\_entropy.clustering.nli\_cluster}).}
\label{alg:clustering}
\KwIn{question $x$; normalized answers $y_1,\ldots,y_M$; NLI model
  $\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot\mid x)$ returning entailment, neutral, or
  contradiction}
\KwOut{one cluster identifier per answer}
initialise union--find: $\mathrm{parent}[i] \gets i$ for $i=1,\ldots,M$\;
\For{$i \gets 1$ \KwTo $M-1$}{
  \For{$j \gets i+1$ \KwTo $M$}{
    $a \gets \mathcal{M}(y_i, y_j \mid x)$\tcp*{does $y_i$ entail $y_j$?}
    $b \gets \mathcal{M}(y_j, y_i \mid x)$\tcp*{and $y_j$ entail $y_i$?}
    \If{$a$ is entailment \textbf{and} $b$ is entailment}{
      $\textsc{union}(i,j)$\tcp*{merge the two answers' groups}
    }
  }
}
form groups $C$ from the connected components of the union--find\;
\Return $C$ (one cluster id per answer; the first-seen answer is each
  group's representative)\;
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discrete semantic entropy}

After clustering, let $C(x)=\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}$ be the resulting set of
clusters and let $n_k$ be the number of sampled answers assigned to
cluster $c_k$. The empirical probability of cluster $c_k$ is
\[
  \hat{p}_k \;=\; \frac{n_k}{M},
\]
and the discrete semantic entropy score is the Shannon entropy of these
cluster probabilities,
\[
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)
  \;=\; -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{p}_k \log \hat{p}_k .
\]
A low score means the samples mostly agree on one cluster, i.e.\ on one
meaning, while a high score means the samples spread across several
clusters. Since outcomes correspond to clusters, entropy is at most
$\log K \le \log M$. The recorded experiments use $M=10$, giving a
maximum possible value of $\ln 10 \approx 2.30$, while the smaller
worked example in Section~\ref{sec:worked_example} uses $M=5$, with
maximum $\ln 5 \approx 1.61$.

\paragraph{The estimator family.} The full evaluation grid reports
both this count-based estimator and the probability-weighted variants
of Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024,%
kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}, which use length-normalized
sequence log-probabilities instead of sample counts. The four
sampling-based entropy estimators are organized by two axes ---
whether paraphrases are clustered by meaning, and whether the score
uses the model's sequence probabilities or just occurrence counts:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & Count-based & Probability-weighted \\
\midrule
Surface only & \makecell[l]{naive sample entropy (raw strings),\\
  surface entropy (normalized strings)} &
  \makecell[l]{naive entropy\\(the paper's naive baseline)} \\
Semantic & \makecell[l]{discrete semantic entropy\\
  (the paper's discrete approx.)} &
  \makecell[l]{full semantic entropy\\(the paper's primary estimator)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The naming is unfortunately confusing, so it is worth stating plainly.
Two of these scores ignore meaning and just look at the answer strings:
\emph{naive sample entropy} counts the raw strings and \emph{surface
entropy} counts them after light clean-up (these are the project's
``surface'' baselines). One score, the paper's \emph{naive entropy}, also
ignores meaning but weights each distinct string by how likely the model
thought it was. The two \emph{semantic} scores first group answers by
meaning: \emph{discrete semantic entropy} counts the groups, and \emph{full
semantic entropy} weights them by probability. A small but useful fact:
when every answer is equally likely, the full version reduces exactly to
the discrete one (the code checks this). In short, the surface baselines
test whether grouping by meaning helps at all, and the
probability-weighted versions test whether the model's own confidence in
each answer adds anything.

\section{Worked example}
\label{sec:worked_example}

The following example makes the pipeline concrete. Consider the factual
question $x = $ ``What is the capital of Australia?''. We instruct the
language model to produce $M=5$ sampled answers under temperature
$T=0.7$. ``Sampling'' here means we run the same prompt through the
model five times with the same decoding configuration but a different
random seed each time, so that we get five different (but plausible)
completions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\hline
Index & Sampled answer $y_i$ \\
\hline
1 & \emph{Canberra} \\
2 & \emph{Canberra is the capital city of Australia} \\
3 & \emph{Sydney} \\
4 & \emph{Melbourne} \\
5 & \emph{Canberra} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The example is intentionally small ($M=5$) and intentionally a case in
which the model is mostly right but somewhat uncertain --- a useful
sanity check on the pipeline.

\paragraph{Step 1: Bidirectional entailment.}
The NLI model is applied to each ordered pair $(y_i, y_j)$ conditioned
on $x$. Table~\ref{tab:entailment_example} shows the resulting binary
entailment decisions; a cell $(i,j)$ equals 1 if the row answer entails
the column answer, 0 otherwise.

\begin{table}[h]
\thisfloatsetup{capposition=above}
\centering
\caption{Pairwise entailment matrix for the worked example.
         A cell $(i,j)=1$ means $y_i$ entails $y_j$ given $x$.}
\label{tab:entailment_example}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccccc}
       & $y_1$ & $y_2$ & $y_3$ & $y_4$ & $y_5$ \\\hline
$y_1$ &  --   &   1   &   0   &   0   &   1   \\
$y_2$ &   1   &  --   &   0   &   0   &   1   \\
$y_3$ &   0   &   0   &  --   &   0   &   0   \\
$y_4$ &   0   &   0   &   0   &  --   &   0   \\
$y_5$ &   1   &   1   &   0   &   0   &  --   \\
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Step 2: Semantic clustering.}
Two answers belong to the same cluster iff both off-diagonal cells are
1 (bidirectional entailment). Reading from
Table~\ref{tab:entailment_example}: $y_1 \sim y_2$, $y_1 \sim y_5$, and
$y_2 \sim y_5$, while $y_3$ and $y_4$ each stand alone. This yields
$K=3$ clusters:
\[
  c_1 = \{y_1, y_2, y_5\},\quad
  c_2 = \{y_3\},\quad
  c_3 = \{y_4\}.
\]

\paragraph{Step 3: Empirical probabilities.}
\[
  \hat{p}_1 = \tfrac{3}{5} = 0.60,\quad
  \hat{p}_2 = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.20,\quad
  \hat{p}_3 = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.20.
\]

\paragraph{Step 4: Discrete semantic entropy.}
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)
  &= -\bigl(0.60\ln 0.60 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20\bigr) \\
  &= -(-0.306 - 0.322 - 0.322) \\
  &\approx 0.95 \text{ nats.}
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Comparison: surface-form (lexical) baseline.}
This is not a step of the semantic-entropy method itself but a separate
baseline used for comparison in the experiments. The surface-form
baseline treats each distinct string as a separate outcome. With
strings $\{$\emph{Canberra}$\times 2$, \emph{Canberra is the capital
city of Australia}$\times 1$, \emph{Sydney}$\times 1$,
\emph{Melbourne}$\times 1\}$, the surface-form entropy is
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{lex}}(x)
  &= -\bigl(0.40\ln 0.40 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.20\ln 0.20\bigr) \\
  &\approx 1.33 \text{ nats.}
\end{align*}

The surface-form score is higher (1.33 vs.\ 0.95) because it treats the
paraphrase \emph{``Canberra is the capital city of Australia''} as a
different answer from \emph{``Canberra''}, inflating the apparent
uncertainty. After semantic clustering, the correct answer class
($c_1$, Canberra) accounts for 60\,\% of samples, and the entropy is
correspondingly lower and arguably better calibrated to the actual
semantic situation. The model is genuinely somewhat uncertain --- two
samples confabulated wrong cities --- but less so than the surface-form
score implies.

\section{Method contract}

A ``method contract'' here means the inputs and outputs that the
discrete-semantic-entropy algorithm \emph{itself} requires, independent
of the larger pipeline that wraps it (which is the topic of
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design}). Intentionally, this contract is
minimal:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Input.} A benchmark prompt $x$ together with $M$ sampled
        answers $y_1,\ldots,y_M$ produced under a fixed decoding
        configuration.
  \item \emph{Normalization.} Apply only obvious formatting
        normalization to each $y_i$: strip surrounding whitespace and
        a small set of simple answer prefixes (e.g.\ ``the answer
        is''). No paraphrase normalization is attempted.
  \item \emph{Clustering.} Group the normalized answers using the
        configured equivalence relation, which is either normalized
        exact match or bidirectional NLI entailment.
  \item \emph{Output.} Return the cluster assignments
        $\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}$, the naive surface-form entropy
        $\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{lex}}(x)$ over normalized strings, and
        the discrete semantic entropy
        $\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{sem}}(x)$ over cluster frequencies.
\end{enumerate}

Correctness labeling and selective-prediction evaluation use these
outputs but are part of the surrounding evaluation pipeline, not of the
method itself.

\section{Related estimators evaluated alongside}

Discrete semantic entropy is the central method, but it is not the only
way to turn a model's behaviour into an uncertainty score. The
experiments compare it against four further estimators, defined here so
that the results chapter can refer to them by name. Two of them
(Kernel Language Entropy and Semantic Entropy Probes) come from the
recent literature and address a practical weakness of the main method:
its cost. Computing discrete semantic entropy needs $M$ sampled answers
per question and up to $M^2$ pairwise entailment checks to build the
clusters --- affordable for a study, but heavy at deployment scale. The
other two (P(True) and embedding regression) are simple, widely used
baselines.

\subsection{Kernel Language Entropy}
\label{sec:kle}

Kernel Language Entropy
(KLE)~\cite{nikitin_kernel_language_entropy_2024} keeps the idea of
measuring how spread out the sampled answers are, but replaces the
\emph{hard} yes/no clustering step with a \emph{soft}, continuous
measure of semantic spread. It still draws the same $M$ samples, but
instead of grouping them with NLI it compares them through
sentence-embedding similarity.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Embed.} Each sampled answer $y_i$ is turned into a dense
    vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ by a sentence-embedding model (the recorded
    runs use a small general encoder, all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and a larger
    NLI-tuned one, nli-roberta-large).
  \item \textbf{Build a similarity matrix.} A Gaussian kernel turns the
    pairwise distances between these vectors into similarities
    $K_{ij}=\exp(-\lVert\mathbf{v}_i-\mathbf{v}_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$,
    with the bandwidth $\sigma^2$ set to the median pairwise distance.
    The matrix is rescaled so its entries behave like a probability
    table (it sums to one along the diagonal).
  \item \textbf{Take the entropy of the matrix.} The KLE score is the
    von~Neumann entropy of that matrix --- which is just the Shannon
    entropy of its eigenvalues. It is low when the answer vectors point
    in nearly the same direction (the samples agree) and high when they
    spread across many directions (the samples disagree).
\end{enumerate}

KLE and discrete semantic entropy measure related but different things:
the discrete score makes a hard merge/split decision for each pair,
while KLE uses the full embedding geometry. They agree at the extremes
(identical answers give zero; totally dissimilar answers give the
maximum) and disagree mainly on the boundary cases where the NLI judge
would have made a marginal merge or split. KLE is still $O(M^2)$ in the
samples, but each comparison is a cheap dot product rather than a full
NLI forward pass.

\subsection{Semantic Entropy Probes}
\label{sec:sep-method}

A Semantic Entropy Probe
(SEP)~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024} attacks the cost
problem from the other side: it tries to read the uncertainty directly
off the model's internal state, so that at test time only a
\emph{single} forward pass is needed --- no multi-sampling and no
entailment comparisons.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Training.} On a set of questions for which we already
    have answers and correctness labels, the model is run once per
    question and the hidden state of the last generated token (at the
    final Transformer layer) is recorded as a feature vector. A simple
    logistic-regression classifier is then trained to predict, from that
    vector alone, whether the answer is incorrect. This connects to a
    broader line of work showing that a model's internal representations
    carry signals about
    truthfulness~\cite{burns_latent_knowledge_2023,%
    azaria_internal_state_lying_2023,chen_inside_2024,gao_h_neurons_2025}.
  \item \textbf{Inference.} For a new question, generate one answer, read
    the same hidden state, and apply the trained classifier. Its output
    (a number between 0 and 1) is used as the uncertainty score, larger
    meaning riskier.
\end{itemize}

Because the probe is trained and evaluated on \emph{disjoint} sets of
questions, its scores are a genuine held-out test rather than a re-scoring
of the same records. The feature choices (which layer, how to pool
tokens) are fixed in advance rather than tuned on the test set, so they
do not leak information. The trade-off is the obvious one: the probe is
far cheaper than full semantic entropy, but it can only use what is
linearly readable from one hidden state.

\subsection{P(True) and embedding regression}
\label{sec:other-estimators}

Two further baselines round out the comparison.

\textbf{P(True)} asks the model to grade itself: after it produces an
answer, the model is prompted to say how likely that answer is to be
true, and the probability it assigns to ``true'' is read off as a
(inverted) confidence score. It needs no extra samples and no probe
training, which makes it a natural cheap reference point.

\textbf{Embedding regression} is a close cousin of the SEP probe: the
same kind of linear model trained on the same last-token hidden states,
but supervised directly on \emph{judged correctness} rather than on a
semantic-entropy threshold. It is reported in two forms ---
\emph{in-distribution}, trained and tested on the same task, and
\emph{out-of-distribution}, trained on one task and tested on another ---
so that the experiments can measure how well a hidden-state probe
transfers between datasets.
