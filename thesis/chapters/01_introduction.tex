\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Large language models answer questions fluently even when the answer is wrong.
These confident mistakes are usually called \emph{hallucinations}. Here the
word covers any answer that the input and the known facts do not support.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} makes it precise. The problem is not only that some
answers are wrong. A useful system should also say \emph{when} its answer is
unreliable, so that a person or a later check can decide whether to trust it.

Studies of adversarial prompts in clinical decision support illustrate the
risk~\cite{omar_adversarial_hallucination_clinical_2025}. Checking factual
claims automatically remains
difficult~\cite{augenstein_factuality_challenges_2024}. Language has many ways to
say the same thing. Two samples can use different words for one claim, and two
similar-looking answers can contradict each other. Any method that judges
reliability by comparing answer strings has to separate differences in wording
from differences in meaning.

This thesis builds on Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, who
studied \emph{semantic entropy} as a warning signal that needs no training.
The idea is to measure how much the \emph{meanings} of repeated samples vary
rather than how much the strings vary. If repeated samples mostly say the same
thing, the model may be unsure only about wording. If they split across
different meanings, it is unsure about the content, and the answer is riskier.
Kuhn et al.\ call this idea semantic
uncertainty~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:intro-schematic} shows the idea on a small example.

Semantic entropy does not train or alter the language model. It is calculated
after the model has answered: ask the same question several times, compare the
answers, and turn their disagreement into a risk score.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The method, evaluation procedure, datasets, and question selection follow
Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} and their released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}. Where the paper and code
disagree, this thesis follows the code and records the difference. The
reproduction answers three questions.

The first question is whether semantic entropy detects confabulations better
than the comparison methods in the original paper. Here, a
\emph{confabulation} is a wrong answer whose meaning changes across repeated
samples from the same prompt. A wrong answer that the model repeats every time
creates no disagreement between samples, so semantic entropy cannot detect
it~\cite{kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025,simhi_choke_2025}. The
comparison methods are entropy over sequence log-likelihoods, the
self-evaluation method P(True), and a regression from the model's hidden state
to correctness. Semantic entropy probes from Kossen et
al.~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024} are also included.

The second question is which part of semantic entropy explains any improvement.
Each original comparison method differs from semantic entropy in several ways.
Entropy over log-likelihoods reads a different quantity and does not group
answers. P(True) asks the model to judge its own answer using sampled
alternatives as context. The probes read hidden states rather than answers.
These comparisons can show whether the complete method performs better, but they
cannot show which step is responsible.

This thesis therefore compares the estimators one step at a time. It starts
with entropy over sequence log-likelihoods, then counts repeated answer strings,
normalizes the strings before counting them, groups answers by meaning, and
weights the meaning groups by likelihood. Each comparison changes one main
step. The effect of grouping is tested by comparing discrete semantic entropy
with \emph{surface entropy}, which counts distinct normalized strings but does not
judge whether differently worded answers mean the same thing. Surface entropy
requires no extra model call.

The third question is how strongly the result depends on three evaluation
choices: the model that decides whether two answers mean the same thing, the
rule that decides whether an answer is correct, and the temperature used to
sample answers. The first two choices can be compared on the same stored
answers. Changing the temperature requires generating new samples.

\section{What the study finds}
\label{sec:what-the-study-finds}

On sentence-length answers, semantic entropy performs better than every
comparison method from the original paper. This reproduces the paper's main
comparison. The hidden-state probes were evaluated with a different entailment
model, so their results do not provide a comparison under identical settings.

The step-by-step comparison points to answer normalization rather than grouping
by meaning. Normalization means lowercasing, removing punctuation and articles,
and extracting the number from an answer to an arithmetic problem. It is the
only step that improves the result consistently across the main short-answer
comparisons and by more than the variation between random seeds.

With the entailment model loaded by the released code, grouping by meaning gives
no clear improvement over counting normalized strings. This does not show
that the true effect is exactly zero: small effects may go undetected. Even so,
surface entropy, which never groups by meaning, performs
better than every original comparison method wherever both were tested.

A larger entailment model does show a clear benefit from grouping
sentence-length answers by meaning. The result therefore depends on which
model makes the grouping decisions.

Evaluation choices change the result more than the choice between semantic and
surface entropy. Changing only the entailment model on the same generated
answers changes the score substantially. Sampling temperature changes the
ranking of the methods, and the correctness rule changes which method performs
better. The study has no human correctness labels with which to choose between
the correctness rules. This limits every reported result.

The short-answer study treats its nine dataset--generator pairs as independent
statistical units. The three random seeds are repeated runs within each pair,
not separate independent observations. The main paired bootstrap accounts for
both these pairs and the records within them. The limited number of independent
pairs restricts how precisely small differences can be estimated.

A separate biography experiment is exploratory and is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary-results}. Its incomplete provenance and different
clustering rule limit the conclusions, so it is not part of the main evidence.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The thesis does not introduce semantic entropy. It makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A comparison of semantic entropy with the original study's baselines
    on selected open language models.
  \item A step-by-step comparison that separates the effect of answer
    normalization from the effect of grouping by meaning.
  \item Tests of how the results depend on the entailment model, correctness
    rule, and sampling temperature.
  \item A documented implementation and evaluation procedure that make the
    recorded results traceable and state the limits of their precision.
\end{enumerate}

The implementation follows the released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}, with differences documented.
Checks of prompting choices and the AURAC evaluation metric are secondary
analyses, not separate headline contributions.

\section{Reader's guide}
\label{sec:readers-guide}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} makes the target precise. It separates
confabulation from the other failure modes collected under ``hallucination'',
and explains why measuring uncertainty over free text is harder than over a
fixed set of options. It ends with the gap this thesis addresses.

Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy} defines the method and the estimators
evaluated beside it. Its central section is the clustering step, stated as the
released code performs it rather than as it is usually described.

Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} specifies the experiment. It covers
datasets and question selection, the prompting conditions, the generators and
decoding budgets, the entailment models, the correctness rules, and the metrics.
Appendix~\ref{app:method-details} gives the detailed implementation choices,
execution procedure, and differences from the original study.

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the main short-answer results. It compares the
original methods, tests the individual steps, and examines the evaluation
choices. Appendix~\ref{app:secondary-results} contains the complete comparison
table, secondary analyses, and exploratory biography results.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} explains which step improves the results, what that
improvement depends on, how strongly the evaluation setup affects the reported
scores, and which original findings reproduce.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes and states the limitations. Two of those
bear on every number here. No human labels were collected to check the
correctness judges against. And the entailment model and the judge answer
versions of the same question, so a method that groups by meaning may be favored
when correctness is also judged through meaning. Without human labels, this
study cannot separate that dependence from genuine improvement.
