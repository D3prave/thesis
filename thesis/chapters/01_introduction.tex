\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Large language models answer questions fluently even when the answer is wrong.
These confident mistakes are usually called \emph{hallucinations}. Here the
word covers any answer that the input and the known facts do not support.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} makes it precise. The problem is not only that some
answers are wrong. A useful system should also say \emph{when} its answer is
unreliable, so that a person or a later check can decide whether to trust it.

The risk is real. Under adversarial prompting, hallucination rates in clinical
decision support have been measured as high as
50--82\,\%~\cite{omar_adversarial_hallucination_clinical_2025}. That is an
upper bound rather than a typical rate. But factuality failures are common
enough that checking them automatically is still
hard~\cite{augenstein_factuality_challenges_2024}. The reason matters more here
than the rate. Language has many ways to say the same thing. Two samples can
use different words for one claim, and two similar-looking answers can
contradict each other. Any method that judges reliability by comparing answer
strings has to separate differences in wording from differences in meaning.

This thesis builds on Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, who
introduced \emph{semantic entropy} as a warning signal that needs no training.
The idea is to measure how much the \emph{meanings} of repeated samples vary
rather than how much the strings vary. If repeated samples mostly say the same
thing, the model may be unsure only about wording. If they split across
different meanings, it is unsure about the content, and the answer is riskier.
This follows the semantic uncertainty framing of Kuhn et
al.~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:intro-schematic} shows the idea on a small example.

Semantic
entropy is not a training objective. It is not added to a loss function, it
does not fine-tune anything, and it never changes the model's weights. The
generator is an ordinary trained model used exactly as it comes. Everything
here happens after the model has answered. Ask the same question a few times,
look at the answers, turn them into a risk score.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The method, the evaluation protocol, the
datasets and the question selection all come from Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} and their released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}. Where the paper and the code
disagree, the code is followed and the disagreement is recorded. The
reproduction serves three questions.

\paragraph{RQ1. Does semantic entropy detect confabulations better than the
baselines the original paper uses?} The
target is \emph{confabulation}, defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. It
means an answer whose meaning changes across samples drawn from the same
prompt. Errors the model repeats every time are out of scope by
construction~\cite{kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025,simhi_choke_2025}.
The comparators are the paper's own. They are entropy over sequence
log-likelihoods, the self-evaluation baseline P(True), and a regression from
the model's hidden state to correctness. Semantic entropy probes from Kossen et
al.~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024} are added to those.

\paragraph{RQ2. Which part of the method earns the advantage?} Every baseline
in the original evaluation differs from semantic entropy in more than one way
at once. Entropy over log-likelihoods reads a different quantity \emph{and}
does no grouping. P(True) asks the model about its own answer instead of
sampling. The probes read hidden states rather than outputs. None of them
isolates a single step. So that design cannot say which part earns the gain,
even though it is usually read as crediting one part in particular, the
grouping of answers by meaning.

Breaking the method into steps can say. Semantic entropy differs from the string baseline in four steps. Count repeated strings instead of
reading likelihoods. Normalize those strings before counting. Group the answers
by meaning instead of by exact match. Weight the resulting groups by
likelihood. Comparing each step against the one below it gives the value of
that step on its own, because neighbouring comparisons then differ in
essentially one way. The third step is the one the method is named after. Its
comparator is \emph{surface entropy}, the entropy over distinct normalized
answer strings. It keeps the sampling, the counting and the entropy, and drops
only the judgment of meaning. It costs no model call.

\paragraph{RQ3. How much do measurement choices move the answer?} Three choices
that the literature treats as configuration are varied here on otherwise
identical data. They are the model that decides whether two answers mean the
same thing, the rule that decides whether an answer is correct, and the
temperature the answers are sampled at.

\section{What the study finds}
\label{sec:what-the-study-finds}

\textbf{To RQ1, yes.} On sentence-length answers, semantic entropy leads every
baseline the paper uses. Under the entailment model the released code loads and
an LLM judge for correctness, likelihood-weighted semantic entropy reaches
AUROC 0.758. The comparators reach 0.694 for entropy over log-likelihoods and
0.714 for P(True). The two hidden-state probes were collected on a different
backend, \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large}, and reach 0.718 for the
correctness-trained probe and 0.672 for the semantic entropy probe. That ordering holds in every slice of the data where
each comparator was measured.

\textbf{To RQ2, normalization rather than grouping by meaning.} Of the four
steps, one is clearly worth something. Normalizing the answer strings before
counting them is worth $+0.040$ AUROC. Normalizing means lowercasing, dropping
punctuation and articles, and pulling out the number on the arithmetic task. It
is positive in all 24 slices of the grid and resolved in all 24. It is also the
only step whose margin is larger than the variation between run seeds. Counting
raw strings instead of reading likelihoods is positive in 4 slices of 24.
Weighting the meaning-groups by likelihood adds $+0.002$.

The step the method is named after adds nothing measurable. On the entailment
model the released code actually loads, grouping answers by meaning does not
beat counting normalized strings. The evidence is eight slices, being four
prompting conditions crossed with two independent LLM judges. A paired
bootstrap that resamples both the nine independent (dataset, model) units and
the records inside them resolves none of them. The margins run from $-0.002$ to
$+0.022$ AUROC and every interval covers zero. This is a tested null, not an
underpowered one. Pairing the two methods on the same records was the analysis
that could have found a small consistent effect, and it does not.

The same point can be made without any test. The clustering-free comparator
passes every comparison the original study runs. It beats entropy over
log-likelihoods in 24 slices of 24, P(True) in 6 of 6, and both hidden-state
probes in 6 of 6. No slice anywhere goes the other way. So the evidence the
original assembles is also produced by a method that never judges meaning.

Two conditions change the verdict, and both are stated here so that no later
chapter appears to contradict this one. Substituting a 72-billion-parameter
model as the entailment judge gives a resolved margin of $+0.087$ on
sentence-length answers. And on paragraph-length output no two samples are ever
identical, so every sample forms its own group and the string scores sit at
chance. There, grouping by meaning is not an improvement but a precondition for
measuring anything. The size of the design bounds the null from the other side
too. With nine units the minimum detectable effect is about 0.028 AUROC, and
the intervals computed here reach roughly 0.01 only in the tightest slices.

\textbf{To RQ3, more than the choice of method does.} Holding the generations
fixed and changing only the entailment model moves discrete semantic entropy
from 0.751 to 0.823. That spread is larger than the method's whole margin over
the clustering-free comparator. Sampling temperature reorders the methods.
Diversity-based detectors fall from 0.757 at $T=1.0$ to 0.568 at $T=0.1$, while
the likelihood-based one is nearly flat. The usual setting of $T=1.0$ is the
most favorable point in the range for the sampling-based approach. The
correctness rule flips the sign of the headline contrast. In one condition
semantic entropy leads surface entropy by $+0.020$ under LLM grading and trails
it by $0.051$ under the token-overlap rule the released code uses by default.
This thesis has no human correctness labels to settle which rule is right.
Chapters~\ref{ch:discussion} and~\ref{ch:conclusion} treat that as a limit on
every number here.

One property of the grid matters for every comparison in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The short-answer grid holds nine independent units,
not twenty-seven. The three run seeds sit inside each (dataset, generator) pair
and vary sampling alone. Seeds account for about 15\,\% of the variance of the
semantic-minus-surface contrast, and the differences between units for the
rest. A test that treats the twenty-seven cells as independent overstates the
number of resolved contrasts by roughly a third. Every paired test here resamples units
as well as records.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The method itself is Farquhar et al.'s. What this thesis adds is a
reimplementation faithful to their released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}, together with comparisons
their design does not make. The main one is a four-step decomposition in which
neighbouring steps differ in essentially one way, so the value of each step is
measured on its own. It supplies the comparator the original evaluation leaves
out, and it locates the reliable gain in answer normalization rather than in
grouping by meaning.

Several smaller additions sit beside it: the paired bootstrap that respects the
nine independent units, the entailment model varied afterwards on identical
generations, the $2\times2$ separating instruction wording from worked-example
count, three correctness rules on the same answers, a five-point temperature
sweep, and two findings about the metrics rather than the methods.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion-contributions} states all nine in full.

\section{Reader's guide}
\label{sec:readers-guide}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} makes the target precise. It separates
confabulation from the other failure modes collected under ``hallucination'',
and explains why measuring uncertainty over free text is harder than over a
fixed set of options. It ends with the gap this thesis addresses.

Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy} defines the method and the estimators
evaluated beside it. Its central section is the clustering step, stated as the
released code performs it rather than as it is usually described.

Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} specifies the experiment. It covers
datasets and question selection, the prompting conditions, the generators and
decoding budgets, the entailment backends, the correctness rules, the metrics,
and a register of every point where this study departs from the original.

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the measurements without interpreting them.
It gives the two answer-length regimes, a scorecard setting each claim of the
original beside what was measured here, and then the decomposition.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets them, in three arguments. Which step
earns the advantage and what it depends on. How much of the reported margin
belongs to the measurement setup rather than the method. What does and does not
reproduce.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes and states the limitations. Two of those
bear on every number here. No human labels were collected to check the
correctness judges against. And the entailment model and the judge answer
versions of the same question, so a method that groups by meaning is favored
exactly when correctness is scored by meaning.
