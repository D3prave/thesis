\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Large language models often answer open questions fluently even when the
answer is wrong or unsupported. These confident mistakes are usually called
\emph{hallucinations}. In this thesis the word is used as a broad label for
several different failure modes, including plain misinformation,
confabulation, and systematic reasoning errors; a more careful taxonomy
follows in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. For now the term simply means an
answer that the input and the known facts do not support. The practical
problem is not only that some answers are wrong. It is that a useful system
should also signal \emph{when} its answer is unreliable, so that a person or
an automatic check downstream can decide whether to trust it.

The risk is real in practice. Under adversarial prompting, hallucination
rates in clinical decision support have been measured as high as
50--82\,\%~\cite{omar_adversarial_hallucination_clinical_2025}. That figure
is an adversarial upper bound rather than a typical rate, but factuality
failures are common enough in ordinary use that automatic verification across
deployed models remains
hard~\cite{augenstein_factuality_challenges_2024}. The exact rate matters
less here than the structural difficulty behind it: free-form language has
many valid surface forms. Two samples can use very different words for the
same claim, and two similar-looking answers can make claims that contradict
each other. A method that judges reliability by comparing answer strings
therefore has to separate differences in wording from differences in meaning.

This thesis builds directly on the work of Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, who introduced \emph{semantic
entropy} as a training-free signal for hallucination risk. The idea, taken
from that paper, is to measure how much the \emph{meanings} of repeated
samples vary, rather than how much the strings vary. If repeated samples from
the model mostly express the same meaning, the model may be unsure only about
phrasing. If the samples split across genuinely different meanings, the model
is uncertain about the content itself, and the answer should be treated as
riskier. This follows the semantic uncertainty framing of Kuhn et
al.~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023} and Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:intro-schematic}
illustrates the idea on a small example: the same five samples look very
uncertain when distinct wordings are counted, but mostly agree once they
are grouped by meaning. The aim of this thesis is not to
propose the method, but to re-implement it in a smaller, locally reproducible
setup and to study how well it works there, and under which conditions.

\section{Research Aim}
\label{sec:research-aim}

The main question is whether semantic entropy is a better signal for
hallucination risk and selective abstention (letting the system
decline to answer the questions it is most likely to get wrong) than
surface-form uncertainty,
and how that comparison changes when the output grows from a short answer to
a paragraph. The study uses TriviaQA, NQ-Open, and SVAMP as short-answer
benchmarks and a FactScore-style biography task as the long-form case,
evaluated both for the whole paragraph and at the level of individual claims.
It runs three open generator models, each three times under different random
seeds, so that every headline number comes with a spread across repeated
runs. The target is
\emph{confabulation}-like failure, where the model's answer varies in meaning
across samples drawn from the same prompt; the long-form task also tests the
harder case where surface variation is almost always present. The study does
not claim to detect every kind of hallucination. In particular, it cannot
flag confident systematic errors, where the model is consistently wrong, a
failure mode discussed by Kalai et
al.~\cite{kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025} and Simhi et
al.~\cite{simhi_choke_2025}.

The starting hypothesis, from Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, is that discrete semantic entropy
over entailment-based clusters gives a higher AUROC (a ranking score for
placing incorrect answers above correct ones) than surface-form entropy
over sampled strings on the same prompts. A secondary hypothesis is that any
improvement is at least directionally consistent across the three
short-answer benchmarks, despite their different answer formats. The
biography task tests a complementary hypothesis: that meaning-aware methods
separate more clearly from count-based surface scores once raw surface
diversity stops being informative.
These expectations are referred to below as H1 (semantic entropy beats
surface entropy on short answers), H2 (any improvement is directionally
consistent across the three short-answer benchmarks), and H3
(meaning-aware methods separate clearly from count-based surface scores on the
long-form task).

One result of this study qualifies the first hypothesis (H1) in a way the original
aggregate evaluation does not show. On short answers, the value of
sampling-based methods such as semantic entropy depends strongly on the
decoding temperature. At low temperature the repeated samples are nearly
identical, so there is little diversity to measure and these methods are weak;
at high temperature they become strong. A single-pass self-evaluation baseline
is close to temperature-independent, so the gap between the two closes as
temperature rises --- and on the smaller generators it reverses, while on the
largest it does not within the range measured. At the temperature where
sampling-based methods work best,
semantic entropy is about as good as a much cheaper surface-form entropy on
short answers. The clearer advantage of meaning-aware methods appears on the
long-form task, where count-based surface scores break down. These points are stated
carefully and with their limits in Chapters~\ref{ch:results}
and~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Experimental conditions at a glance}

The study separates the cheap surface-form baselines from the semantic
clustering condition that follows Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} most directly.

\textbf{Surface-form baselines.} For each prompt the generator is sampled
several times, the answers are lightly normalized (case, punctuation,
and whitespace differences are removed, so ``Paris!'' and ``paris''
count as the same string), and two answers are grouped
only if their normalized strings are identical. This is called
\emph{exact-match} clustering, and the entropy over these normalized
string groups is called \emph{surface entropy} throughout. A
deliberately naive companion score, \emph{naive sample entropy}, counts
the raw strings before any normalization. These baselines make the whole pipeline easy to
check end to end. Exact-match grouping is \emph{not} semantic entropy in
Farquhar et al.'s sense~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}, because it
ignores paraphrases; it is therefore reported as a surface-form baseline, not
as semantic entropy.

\textbf{Replication condition.} The main semantic-entropy condition repeats
the same model--dataset grid on the NHR@FAU high-performance computing
cluster and replaces exact-match clustering with a bidirectional Natural
Language Inference (NLI) clustering step using DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoders
(the base model end to end, and the large model as the canonical
post-hoc backend).
This is the configuration that corresponds most closely to the method of
Farquhar et al.

\textbf{Additional analyses.} Separate sections report sensitivity checks with
larger NLI judges and a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct judge, a continuous kernel-based
variant called Kernel Language
Entropy~\cite{nikitin_kernel_language_entropy_2024}, Semantic
Entropy Probes evaluated on held-out questions (questions the probe was
not trained on), and a sampling-temperature sweep. These extend the
replication question rather than forming part of the baseline.

\section{Contributions}

The method itself comes from Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. The contribution of this thesis is
to re-implement it in a smaller, fully local setup and to use that setup to
ask a question the original aggregate evaluation does not isolate: how the
value of meaning-level clustering changes as outputs get longer, and how it
depends on decoding temperature. This thesis contributes an implementation,
an evaluation, and a set of scope choices that together isolate when
meaning-level clustering helps. Concretely, it delivers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item an implementation of discrete semantic entropy for short-answer and
    long-form hallucination detection, covering answer normalization,
    exact-match clustering, bidirectional NLI clustering with DeBERTa-v3
    cross-encoders,
    entropy scoring, and correctness labeling;
  \item an experimental design that separates surface-form baselines, the
    NLI-clustered replication condition, and additional analyses, with Slurm
    templates and reproducibility logging for each run;
  \item an auditable JSONL output schema that records, for every prompt, the
    sampled answers, the semantic clusters, the cluster representatives, the
    entropy scores, and the correctness labels;
  \item an evaluation framework for selective prediction using AUROC,
    rejection-accuracy curves, and their area summary (AURAC), which applies
    to any uncertainty estimator that writes a score to the shared schema;
  \item extension baselines and sensitivity checks covering Semantic Entropy
    Probes, Kernel Language Entropy, P(True), in- and out-of-distribution
    embedding regression, NLI judges of different sizes, and a
    sampling-temperature sweep that shows sampling-based entropy strengthening
    while the single-pass P(True) baseline remains comparatively flat;
  \item a controlled short-versus-long-form comparison that places the
    FactScore-style biography task next to the short-answer benchmarks,
    including a claim-level long-form extension, and shows the regime where
    meaning-aware methods separate clearly from count-based surface-form
    baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews the forms of LLM hallucination, the role
of uncertainty estimation, the notion of semantic equivalence, and related
semantic uncertainty methods. Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy} defines the
discrete semantic entropy method that the empirical work is based on,
together with the related estimators it is compared against: Kernel
Language Entropy, Semantic Entropy Probes, P(True), and embedding
regression. Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental_design} turns the method into a
reproducible experimental design and documents how it was implemented and
executed on the computing cluster.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the empirical results,
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses interpretation and limitations, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} states the conclusion.
