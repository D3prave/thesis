\chapter{Introduction}

Large language models can answer open-ended questions fluently even when the
answer is unsupported or wrong. This makes hallucination detection a practical
problem rather than only a benchmark problem: a useful system should not merely
produce an answer, but should also indicate when that answer is unreliable. The
central difficulty is that free-form language has many valid surface forms. Two
sampled answers may use different words while expressing the same meaning, and
two similar-looking answers may make incompatible claims.

This thesis studies semantic entropy as a detector for this kind of unreliability.
The core idea is to measure uncertainty over meanings rather than over strings.
If repeated samples from a model mostly express the same answer, the model may
be uncertain only about phrasing. If the samples split across different semantic
answers, the model is semantically uncertain and the answer should be treated as
riskier \cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023,semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\section{Research Aim}

The main research question is whether semantic entropy provides a better signal
for hallucination risk and selective abstention than surface-form uncertainty.
The first target is deliberately narrow: short-answer question answering where
sampled answers can be clustered by meaning and compared with reference answers.
The thesis therefore focuses on confabulation-like failures, where the model's
answer varies semantically across samples. It does not claim that semantic
entropy detects every hallucination, especially high-confidence systematic
errors \cite{kalai_why_language_models_hallucinate_2025,simhi_choke_2025}.

\section{Two-Phase Study}

The study is organized in two phases. Phase 1 is a constrained pilot that can be
debugged locally and on TinyGPU resources. It will use small local models first,
then 7B--8B instruct or chat models, and will evaluate discrete semantic entropy
on TriviaQA and SVAMP. The purpose of Phase 1 is to make the full pipeline
auditable: sample answers, normalize them, cluster them by bidirectional
entailment, compute uncertainty scores, attach correctness labels, and write
machine-readable records.

Phase 2 repeats the same measurement plan at larger scale on NHR@FAU resources.
Alex is planned for scaled inference, larger sweeps, and stronger semantic
clustering runs. Helma is reserved for 70B+ models and high-compute extensions
such as Semantic Entropy Probes, Kernel Language Entropy, Semantic Energy, and
Semantic Volume. The key constraint is continuity: Phase 2 should test whether
the Phase 1 finding survives stronger models and better judges, not replace the
research question with a different one.

\section{Contributions}

The intended contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a concise reproduction-oriented formulation of discrete semantic entropy
    for short-answer hallucination detection;
  \item a two-phase experimental design that separates local validation from
    high-compute HPC runs;
  \item an auditable output schema for sampled answers, semantic clusters,
    entropy scores, and correctness labels;
  \item a selective-prediction evaluation using AUROC, AURAC, and
    rejection-accuracy curves;
  \item a scoped plan for Phase 2 extensions once the discrete baseline is
    stable.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}

Chapter 2 reviews hallucination, uncertainty estimation, semantic equivalence,
and related semantic uncertainty methods. Chapter 3 defines the discrete
semantic entropy method used as the first baseline. Chapter 4 turns the method
into a two-phase experimental design. Chapter 5 describes the local
implementation boundary and the HPC execution plan. Later chapters will report
results, discuss Semantic Entropy Probes and other extensions, and summarize the
limitations of the approach.
