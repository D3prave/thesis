\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reimplemented semantic entropy following Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} and the released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}. Its main contribution is a
comparison that distinguishes the performance of the complete estimator from
the added value of grouping answers by meaning.

Semantic entropy provides useful detection in the main LLM-graded comparisons
and leads the measured original baselines on sentence answers. Its discrete
and likelihood-weighted forms perform similarly.

The results make normalized-string entropy an essential comparator: it captures
the most consistent improvement in the decomposition without entailment calls.
On the short-answer tasks under LLM grading, no additional benefit is detected
with the tested DeBERTa cross-encoders. The Qwen entailment model yields gains
under both judges, with stronger robustness evidence for sentence answers.
The smaller short-phrase ranges include zero under crossed resampling.

The separate biography evaluation shows where string counting loses its
usefulness: nearly every sampled paragraph is distinct. The two larger
entailment models produce informative scores in this setting and the largest
recorded gaps over the string baselines. The changed task and grading target
prevent attributing those gaps to length alone.

The comparison that carries information about semantic grouping is the one
against normalized-string counting, and its outcome depends on the entailment
model, the sampling temperature, and the correctness rule. Each of the three
changes the observed ranking inside the recorded grid, and the spread they
produce is larger than the spread between the estimators themselves. All three
were varied, and every result is reported with the setting it came from. The
results do not identify a best setting, because that would need a reference
that was not available: correctness and equivalence labels checked by people rather than
by models, on a grid wider than three datasets and three generators.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

\paragraph{Correctness labels and shared errors.}
Every accuracy and AUROC estimate depends on token overlap or LLM judgments
without human validation. Agreement between judges establishes consistency
only. Both entailment and LLM grading compare meanings, so shared preferences
or errors could favor semantic entropy. Re-grading preserves the main
entailment-model ranking, reducing concern about one model serving both roles,
but shared errors across models remain possible.

\paragraph{Statistical units and generalization.}
Seeds repeat runs within dataset--generator pairs. The hierarchical analysis
resamples these pairs and their records, and the additional checks in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} address shared datasets, generators, and questions.
Three datasets and three generators still limit generalization, and repeated
seeds or more records do not widen that range. An interval that includes zero
does not establish equivalence or rule out a small benefit. The biography
result is the most restricted: it uses one dataset and one Qwen judge, and its
intervals resample records while holding generators and seeds fixed.

\paragraph{Input and protocol differences.}
Surface entropy counts normalized answers, whereas semantic clustering receives
raw answers. The fixed-subset check in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} finds many
partition changes after normalization despite small mean AUROC changes.
The sentence condition produces longer answers than the original study, and
graded answers and uncertainty samples use different token limits. The
biography protocol uses one dataset and one Qwen judge, also used for entailment
in one setting. Its different task, prompts, and reference-consistency target
prevent a pure length comparison.

\paragraph{Benchmark and computational scope.}
Probe-training holdouts may overlap with pretraining data, and historical
references may omit later changes. End-to-end computational cost was not
benchmarked. String counting needs no entailment calls, while semantic grouping
adds pair comparisons to repeated generation, so the results do not quantify
an accuracy--runtime tradeoff.

\paragraph{Order-dependent clustering.}
Changing answer order can change the groups with entailment judgments fixed.
The permutation check in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} covers a fixed subset
with DeBERTa-v3-large. Its observed ranges do not bound all orders or establish
the effect for other entailment models.
