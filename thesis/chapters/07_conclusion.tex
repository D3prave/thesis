\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}

This thesis asked whether semantic entropy provides a better
hallucination-risk and abstention signal than surface-form uncertainty,
and how that answer depends on the surface-form variability of the
task, the clustering backend, and the scale of the generator. The
method follows Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024};
the contribution here is a reproducible reimplementation evaluated on
a full cross-seed grid (three open generators, three short-answer
datasets and a long-form biography task, three decoding seeds under
the original paper's sampling and labeling protocol) plus a
claim-level long-form extension, rather than a new semantic-entropy
method.

The answer depends on the length of the output, and it points in opposite
directions at the two ends.
On short answers the sampling-based detectors all work: AUROC lands roughly
between $0.70$ and $0.90$. But the cheapest score in the family wins.
Entropy over normalized strings is the strongest of the three count-based
scores in eight of the nine model--dataset cells, and the strongest of all
detectors in six. No semantic estimator beats it by a margin that matters:
every mean difference for discrete semantic entropy is negative, and the one
Holm-significant result favors the surface baseline. The single exception
anywhere is a $0.002$ lead for the probability-weighted variant on SVAMP
with Llama-3.1-70B, which is noise. A record-level
analysis explains why: dataset normalization already removes almost all
surface variation before any semantic step runs, so clustering has little
left to merge. On long-form text the two count-based string-frequency scores
have little to measure: paragraphs almost never repeat as identical strings,
so the scores are nearly pinned at their ceiling and sit near chance.
Surface-only probability-weighted naive entropy remains strong, while
meaning-level clustering and a correctness-trained probe recover signal by
different routes.

Scored one claim at a time against human factuality labels, semantic entropy
detects confabulated claims at AUROC 0.748, 0.724 and 0.701 for the three
generators. Here it does beat the normalized surface baseline, by $0.07$ to
$0.11$ AUROC in every cell. What makes that comparison worth more than the
whole-paragraph one is the baseline: at claim level it still ranks usefully,
at $0.642 / 0.650 / 0.619$, rather than sitting at its ceiling. So the
meaning-clustering step is worth its cost at claim granularity, and is not on
short answers. On whole paragraphs the picture is weaker and, on the evidence
of two independent diagnostics, partly an artifact of granularity rather
than of the method: the score is pinned at its ceiling for a large minority
of records, and one generator's paragraphs are frequently cut off at the
decoding limit.

Two further findings qualify the method's practical use.

The first is that the clustering backend decides the long-form result. Across
NLI cross-encoders and an LLM judge, the same saved samples yield anything
from anti-predictive to moderately useful behavior. On short answers the
same four backends span at most 0.057 AUROC. Backend capacity seems to matter
in proportion to the length of the text being compared. That makes it an
implementation detail on short answers and a first-order design decision on
long-form text.

The second is a scale effect. Claim-level detection falls steadily as the
generator grows, from AUROC $0.748$ through $0.724$ to $0.701$ between 7B and
70B. A larger model that has invented a detail may simply repeat it more
consistently, leaving less disagreement to measure; the experiment does not
establish that mechanism directly. The effect is a gentle slope rather than
a collapse: $0.047$ AUROC across a tenfold size difference, with the largest
model still clearly useful. It should not be extrapolated beyond the three
models measured here.


Hidden-state probes offer a cheaper, single-pass alternative. On short
answers the entropy-trained SEP and correctness-trained accuracy probe land
close together, with the correctness target ahead in eight of nine held-out
cells. The whole-paragraph biography design reports only the accuracy probe,
at $0.704 / 0.680 / 0.773$ from 7B to 70B; it defines neither a genuine SEP nor
an OOD transfer result. Its scale pattern is not monotonic, so those three
cells give no evidence that hidden-state detection strengthens with model
size. The measured conclusion is narrower: the correctness-trained probe remains a
useful single-pass comparator when whole-paragraph cluster scores saturate.

\section{Contributions}

The substantive method comes from Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. The contributions promised in
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} were delivered as six artifacts:
(1)~a reproducible \texttt{semantic\_entropy} implementation
(Chapters~\ref{ch:semantic_entropy}--\ref{ch:experimental_design});
(2)~the complete cross-seed experiment record, with paired-bootstrap
significance kept separate from seed stability
(Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}--\ref{sec:hpc-execution});
(3)~the extension baselines, including P(True), in- and out-of-distribution
embedding regression, Kernel Language Entropy under two encoders, and the
Semantic Entropy Probe~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024}, with the
observation that correctness-trained and SE-trained probes perform similarly
in this grid, although the correctness-trained probe is ahead in eight of
nine cells (Sections~\ref{sec:related-estimators}, \ref{sec:probes-results});
(4)~the two-granularity long-form evaluation, whole-paragraph and claim-level,
which yields the scale trend, the clustering-artifact result, and evidence
from this biography task that a consistency-based detector should be
evaluated at a granularity where independent samples can plausibly agree, a
condition that can be checked from the samples before any AUROC is computed
(Sections~\ref{sec:bio-results}, \ref{sec:factualbio-results});
(5)~the record-level worked audits that give mechanistic rather than purely
metric-level evidence for the main conclusions
(Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits}); and (6)~the sampling-temperature sweep, which
isolates the steep temperature dependence of the sampling-based family
against a near-flat single-pass baseline and shows the resulting crossing to
depend on model scale (Section~\ref{sec:temperature-sensitivity}).


The full source code, result tables, Slurm templates, and LaTeX thesis
source are available at \url{https://github.com/D3prave/thesis}.
The Python implementation lives in the \texttt{src/semantic\_entropy/}
package; entry points are documented in \texttt{README.md}.

\section{Limitations}

The detection target is the confabulation setting of
Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, so high-confidence systematic errors fall outside
it by construction. The FactualBio scale trend makes that limit concrete:
consistent confabulation at 70B is precisely the confident-error
case~\cite{simhi_choke_2025}.

Within the empirical results, the main residual limitations are the
automated LLM judging behind the whole-paragraph long-form labels (the
claim-level evaluation uses published human labels), the three-seed basis of
the stability estimates, and the English-only, biography-only long-form
scope. The hidden-state probes were not included in the temperature sweep, the
granularity interpretation comes from one long-form task, and AUROC ranking
alone does not establish deployment utility. The claim-level
surface comparison is exploratory rather than preregistered, and rests on one
claim set and three generators.
Record-level issues (alias-type oracle failures on TriviaQA,
semantically consistent refusals on biography, and SVAMP's
integer-arithmetic structure) are documented in the audits; the
fuller discussion is in Section~\ref{sec:discussion-limitations}.
