\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reimplemented the semantic entropy method of Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} against their released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}, ran it on three open
generators and three short-answer benchmarks plus a long-form biography task,
and used that setup to ask three questions.

\textbf{Does semantic entropy detect confabulations better than the baselines
the original paper uses?} Yes. On sentence-length answers under the canonical
entailment model and an LLM judge, likelihood-weighted semantic entropy reaches
AUROC 0.758 and the discrete variant 0.757, against 0.718 for a
correctness-trained hidden-state probe, 0.714 for P(True), 0.694 for entropy
over sequence log-likelihoods and 0.672 for the semantic entropy probe. The
ordering holds in every slice where each comparator was measured, and the
headline comparison against naive predictive entropy reproduces at $+0.063$ to
$+0.162$ against the $+0.099$ the original's own reported figures imply.

\textbf{Which part of the method earns the advantage?} Normalization, not
grouping by meaning. Every baseline the original uses differs from semantic
entropy in more than one way at once, so that design cannot attribute the
margin to any single part. Breaking the method into four steps, each differing
from the one below it in exactly one way, does attribute it. Of the four, only
normalizing the answer strings before counting them is clearly worth anything:
$+0.040$ AUROC, positive and resolved in all 24 slices of the grid, and the
only step above the seed noise floor. Counting raw strings rather than reading
likelihoods is positive in 4 slices of 24. Weighting the meaning-groups by
likelihood adds $+0.002$.

The step the method is named after is not beaten by its own comparator. Eight
slices spanning four prompting conditions and two independent LLM judges give
margins between $-0.0020$ and $+0.0223$, and a hierarchical paired bootstrap
resampling both the nine independent units and the records within them resolves
none of them. Pairing on records was the analysis that could have rescued a
small consistent effect; it narrows the intervals threefold and changes no
verdict. The same conclusion follows without any test: the clustering-free
comparator passes every comparison the original study runs, with no slice
anywhere going the other way.

That answer is conditional in two specific ways, and neither is a hedge.
Substituting a 72-billion-parameter model as the entailment judge, on identical
generations, gives a resolved margin of $+0.087$ on sentence-length answers and
$+0.012$ to $+0.014$ on short phrases. And on paragraph-length output every
sampled answer is unique, so the string estimators are constant across records
and sit at chance. There, grouping is not an improvement over them but a
precondition for measuring anything at all. The scale of the design bounds the
null from the other side as well: an effect below roughly 0.01 AUROC would not
be visible here, so what is established is the absence of a measurable benefit
rather than of any benefit.

\textbf{How much do measurement choices move the answer?} More than the choice
of method does. On one fixed set of generations the entailment model moves
discrete semantic entropy from 0.751 to 0.823, a spread three times the 0.020
margin between semantic and surface entropy on the canonical backend. Sampling
temperature reorders the families of detector, with diversity-based estimators
falling from 0.757 to 0.568 between $T=1.0$ and $T=0.1$ while a
likelihood-based one moves only from 0.694 to 0.644. The conventional $T=1.0$
is the most favorable point in the measured range for the sampling-based
approach. The correctness rule reverses the sign of the headline
contrast, from $+0.020$ under LLM grading to $-0.051$ under the token-overlap
rule the released code applies by default. And the choice between AUROC and the
rejection-accuracy summary reported beside it moves the verdict from a bare
majority for semantic entropy to a majority for surface entropy.

Taken together: the method is sound and it reproduces, but the number attached
to it measures the setup as much as the method, and the part of the method that
the margin is usually credited to is not the part that produces it.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A four-step decomposition of the method}, in which neighbouring
    steps differ in exactly one way, so the value of each is measured on its
    own. It locates the reliable gain in answer normalization rather than in
    grouping by meaning, and it supplies the comparator the original evaluation
    leaves out.
  \item \textbf{The finding that the clustering-free comparator passes every
    comparison the original study runs}. That is 24 slices of 24 against entropy
    over log-likelihoods, 6 of 6 against P(True) and against each probe, with none
    going the other way. That evidence base therefore does not separate
    semantic entropy from a method that never judges meaning.
  \item \textbf{A hierarchical paired bootstrap over 24 slices}, with the
    demonstration that the natural analysis, resampling records with the
    task units held fixed, gives intervals three times too narrow and flips
    the direction of the conclusion. The grid holds nine independent units, not
    twenty-seven cells, and treating the cells as independent inflates the
    count of significant contrasts by 44\,\%.
  \item \textbf{The entailment model varied afterwards on identical
    generations}, showing that choice is worth more than the choice between
    methods, and that three implementations of ``bidirectional entailment
    clustering'' agree on cluster count for only 60--73\,\% of records.
  \item \textbf{A $2\times2$ separating instruction wording from worked-example
    count}, which the original's two conditions confound, showing that the
    worked examples rather than the instruction text are what matters.
  \item \textbf{Three correctness rules applied to identical answers}, two of
    them independent LLM judges, and the finding that the ranking of methods
    depends on which is used.
  \item \textbf{A five-point temperature sweep}, showing that diversity-based
    and likelihood-based detectors part ways as temperature falls, and that the
    conventional setting is the most favorable one for the sampling-based
    family.
  \item \textbf{Two findings about the metrics rather than the methods}: the
    two rejection-accuracy definitions rank the methods differently from AUROC
    and from each other, and both track the generating model's own accuracy at
    $r \approx 0.97$ against $r = 0.50$ for AUROC, so neither isolates the
    detector.
  \item \textbf{An implementation faithful to the released code rather than to
    its description}, including the order-dependent anchor scan the code
    performs, with a register of the nine points where code and paper disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

Two limitations are not qualifications on individual tables. They condition
every number here, and they are stated in full because a reader who took the
conclusions above without them would be taking more than the data support.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} states both where the results are interpreted; they
are repeated because they belong to the conclusions as much as to the analysis.

\paragraph{The correctness judges were never validated against human labels.}
Every AUROC here is computed against a correctness label, and every label came
either from a token-overlap rule or from a language model asked to grade. The
two LLM judges agree on 93.7\,\% of records in the sentence-length condition,
and that figure is easy to over-read: it bounds how consistent the judges are
with each other, not how often they are both wrong. Two models of similar
construction, given the same prompt, can agree closely and be wrong in the same
direction. Farquhar et al.\ run exactly this check against human annotation on
100 questions in their Supplementary Note~6. This study runs no equivalent, so
every accuracy figure, and therefore every AUROC, rests on an unvalidated
judge.

\paragraph{The entailment model and the grader answer the same question.} Both
are asked, in effect, whether two texts mean the same thing, and both are
models of the same kind. A method that groups by meaning is therefore favored
exactly when correctness is scored by meaning, for reasons unrelated to whether
it detects confabulation. The data carry the fingerprint: semantic entropy's
margin over surface entropy is $+0.020$ under LLM grading and $-0.051$ under
token-F1 in the same condition, and the 72B entailment backend gains $+0.065$
under LLM grading while losing $0.015$ under token-F1. Each estimator does best
where its notion of sameness matches the grader's.

Without human labels this cannot be settled, and the thesis does not settle it.
In particular it does not treat the LLM-graded numbers as the true ones and the
token-F1 numbers as an artifact. Surface entropy resolvably beats semantic
entropy under token-F1 in seven slices, and under the LLM judges the two are
indistinguishable on the canonical backend. So the judges do not vindicate
semantic entropy, they fail to separate the methods. What can be said is that
the ranking depends on which definition of correctness is adopted, and that
this study has no way to choose between them. The narrower circularity check of
Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity} does not cover this: it rules out one
particular model favoring its own output, and finds that it does not, but the
concern is the class of judge rather than the identity of the model.

\paragraph{Further limitations.} Six more qualify particular results and are
set out in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. The sentence-length condition here is not
the original's, averaging 255.2 characters against 95.6. Nine independent units
cannot resolve effects below roughly 0.01 AUROC. The clustering step is
order-dependent by construction. The long-form arm has degenerate string
baselines, one grader that is also one of its backends, and a stricter
clustering rule than the original uses for paragraphs. The probes and P(True)
sit on different backends and are never directly comparable. And the two added
prompting conditions exist on one backend, so the $2\times2$ cannot be
estimated on the backend most favorable to semantic entropy.
