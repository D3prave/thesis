\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reimplemented semantic entropy using the method of Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} and its released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}. It compared the method with
the original baselines and with surface entropy, a simpler method that counts
normalized answer strings. The main evaluation uses short-answer benchmarks
and open generating models.

Semantic entropy performs well against the original baselines. However, those
comparisons alone do not show that grouping answers by meaning causes the
improvement. Normalizing answer strings gives the most consistent improvement
in the tested decomposition. With the released entailment models, grouping by
meaning does not show a clear further benefit over surface entropy under LLM
grading. This does not establish that the methods are equivalent or that a
small benefit is absent.

Changing the entailment model changes the conclusion. With the tested Qwen
entailment model, semantic entropy improves on surface entropy under both LLM
judges. Sampling temperature also changes which detector leads, and the
correctness rule can reverse the semantic-versus-surface comparison. The
method's reported performance therefore needs to be read together with its
entailment model, sampling settings, and grading rule.

The main conclusion is not that semantic grouping is unnecessary. It is that
its added value must be tested against normalized-string counting and cannot
be assumed from a comparison with weaker baselines.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A comparison with the original baselines.}
    The study tests the published method on open generating models and
    separates findings that reproduce from differences caused by the tested
    setting.
  \item \textbf{A comparison of normalization and meaning-based grouping.}
    The added surface-entropy baseline separates the consistent benefit of
    string normalization from the more conditional benefit of semantic
    grouping.
  \item \textbf{A test of the main evaluation choices.}
    The study varies the entailment model, correctness rule, and sampling
    temperature, showing where the method ranking depends on those choices.
  \item \textbf{A documented implementation and uncertainty analysis.}
    The repository records implementation choices and deviations from the
    published description. The paired analysis accounts for variation between
    dataset--model units as well as between question records, and states the
    limits of the available evidence.
\end{enumerate}

The prompting-factorial and rejection-accuracy comparisons are secondary
analyses. The biography experiment is exploratory because of its incomplete
provenance and different clustering rule. Their detailed results remain in
the appendices and do not support the main contributions.

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

The most important limitation is the absence of human correctness labels.
Token overlap and LLM judgments are the only grading rules used here. The
LLM judges agree closely, but they may share mistakes. Using a different model
for grading reduces concern about one model serving in both roles; it does
not establish that the grades are correct or remove possible shared biases
between grading and semantic clustering.

The number of independent dataset--model units is also limited. Repeated
seeds and more question records do not provide the same evidence as testing
more datasets and generating models. The study can therefore report that it
did not detect a benefit in some conditions, but cannot rule out small
improvements or generalize to all language-model tasks.

Further limits include different answer lengths from the original study,
different token limits for uncertainty samples and graded answers,
order-dependent clustering, and incomplete coverage of detector and
entailment-model combinations. The full discussion is in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

The clearest next step is to obtain human correctness labels for a sample of
the existing answers and repeat the main semantic-versus-surface comparison
against those labels. Broader dataset--model coverage would then help determine
whether the conditional benefit found here holds beyond this evaluation.
