\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis reimplemented semantic entropy using the method of Farquhar et
al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} and its released
code~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}. It compared the method with
Farquhar et al.'s baselines and with surface entropy, a simpler method that counts
normalized answer strings. The evaluation covers short-answer benchmarks and a
long-form biography task using open generating models.

The main question was how well semantic entropy detects incorrect answers,
whether it performs better than other estimators, and when it has an
advantage. In the main LLM-graded comparisons it ranks incorrect answers above
correct ones better than chance. On sentence-length answers, its discrete and
likelihood-weighted forms perform similarly and lead the measured Farquhar
baselines. Their advantage over naive predictive entropy is larger for
sentence-length answers than for short phrases, where several leading
methods are nearly tied.

The recorded long-form biography arm shows a stronger separation from string
baselines: semantic entropy reaches 0.670 with large DeBERTa and 0.708 with
Qwen, while surface and naive sample entropy are near 0.497. This extends the
comparison to paragraph answers. Because it is a separate paragraph-level arm,
it supports a long-form setting claim rather than a causal claim about length
alone.

Changing the entailment model changes the conclusion. With the tested Qwen
entailment model, semantic entropy improves on surface entropy under both LLM
judges. Sampling temperature also changes which detector leads, and the
correctness rule can reverse the semantic-versus-surface comparison. The
method's reported performance therefore needs to be read together with its
entailment model, sampling settings, and grading rule.

The decomposition provides a supporting explanation. Normalizing answer
strings gives the most consistent improvement across the four method steps.
With the released entailment models, semantic grouping adds no detectable
benefit over surface entropy under LLM grading. This does not establish
equivalence or rule out a small benefit.

Semantic entropy is a useful detector in this evaluation, but it does not
consistently outperform every comparator. The strongest conclusion concerns
the conditions in which it helps: sentence-length answers relative to naive
entropy, and the tested Qwen entailment model relative to normalized-string
counting under LLM grading.

The study contributes a comparison with Farquhar et al.'s baselines, the
normalized-string comparator, tests of evaluation choices, and a documented
implementation. The grouping comparison remains subject to the raw-versus-normalized
input difference described in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

The most important limitation is the absence of human correctness labels.
Token overlap and LLM judgments are the only grading rules used here. The
LLM judges agree closely, but they may share mistakes. Using a different model
for grading reduces concern about one model serving in both roles; it does not
show that the grades are correct, and it does not remove possible shared
biases between grading and semantic clustering.

The dataset--model grid is also limited and shares datasets and generators across
its resampling units. Repeated
seeds and more question records do not provide the same evidence as testing
more datasets and generating models. The study can therefore report that it
did not detect a benefit in some conditions, but cannot rule out small
improvements or generalize to all language-model tasks.

Further limits include different answer lengths from the original study,
different token limits for uncertainty samples and graded answers,
and order-dependent clustering. The full discussion is in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

The clearest next step is to obtain human correctness labels for a sample of
the existing answers and repeat the comparison of uncertainty estimators
against those labels. Broader dataset--model coverage would then help determine
whether the conditional benefit found here holds beyond this evaluation.
