\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rather than
restating the tables. It works through five questions. Why does meaning-based
clustering add almost nothing on short answers, yet recover a signal on
long-form text? What does the backend comparison say about judge quality? Why
does model scale erode the signal? How do the sampling methods compare with
P(True) as decoding temperature changes? And how does all of this relate to
the original semantic entropy paper~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}? The
chapter closes with the limitations the numbers do not show on their own.

\section{The two-regime picture}
\label{sec:two-regimes}

The most consequential pattern in the study is the split between the
short-answer and long-form regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:regime-split}). On
the three short-answer tasks the core count-based sampling detectors generally
reach AUROC values of roughly $0.70$--$0.90$, and the cheapest of them, entropy over
normalized answer strings, is the strongest single score in most
cells, with no method beating it by a margin of practical size anywhere
(Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface}). On long-form
biography the two count-based string-frequency scores sit near chance because
they are almost pinned at their ceiling. Meaning-level grouping recovers a
signal (discrete semantic entropy: $0.734 / 0.590 / 0.648$ for Mistral-7B /
Llama-3.1-8B / Llama-3.1-70B), as does the correctness-trained accuracy probe
($0.704 / 0.680 / 0.773$). A genuine SEP is not defined for this answer-only
whole-paragraph protocol. The surface-only probability-weighted
naive entropy also remains strong, at $0.769 / 0.769 / 0.757$, and is best for
the two smaller generators.

The mechanism behind both halves is visible in the worked examples of
Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits}. On short answers (Example~1,
Section~\ref{sec:audit-merge}) the model emits only a handful of distinct
normalized strings, so simply \emph{normalizing} the answers already does
most of the grouping; the extra meaning-based step only relabels a few
boundary cases. On biographies (Example~3, Section~\ref{sec:audit-bio}),
sampled paragraphs almost never repeat as identical strings, so the
count-based surface score is nearly pinned at its maximum of $\ln 10$ and has
little ranking information. Probability weighting and meaning-level grouping
provide two different ways to recover signal. The biography task was added
precisely to test this prediction, and it confirms the count-saturation
mechanism.

\section{Does meaning-based clustering help?}
\label{sec:does-nli-help}

At the level of the full grid the answer is now clear in both directions. On
short answers, no --- and without exception. Every one of the nine cells has
a negative mean difference, so not a single cell favors meaning-based
clustering even before significance is considered, and the one
Holm-significant result runs the other way, with surface entropy ahead on
NQ-Open with Llama-3.1-8B (Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface}). The
closest any cell comes to favoring the semantic score is SVAMP with the 70B
model, where two of three seeds are positive by $0.001$ but the mean is
$-0.002$ with a Holm-adjusted $p$ of $1.0$: noise, not a narrow win. On
long-form generation, yes: the NLI-clustered and probability-weighted
entropy estimators beat the chance-level surface baseline by $+0.08$ to
$+0.28$ AUROC on biography, while KLE, meaning-aware only in a soft,
embedding-based sense, does not (Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}); at claim
level, against human labels, semantic entropy detects at $0.701$ to $0.748$
without relying on whole-paragraph string identity.

In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:research-aim}, H1 is not supported on these
datasets. The direction is in fact uniform in the opposite sense, all nine
cells favoring the surface baseline, which leaves H2 --- a claim about the
consistency of an improvement --- with no improvement to describe. H2 is
therefore not assessable as stated, rather than confirmed or refuted. What can
be said is that the \emph{absence} of an improvement is itself consistent
across all three short-answer benchmarks. H3 is
supported, and by two measurements rather than one. On whole paragraphs the
count-based surface scores sit at chance while every meaning-aware or
probability-weighted estimator is clearly above them; that comparison is a
diagnostic rather than a detector benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}), because a baseline pinned at its ceiling is
easy to beat. The claim-level evaluation, which carries the long-form
conclusions of this thesis, settles the question on its own terms: against
human labels, and against a surface baseline that still works
($0.642 / 0.650 / 0.619$), meaning-based clustering leads in every cell by
$0.07$ to $0.11$ AUROC (Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}). That is the
comparison H3 asks for, made where the thesis says its long-form evidence
lies.

The short-answer non-result deserves a plain statement, because the
theoretical motivation for semantic entropy is that surface variation is
noise to be removed. On these datasets that motivation is mostly
already satisfied by normalization. SVAMP is the extreme case: integer
answers normalize to identical digit strings, so meaning-based and
exact-match clustering produce nearly the same groups and nearly the same
scores. TriviaQA leaves a little more room (Example~1 shows NLI
merging paraphrases such as ``Triumph and Disaster'' with
``Triumph and defeat''), but the records where this changes the ranking
are a minority, and the net effect is not large enough to separate from
run-to-run noise at $N=500$. Appendix~\ref{app:gallery} quantifies this
record by record: NLI merges help on some model cells and hurt on
others, with no consistent net gain, and the appendix's
false-confidence example shows the failure mode: a consistently
wrong answer whose ten paraphrases NLI correctly merges, converting a
correctly flagged record into a maximally confident error. What these
numbers support is a small positive
tendency on TriviaQA and essentially nothing on SVAMP, which matches the
modest size of the improvement reported in the original
work~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\section{Sensitivity to the clustering backend}
\label{sec:nli-sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:clustering-sensitivity} established that the
equivalence backend barely matters on short answers but is
\emph{result-determining} on biography
(Figure~\ref{fig:bio-clustering-sensitivity}), where the Qwen-72B judge
($0.755 / 0.678 / 0.695$) clusters meanings more reliably than the
DeBERTa cross-encoders and lifts AUROC accordingly.

Two cautions follow. First, ``bigger judge'' is not automatically
``better judge'': the relationship between backend capacity and which
generator it helps is not monotone, and the failure mode is not a graceful
one --- as Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity} shows, a weak backend can
drive the score below chance rather than merely blunting it.
Second, this is the long-form counterpart of the short-answer negative
result: clustering quality matters exactly where clustering matters at all.
The two ends of the study bound the effect. On short answers, where the
strings being compared are a few words long, the four backends span at most
$0.057$ AUROC and plain exact matching is best in seven of nine cells. On whole
paragraphs the same swap decides whether a signal exists. The claim-level
evaluation sits at the short-string end of that range by construction, since
it re-samples brief answers to specific questions, which is one reason it is
the more trustworthy long-form measurement. Backend capacity appears to
matter in proportion to the length of the text being compared, and promoting
clustering choice from an implementation detail to a first-order design
decision on everything long-form is one of the study's main methodological
points.

\section{Hidden-state probes as cheap single-pass alternatives}
\label{sec:sep-scaling-discussion}

The single-pass hidden-state probe (SEP) and correctness-trained accuracy
probe read the model's internal state once, without drawing ten samples. On
short answers this thrift can cost accuracy. The probes are trained on a
disjoint set of prompts and scored on the same evaluation records as the
entropy tables earlier in this chapter, so the two are directly comparable:
SEP lands between
$0.726$ and $0.900$ across the nine cells
(Table~\ref{tab:probes}) against a surface baseline running
from $0.755$ to $0.901$. The held-out comparisons do not show a uniform loss,
so the relevant trade-off is the probe's $M$-fold test-time cost saving against
its cell-specific ranking quality. A
secondary observation is that the two probe targets land close together in
every cell (Section~\ref{sec:probes-results}), which suggests that a single
shared signal is what a linear reader of one hidden state can extract, and that
its level is the practical ceiling for this probe family.

The whole-paragraph biography evidence is narrower because the sealed design
defines only the correctness-trained accuracy probe, not a genuine SEP. Its
AUROC is $0.704 / 0.680 / 0.773$ from 7B to 70B. It exceeds all reported
meaning-based entropy scores on the two Llama generators, but not on
Mistral-7B; probability-weighted naive entropy remains higher on the two
smaller models, while \mbox{P(True)} is highest on the 70B model at $0.835$.
The sequence is not monotonic in model size, so these three cells give no
evidence that hidden-state probing strengthens as the generator grows. What
they do support is more limited: a
correctness-trained single-pass probe can remain useful when repeated
whole-paragraph samples are too diverse for hard clustering to rank reliably.

\section{Temperature decides which detector wins}
\label{sec:temperature-discussion}

The temperature sweep (Section~\ref{sec:temperature-sensitivity},
Figure~\ref{fig:temp-sweep}) gives the P(True)-versus-entropy comparison
a practical form. Sampling-based entropy depends on the samples
disagreeing, so it is weak when decoding is near-greedy and strengthens
as temperature rises (TriviaQA/70B semantic entropy climbs from $0.52$
at $T{=}0.1$ to $0.75$ at $T{=}1.0$). The single-pass baseline does not
move with it: the information it reads is present regardless of how
diverse the samples are, and \mbox{P(True)} shifts by only $+0.031$ on
average across the sweep. The gap therefore closes in every short-answer
cell and reverses in five of the nine, on Llama-3.1-8B in all three tasks
and on Mistral-7B in two; on the third, SVAMP, semantic entropy already
leads at the lowest temperature, so at $T{=}1.0$ it is ahead in every cell
of both smaller generators. On Llama-3.1-70B it does not overtake at any
temperature measured. The practical consequence is concrete. Under the
near-greedy decoding common in production, the single-pass score is both the
cheaper and the stronger choice in eight of the nine cells. A sampling-based
estimator only earns its cost once the decoder is warm enough for the samples
to genuinely disagree, and how warm that is rises with the strength of the
generator --- beyond $T{=}1.0$ for the largest model here.

Three qualifications apply. The temperature gain is shared by the surface
baseline, so it reflects a general need for sampling diversity rather than
anything specific to semantic clustering. P(True) barely moves with
temperature at all. And the sweep covers the short-answer tasks only, because
biography was generated at the canonical temperature alone, so this
trade-off is established on short answers and not on long-form text.
One concern that would have qualified the Mistral-7B curve does not apply. An
answer-extraction artifact can empty a Mistral sample and so add a spurious
cluster, and that artifact might plausibly grow with temperature. It was
measured directly in every instance of the sweep, and stays at or below one
sample in five thousand throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:budgets-and-exclusions}).

\section{Model scale erodes the signal}
\label{sec:scale-factualbio}

The FactualBio claim-level extension
(Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}) gives the clearest scale result in
the study, and the only long-form one measured against human labels:
claim-level semantic entropy weakens monotonically as the generator grows,
from AUROC $0.748$ (Mistral-7B) through $0.724$ (Llama-3.1-8B) to $0.701$
(Llama-3.1-70B), with seed standard deviations of $0.017$ or less. The
whole-paragraph biography numbers do not follow the same monotonic order:
$0.734 / 0.590 / 0.648$ in Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}.

The reason follows from what semantic entropy actually measures:
\emph{inconsistency}. It flags an answer when regenerated samples disagree
in meaning. A possible explanation is that a larger model which has
confabulated a biographical detail may regenerate the \emph{same} wrong detail
fluently and consistently, leaving less disagreement to detect; the experiment
does not measure that mechanism directly. The size of the effect
deserves emphasis, because it is easy to overstate: across a tenfold
difference in parameter count the loss is $0.047$ AUROC, and the largest
model is still a useful detector at $0.701$. The trend is gradual rather than
a collapse. It is also not a uniform decay of the method with scale --- the
whole-paragraph probe moves the other way --- but a specific erosion of the
regime where detection depends on the model re-rolling its errors
differently each time. Extrapolating the slope beyond the three models
measured here would not be warranted, but the direction is a caution worth
recording: the detectors that look best on small models may be the ones that
fade first as models improve.

\section{Comparison with Farquhar et al.}
\label{sec:comparison-farquhar}

Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} report an average
AUROC of $0.790$ for semantic entropy across thirty task--model
combinations, against $0.691$ for naive entropy and $0.698$ for P(True).
The grid here was deliberately moved toward their protocol: matched
sampling ($M=10$, $T=1.0$, top-$p=0.9$, top-$k=50$, with a low-temperature
most-likely answer for accuracy labeling via SQuAD token-F1), three
shared datasets (TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP), and both probability-weighted
estimators computed alongside the discrete approximation. The remaining
differences are deliberate and disclosed: a narrower set of generators,
mostly newer, with Mistral 7B Instruct the one model family common to both;
entailment judged throughout with local NLI cross-encoders, which is one of
the two options the original uses, plus an open Qwen judge in place of its
GPT-3.5 alternative; and 500-record evaluation sets.

Under these settings the method itself reproduces closely, while the
comparison it was originally judged by does not.
Table~\ref{tab:farquhar-comparison} puts the two aggregates side by side.
Semantic entropy lands at $0.805$ here against the reported $0.790$, a
difference of $0.015$ across a different set of generators and a third as many
task--model cells; on the method's own terms this is a successful
reproduction. What does not carry over is the gap to the baselines.
\mbox{P(True)} reaches $0.771$ here rather than $0.698$, and the
probability-weighted naive estimator $0.806$ rather than $0.691$ --- so the
baseline that the original work reports semantic entropy as beating by about
ten AUROC points is, in this setup, level with it. Adding the normalized
surface baseline, which the original does not report at all, puts a
non-semantic score in front at $0.817$.

The divergence is therefore in the baselines rather than in the method, and
that is the more informative half of the comparison: it locates the
disagreement in what semantic entropy is being measured \emph{against}. One
claim of the original work does not reproduce as stated --- that semantic
entropy beats the naive baselines. Count-based surface entropy, which the
paper does not report as a separate baseline, is the strongest single
short-answer score in most cells, and no semantic estimator beats it by more
than a practically negligible margin (Section~\ref{sec:significance}).
Cell by cell, semantic entropy is above the raw-string naive estimator in six
of the nine cells, so the agreement is at the level of the aggregate rather
than a cell-for-cell match.
Semantic clustering provides measurable value only in the long-form regime, where
the paper's qualitative claim is confirmed for the count-based scores: they
collapse to chance while NLI-clustered entropy recovers a clearly
above-chance signal for every generator. That long-form comparison is
descriptive rather than tested, since the preregistered family covers the
short-answer cells alone
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}). The grid also extends the original analysis in a
direction it did not quantify: the entailment backend, which is
immaterial on short answers, is result-determining on whole paragraphs
(Section~\ref{sec:nli-sensitivity}).

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

\paragraph{Confabulation, not all hallucination.} Semantic entropy
targets the confabulation case defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: it
detects answers the model is internally inconsistent about. It is blind
to \emph{confident systematic errors}, where the model is wrong the same
way every time. Example~2 (Section~\ref{sec:audit-artifact}) is exactly
this case: ten identical wrong answers, entropy zero, maximum
(mis)confidence. A deployed system would need a complementary detector
for that failure mode.

\paragraph{Noisy correctness labels.} On TriviaQA and NQ-Open, correctness is
decided by SQuAD token-F1 against a reference list; SVAMP uses normalized
numeric exact match. These automatic rules can mark a
correct answer wrong whenever the model uses an unlisted alias, stage
name, or title; see again Example~2, where ``Stansfield Turner'' is graded
against a gold list of ``George Bush''. This adds noise to every
short-answer AUROC and is a lower bound on the gap to studies that use an
LLM-based correctness judge throughout. The numeric SVAMP task is largely
immune, since integer answers normalize deterministically.

\paragraph{Automated judging on whole paragraphs.} The whole-paragraph
biography labels come from an open LLM judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) against
pinned Wikipedia references, not human annotators. This scales to every
model--seed cell but inherits the judge's error modes, and a systematic
judge bias would propagate into every whole-paragraph AUROC. The claim-level
evaluation does not share this weakness: it uses the published human
factuality labels (Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}), which is a second
reason the long-form conclusions of this thesis are drawn from it. Where the
two disagree in magnitude, the human-labeled measurement is the one to
believe.

\paragraph{Accuracy probe on biography.} The correctness-trained accuracy
probe is reported on biography across all three seeds. The corresponding SEP
target is unavailable because it would require question-conditioned entropy,
which the answer-only whole-paragraph protocol does not define. The sealed OOD
grid likewise covers leave-one-out transfer only among the three short-answer
tasks. Consequently, the biography result supports neither equivalence between
the two probe targets nor a claim about short-to-long transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:probes-results}).

\paragraph{Judge coupling on biography.} A further long-form caveat
concerns the correctness labels, which are produced by the
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct judge. That same model is also one of the clustering
backends, so in the Qwen-clustered sensitivity variant the clusterer and
the grader coincide and could share a bias. The canonical headline
numbers are clustered with the independent DeBERTa-v3-large
cross-encoder, so there the clusterer and grader differ; and the backend
comparison (Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}), which varies the
clusterer while holding the label fixed, still recovers a signal, which
argues against a pure clustering artifact. What no such control rules out
is a bias in the grader itself, shared across every backend; only
human-validated labels could settle that, and it remains the main
long-form threat.

\paragraph{Clustering backend as a researcher degree of freedom.} The
choice of canonical clustering backend (DeBERTa-v3-large) is itself a
researcher degree of freedom. Because the backend is result-determining
on long-form inputs (Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}), the long-form
numbers should be read as conditional on that choice; the
clustering-sensitivity table reports the spread across backends so the
dependence is explicit rather than hidden.

\paragraph{Three seeds, one temperature, and dataset scope.} Cross-seed spreads
come from three decoding seeds; they reliably flag gross instability but cannot
fully characterize the seed distribution, so the paired bootstrap carries
the inferential weight. The primary grid is also run at a single decoding
temperature, and Section~\ref{sec:temperature-discussion} shows that the
ranking between detector families depends on it, so the headline comparison is
conditional on $T=1.0$ as well as on the three seeds. The long-form evaluation
is biography only, English only, and judge-labeled at paragraph level, so the
short-versus-long contrast and the scale trend should not be over-generalized
to all long-form generation, other languages, or settings where a different
correctness oracle would change the labels. More broadly, the grid is three
models from two families, and only the Llama-3.1 8B/70B contrast is
within-family, so every observation about scale is descriptive rather than
causal and the claim-level slope should not be extrapolated past this range.
All three generators are instruction-tuned chat models; nothing here speaks to
base models, other decoding schemes, or retrieval-augmented settings.

\paragraph{What the statistics do and do not establish.} The estimand is
conditional on seeds 42/43/44, with prompts as the resampling unit
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}). One preregistered family of nine cells
carries Holm correction; everything else is exploratory and is labeled as such.
The negative short-answer result is a failure to reject, not a demonstration of
equivalence, but its precision is quantified rather than left vague
(Table~\ref{tab:equivalence-bound}, discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:significance}). The claim those bounds support is that any
advantage for meaning-based clustering on these datasets is small, not that it
is exactly zero --- and in the TriviaQA/70B cell, where the interval is widest,
not even that much can be said.

\paragraph{The measured artifacts do not manufacture the null.} The primary
comparison is paired within each cell, which removes prompt difficulty and
label noise as confounders between the two methods. Two generation artifacts
remain, and both were measured rather than assumed: the Mistral empty-sample
rate (Section~\ref{sec:budgets-and-exclusions}) and the whole-paragraph
saturation (Section~\ref{sec:bio-results}). Each of them adds noise to a score,
so each \emph{attenuates} the performance it affects rather than inflating it.
Neither can therefore have produced the negative short-answer result, which is
a finding of no difference rather than of a difference.
