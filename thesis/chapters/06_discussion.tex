\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results from Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rather
than restating the tables. It works through five questions: why
meaning-based clustering adds almost nothing on short answers but is
essential on long-form text; what the clustering-backend comparison says
about judge quality; why model scale erodes the signal; how the
sampling methods and the hidden-state probe trade places with decoding
temperature; and how all of this relates to the original semantic
entropy paper of Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}. It
closes with the limitations the numbers do not show on their own.

\section{The two-regime picture}
\label{sec:two-regimes}

The most consequential pattern in the study is the split between the
short-answer and long-form regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:regime-split}). On
the three short-answer tasks every sampling-based detector works (AUROC
roughly $0.74$--$0.88$), and the cheapest of them --- entropy over
normalized answer strings --- is the strongest single score in most
cells, with no method beating it by a statistically significant margin
anywhere (Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface}). On long-form
biography the same surface score sits at chance ($\approx 0.50$), and a
usable signal has to be created either by meaning-level grouping
(discrete semantic entropy: $0.708 / 0.618 / 0.612$ for
Mistral-7B / Llama-3.1-8B / Llama-3.1-70B) or by reading the model's
internal state with a probe (SEP: $0.656 / 0.728 / 0.731$).

The mechanism behind both halves is visible in the worked examples of
Section~\ref{sec:hand-audits}. On short answers (Example~1,
Section~\ref{sec:audit-merge}) the model emits only a handful of distinct
normalized strings, so simply \emph{normalizing} the answers already does
most of the grouping; the extra meaning-based step only relabels a few
boundary cases. On biographies (Example~3, Section~\ref{sec:audit-bio})
every sampled paragraph is a unique string, so the surface score is
permanently pinned at its maximum of $\ln 10$ and can rank nothing --- it
must be replaced by a method that groups paragraphs by what they claim.
The biography task was added precisely to test this prediction, and it
confirms it cleanly.

\section{Does meaning-based clustering help?}
\label{sec:does-nli-help}

At the level of the full grid the answer is now clear in both
directions. On short answers, no: discrete semantic entropy never
significantly \emph{exceeds} surface entropy in any short-answer cell ---
wherever the paired bootstrap is significant it runs the other way, with
surface entropy ahead (Table~\ref{tab:significance-vs-surface}). On
long-form generation,
yes, decisively: every meaning-aware and probability-weighted estimator
beats the chance-level surface baseline by a wide margin on biography
(gains of roughly $+0.11$ to $+0.21$ AUROC).

The short-answer non-result is worth stating plainly, because the
theoretical motivation for semantic entropy is that surface variation is
noise to be removed. On these datasets that motivation is mostly
already satisfied by normalization. SVAMP is the extreme case: integer
answers normalize to identical digit strings, so meaning-based and
exact-match clustering produce nearly the same groups and nearly the same
scores. TriviaQA leaves a little more room --- Example~1 shows NLI
genuinely merging paraphrases such as ``Triumph and Disaster'' with
``Triumph and defeat'' --- but the records where this changes the ranking
are a minority, and the net effect is not large enough to separate from
run-to-run noise at $N=500$. The honest reading is a small positive
tendency on TriviaQA and essentially nothing on SVAMP, which matches the
modest size of the improvement reported in the original
work~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024}.

\section{Sensitivity to the clustering backend}
\label{sec:nli-sensitivity}

The meaning-based step needs a model to decide whether two answers are
equivalent, and Table~\ref{tab:clustering-sensitivity} shows that the
importance of that choice is itself regime-dependent. On short answers
the backend barely matters: exact-match, DeBERTa-v3-base, DeBERTa-v3-large
and the Qwen judge all land within a few AUROC points of each other,
which is unsurprising given that normalization is doing the work. On
biography the backend is \emph{result-determining}
(Figure~\ref{fig:bio-clustering-sensitivity}): on the same saved
paragraphs, the Qwen-72B judge ($0.736 / 0.685 / 0.666$) clusters
meanings more reliably than the DeBERTa cross-encoder and lifts AUROC
accordingly.

Two cautions follow. First, ``bigger judge'' is not automatically
``better judge'': the relationship between backend capacity and which
generator it helps is not monotone, and a backend that mis-merges
paragraph-length pairs does not just add noise --- it can invert the
entropy ranking, turning a useful detector into an anti-predictive one.
Second, this is the long-form counterpart of the short-answer negative
result: clustering quality matters exactly where clustering matters at
all. The same backend effect reappears at claim level in FactualBio
(Section~\ref{sec:scale-factualbio}), where exact-match grouping of the
regenerated claims understates AUROC everywhere and pushes the largest
model artificially below chance. Promoting clustering choice from an
implementation detail to a first-order design decision on everything
long-form is one of the study's main methodological points.

\section{Hidden-state probes: cheap, and strongest where entropy is
weakest}
\label{sec:sep-scaling-discussion}

The single-pass hidden-state probe (SEP) and embedding regression read
the model's internal state once, without drawing ten samples. On short
answers this thrift costs accuracy: the probe never overtakes the surface
baseline and is below it in most cells
(Table~\ref{tab:embedding-regression}), so it is attractive there only
when its $M$-fold cost saving outweighs the AUROC it gives up. A
secondary observation is that the SEP probe and the
correctness-trained embedding regression agree to within a fraction of a
point in every short-answer cell despite being trained on different
targets: whatever uncertainty information is linearly readable from the
last hidden state appears to be a single shared signal, and its level is
the practical ceiling for this probe family.

On biography the probe is far stronger than on short answers: it beats
every meaning-based clustering method for both Llama generators
($0.728$ at 8B, $0.731$ at 70B). It is not, however, the single best
long-form detector --- probability-weighted naive entropy is higher on
the two smaller models, and P(True) is highest on the 70B model
($0.777$). What is most telling is the \emph{direction} of the scale
effect: on biography the probe's AUROC \emph{rises} with model size
($0.656 \to 0.728 \to 0.731$), the opposite of the claim-level entropy
trend in Section~\ref{sec:scale-factualbio}. The probe wins relative to
clustering exactly where sampling-based clustering is weakest --- fluent,
internally consistent confabulated paragraphs --- because it reads the
representation rather than the diversity of the samples. This makes it
the natural complement to semantic entropy: cheap at test time, and
relatively strongest precisely in the regime where entropy struggles.

\section{Temperature decides which detector wins}
\label{sec:temperature-discussion}

The temperature sweep (Section~\ref{sec:temperature-sensitivity},
Figure~\ref{fig:temp-sweep}) turns the probe-versus-entropy comparison
into a deployment rule. Sampling-based entropy depends on the samples
disagreeing, so it is weak when decoding is near-greedy and strengthens
as temperature rises (TriviaQA/70B semantic entropy climbs from $0.54$
at $T{=}0.1$ to $0.78$ at $T{=}1.0$). The hidden-state probe moves the
other way, because the information it reads is present regardless of how
diverse the samples are, and noisier decoding only blurs it. The two
families therefore cross over between $T{=}0.7$ and $T{=}1.0$ on short
answers. The practical consequence is concrete: under the near-greedy
decoding common in production, the single-pass probe is both the cheaper
and the stronger choice; a sampling-based estimator only earns its cost
once the decoder is warm enough for the samples to genuinely disagree. Two
qualifications keep this honest: the temperature gain is shared by the
surface baseline, so it reflects a general need for sampling diversity
rather than something specific to semantic clustering, and P(True) is
largely temperature-insensitive. On biography the curves are flat, so
this trade-off is specific to the short-answer regime.

\section{Model scale erodes the signal}
\label{sec:scale-factualbio}

The FactualBio claim-level extension
(Section~\ref{sec:factualbio-results}) gives the clearest scale result in
the study: claim-level semantic entropy weakens monotonically as the
generator grows, from AUROC $0.688$ (Mistral-7B) through $0.644$
(Llama-3.1-8B) to $0.521$ (Llama-3.1-70B), with seed standard deviations
below $0.01$. The same direction is visible in the whole-paragraph
numbers ($0.708 \to 0.618 \to 0.612$).

The reason follows from what semantic entropy actually measures:
\emph{inconsistency}. It flags an answer when regenerated samples disagree
in meaning. A larger model that has confabulated a biographical detail
tends to regenerate the \emph{same} wrong detail fluently and
consistently, leaving little disagreement to detect. By the 70B model the
claim-level signal is barely above chance. This is not a uniform decay of
the method with scale --- the whole-paragraph probe actually improves with
scale --- but a specific erosion of the regime where detection depends on
the model re-rolling its errors differently each time. It is also a
caution for the field: the detectors that look best on small models may be
the ones that fade first as models improve.

\section{Comparison with Farquhar et al.}
\label{sec:comparison-farquhar}

Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024} report an average
AUROC of $0.790$ for semantic entropy across thirty task--model
combinations, against $0.691$ for naive entropy and $0.698$ for P(True).
The grid here was deliberately moved toward their protocol: matched
sampling ($M=10$, $T=1.0$, top-$p=0.9$, top-$k=50$, with a low-temperature
most-likely answer for accuracy labelling via SQuAD token-F1), three
shared datasets (TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP), and both probability-weighted
estimators computed alongside the discrete approximation. The remaining
differences are deliberate and disclosed: newer open generators in place
of the LLaMA-2 family, local DeBERTa cross-encoders and an open Qwen judge
in place of GPT-style judges, and 500-record evaluation sets.

Under these settings the replication is quantitatively consistent with
the original. Averaged over the nine short-answer cells at the canonical
backend, the semantic estimators land in the same band as the paper's
$0.790$ aggregate and reproduce its headline ordering --- semantic
entropy above naive entropy and P(True). One claim of the original work
does \emph{not} reproduce as stated: that semantic entropy beats the
naive baselines. Count-based surface entropy --- which the paper does not
report as a separate baseline --- is the strongest single short-answer
score in most cells, and no semantic estimator beats it significantly.
Semantic clustering earns its cost only in the long-form regime, where
the paper's qualitative claim is strongly confirmed: surface scores
collapse to chance and meaning-aware entropy recovers a real signal for
every generator. The grid also extends the original analysis in a
direction it did not quantify --- the entailment backend, which is
immaterial on short answers, is result-determining on whole paragraphs
(Section~\ref{sec:nli-sensitivity}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

\paragraph{Confabulation, not all hallucination.} Semantic entropy
targets the confabulation case defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: it
detects answers the model is internally inconsistent about. It is blind
to \emph{confident systematic errors}, where the model is wrong the same
way every time. Example~2 (Section~\ref{sec:audit-artifact}) is exactly
this case --- ten identical wrong answers, entropy zero, maximum
(mis)confidence. A deployed system would need a complementary detector
for that failure mode.

\paragraph{Noisy correctness labels.} On short-answer tasks correctness
is decided by string matching against a reference list, which marks a
correct answer wrong whenever the model uses an unlisted alias, stage
name, or title --- again Example~2, where ``Stansfield Turner'' is graded
against a gold list of ``George Bush''. This adds noise to every
short-answer AUROC and is a lower bound on the gap to studies that use an
LLM-based correctness judge throughout. The numeric SVAMP task is largely
immune, since integer answers normalize deterministically.

\paragraph{Automated long-form judging.} Both the whole-paragraph and the
claim-level biography labels come from an open LLM judge
(Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) against pinned Wikipedia references, not human
annotators. This scales to every model--seed cell but inherits the
judge's error modes; a systematic judge bias would propagate into every
long-form AUROC. The scale trend is robust across seeds but not, on this
evidence, established as robust to the choice of judge.

\paragraph{Embedding regression on biography.} Embedding regression is
reported on short answers but omitted on biography, where its
paragraph-level evaluation degenerates rather than producing a
trustworthy number. The remaining long-form methods (discrete semantic
entropy, naive entropy, P(True), KLE, and the SEP probe) are reported
across all three seeds.

\paragraph{Three seeds and dataset scope.} Cross-seed spreads come from
three decoding seeds; they reliably flag gross instability but cannot
fully characterize the seed distribution, so the paired bootstrap carries
the inferential weight. The long-form evaluation is biography only,
English only, and judge-labelled, so the short-versus-long contrast and
the scale trend should not be over-generalized to all long-form
generation, other languages, or settings where a different correctness
oracle would change the labels.
