\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reported measurements. This chapter makes three
arguments from them: which step of the method earns its advantage and what that
depends on; how much of the reported margin belongs to the measurement setup
rather than the method; and what does and does not reproduce. A final section
gives the threats that qualify all three. Two of those bear on every number in
the thesis, and they are stated here rather than held back to
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\section{Which step earns the advantage, and what it depends on}
\label{sec:what-clustering-is-worth}

\subsection{One step of the four does the work, and it is not the clustering}

Of the four steps, one is unambiguous. Normalizing the answer strings before
counting them is worth $+0.0398$ AUROC, positive in all 24 slices and resolved
in all 24. It is the only step whose margin exceeds the seed noise floor, and
it is also the cheapest thing in the pipeline: lowercasing, stripping
punctuation and articles, collapsing whitespace, pulling the number out of an
arithmetic answer. It needs no second network and no model call.

The other three sit inside or below the noise. Counting raw strings rather than
reading sequence log-likelihoods is positive in only 4 slices of 24 and
resolves against raw counting in 8. So the familiar claim that counting answer
strings beats reading log-likelihoods holds only for \emph{normalized} strings, and the normalization is doing the work there too. Weighting the
meaning-groups by likelihood adds $+0.0015$. And the step the method is named
after adds $+0.0016$.

There is a second route to the same conclusion that uses no significance test
at all. The clustering-free comparator passes every comparison the original
study runs: against entropy over sequence log-likelihoods it is positive in 24
slices of 24, against P(True) in 6 of 6, against the semantic entropy probe in
6 of 6, and against the correctness-trained probe in 6 of 6, with no slice
anywhere going the other way. Whatever the original's evidence establishes, a
method that never judges meaning establishes it too.

\subsection{On the released cross-encoders, the clustering step is null}

On \texttt{deberta-v2-xlarge-mnli}, the entailment model the released code
loads, clustering gives no measurable benefit over surface entropy. Eight
slices, being four prompting conditions crossed with two independent LLM
judges, give margins between $-0.0020$ and $+0.0223$ AUROC, and a paired bootstrap
resampling both the nine units and the records inside them resolves none of
them.

Three things about that result are worth separating.

It is a \emph{tested} null, not an underpowered one. Pairing the two estimators
on the same records could have found a small consistent effect that a
cell-level test would miss, and it was run. What it found is that record-level
pairing narrows the intervals without changing a verdict. The record-only
scheme resolves 18 of 24 slices on intervals about three times too narrow, and
once the between-unit component is carried, seven of those resolutions
disappear and the surviving majority points the other way.

It is bounded from the other side too. Nine independent units give a minimum
detectable effect of about 0.028 AUROC against a mean absolute effect of 0.023,
so an effect much below 0.028 would not reliably show up here. The intervals
that were actually computed are tighter than that in places: on the canonical
eight they run from a width of about 0.016 in the short-phrase conditions to
about 0.10 in the sentence-length ones, so the bound this study can defend is
roughly 0.01 AUROC in its tightest slices and closer to 0.06 in its loosest.
What the data establish is the absence of a \emph{measurable} benefit, not the
absence of any benefit. Settling that would need more datasets and generators, not more
seeds, because seeds account for only about 15\,\% of the variance of this
contrast.

And it is not a claim that the method fails. Semantic entropy is the best
detector among those the original evaluates, by a comfortable margin, and it
reproduces the headline comparison against naive predictive entropy at $+0.063$
to $+0.162$ in the sentence-length condition on the canonical backend, and at
$+0.057$ to $+0.162$ across all three backends. The claim is narrower and is
about attribution: that reproduced effect shows counting \emph{normalized}
answer strings beats reading sequence log-likelihoods. It does not show that
grouping those strings by meaning adds anything further, because the original
design has no comparator that isolates the grouping. This study supplies one,
and under the configuration the released code ships with, that comparator is
not beaten.

\subsection{With a stronger entailment model, a great deal}

Substituting Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as the entailment judge, on identical stored
generations, moves discrete semantic entropy from 0.757 to 0.823 in the
sentence-length condition and turns the margin over surface entropy into
$+0.087$ under both judges, resolved in both. In the short-phrase condition the
same substitution gives $+0.012$ and $+0.014$, also resolved. Surface entropy
is unchanged to four decimal places across all three backends, so the whole
difference comes from the entailment model.

The clustering step is therefore not worthless. It is worth what the model
doing it can deliver, and the two released cross-encoders cannot deliver enough
to be detected at this sample size. That is a statement about the published
configuration, which is what a reader reproducing the result would run.

\subsection{Answer length is a marker, not the mechanism}

It is tempting to read the pattern as a story about answer length, because the
advantage does grow with length across prompting conditions. In the $2\times2$,
holding the worked-example count at zero and varying only the instruction, mean
answer length differs by a factor of 3.7 and the margin falls from $+0.021$ to
$+0.006$. Across all four corners the margin rises with length, and worked
examples enter only by shortening answers.

Two things stop that from being a mechanism.

First, and decisively, there is a within-condition contrast on identical
generations. Table~\ref{tab:backend-by-model} splits the sentence-length margin
by generator. On the canonical cross-encoder the generator with by far the
longest answers, Llama-3.1-8B at 667 mean characters, has the \emph{smallest}
margin, $+0.012$, while two generators producing 38 and 60
characters sit at $+0.026$ and $+0.029$. Substituting the 72B entailment model
inverts the ordering: the same long-answer generator now has the
\emph{largest} margin at $+0.132$, eleven times its own value on the
cross-encoder, against $+0.067$ and $+0.063$ for the two short ones. Nothing
about the answers changed between those columns. If swapping the entailment
model reverses which answer length benefits, length cannot be the variable that
matters.

Second, there is the long-form arm, taken up next.

What survives is weaker and more useful. Length modulates the size of the gain
once a strong enough entailment model is in place. The Qwen advantage is seven
times larger at 255 characters than at 11. But with the released cross-encoders
there is no gain at any length to modulate. Length marks the
regime; entailment quality is the gate.

\subsection{The long-form arm is the limiting case}

On paragraph-length output the string estimators are not weak, they are
degenerate. Ten sampled biographies are never string-identical, so every sample
forms its own cluster and the score is constant within a cell: surface entropy
sits at 0.4965 and naive sample entropy at 0.4973 under every backend, which is
chance up to ties. So the right statement is not that semantic entropy beats
its baselines there. It is that grouping is a \emph{precondition} for having any
signal at all, and a comparison against a constant is vacuous.

What the arm does support is the same conclusion as the short-answer arm,
reached independently: the informative variation is between entailment models,
and it spans 0.184 AUROC, from 0.5245 with \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-base} through
0.6703 with \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large} to 0.7084 with the 72B judge.

Two cautions attach to this arm. It sets \texttt{strict\_entailment} where the
original leaves it at the relaxed default for paragraphs, so its backend
comparison is not a test of the original's Supplementary Figure~1, and the weak
\texttt{nli-deberta-v3-base} figure follows from the rule rather than from the
model. And it has a single grader, which is also one of its clustering
backends, so it is the one place where the two-roles concern of
Section~\ref{sec:threats} is uncontrolled.

\section{The reported margin is largely a property of the setup}
\label{sec:apparatus}

Three choices this literature treats as configuration move the results by more
than the choice of method does. Each was varied on one fixed set of
generations, so each effect belongs to the choice rather than to the data.
Figure~\ref{fig:factor-effects} places them beside the steps of the method.

\subsection{The entailment model is worth more than the method}

Holding the generations, the questions, the correctness labels and the
estimator fixed, and changing only which model judges entailment, discrete
semantic entropy moves from 0.751 to 0.823, a spread of 0.072 AUROC. The
margin between discrete semantic entropy and its clustering-free comparator, on
the canonical backend under the same judges, is 0.020. The choice a reader is
invited to treat as an implementation detail is worth about three times the
choice the literature reports as the finding.

The three backends also disagree about what they are measuring. Taken two at a
time, they assign the same number of clusters to between 60.3\,\% and
72.8\,\% of records, all three agree on at most 60.3\,\%, and their mean
counts differ by 0.33 out of ten samples. ``Bidirectional entailment
clustering'' does not name one procedure.

\subsection{Diversity-based and likelihood-based detectors part ways}
\label{sec:diversity-vs-likelihood}

Sampling temperature does not make detectors uniformly better or worse. It
picks which family wins, and the two families move in opposite directions
relative to each other.

Every estimator that reads spread across samples falls toward chance as
temperature drops, because low temperature is where the model stops producing
variety. Discrete semantic entropy falls from 0.757 at $T=1.0$ to 0.568 at
$T=0.1$, and surface entropy from 0.736 to 0.589. Naive predictive entropy
reads sequence likelihoods rather than spread, and is comparatively flat: 0.694
to 0.644. So the families cross. At $T=0.1$ the likelihood-based estimator
leads both diversity-based ones; at $T=1.0$ it trails both.

Two things follow. The conventional setting of $T=1.0$, which the original
fixes and does not vary, is the most favorable point in the measured range for
the sampling-based approach. So a reported advantage for that family is partly
a statement about the decoding configuration it was measured under. And
a deployment that decodes at low temperature for other reasons would be
choosing between these methods on different evidence than a benchmark at
$T=1.0$ provides.

This is not a test of Kuhn et al.'s intermediate-temperature
optimum~\cite{kuhn_semantic_uncertainty_2023}. There, lowering the temperature
also changed the accuracy of the answer being judged; here the graded answer is
held outside the sweep, so only the diversity side can move.

\subsection{The correctness rule reverses the ranking}

The same answers, scored by the same detectors, rank differently depending on
which rule decides correctness. In the sentence-length condition on the
canonical backend, semantic entropy leads surface entropy by $+0.020$ and
$+0.022$ under the two LLM judges and trails it by $0.051$ under the
token-overlap rule the released code applies by default.

The token-overlap rule is visibly doing something odd in that condition. It
scores the generators at 0.344 mean accuracy where the judges score them at
0.623 and 0.677, and in six of the 27 cells it marks under 5\,\% of answers
correct. In three of them that is three or four answers out of 378. The rule
penalizes long answers, because a correct answer padded with extra words loses
overlap against a short reference.

It would be convenient to conclude that the token-F1 result is an artifact and
the LLM-graded numbers are the real ones. That is not available here, for two
reasons. Surface entropy resolvably beats semantic entropy under token-F1 in
seven slices, so the result is not noise. And under the LLM judges the two
methods are \emph{indistinguishable} on the canonical backend. The judges do
not vindicate semantic entropy, they fail to separate the methods. What can be
said is that the ranking depends on which definition of correctness is adopted,
and that this thesis has no human labels to settle it.
Section~\ref{sec:threats} explains why that is worse than a missing robustness
check.

\subsection{The headline metric changes which method appears to lead}

The original reports a rejection-accuracy summary beside AUROC without noting
that the two can rank the methods differently. Over the same 24 slices,
discrete semantic entropy leads surface entropy in 14 under AUROC, 8 under the
released AURAC definition and 6 under the normalized one. Switching metric
moves the verdict from a bare majority for semantic entropy to a majority for
surface entropy. Given the intervals none of those margins is resolvable, so
the point is not that one metric is right; it is that the choice matters and is
not reported as a choice.

One caution governs either rejection-accuracy definition: over the 648
discrete-semantic-entropy cells at $T=1.0$, the released definition tracks the
generating model's own accuracy at $r = 0.971$ and the normalized one at
$r = 0.965$, against $r = 0.502$ for AUROC. Both are largely reporting how
often the model was right rather than how well the detector ranked, which is
why AUROC is the metric this thesis leads with.

\section{Against Farquhar et al.}
\label{sec:comparison-farquhar}

\subsection{What reproduces}

The central claim reproduces on open models two generations newer. Semantic
entropy is the best detector among the comparators that study uses: it leads
P(True) by $+0.025$, the semantic entropy probe by $+0.051$ and the
correctness-trained probe by $+0.050$, in every slice where each was measured.
Its headline comparison against naive predictive entropy reproduces at $+0.063$
to $+0.162$ on the canonical backend, against the $+0.099$ implied by its own
reported figures.

Two further findings reproduce. Discrete and likelihood-weighted semantic
entropy are interchangeable, differing by $-0.0015$ on average over 648 cells.
That is the most stable contrast measured anywhere here. And the probes lose accuracy
out of distribution, by 0.061 to 0.105 AUROC, with the correctness-trained
probe's in-distribution advantage shrinking in one condition and reversing in
the other. That reversal is the direction Kossen et
al.~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024} predict, since their claim is
that semantic entropy probes generalize better out of distribution than probes
trained directly on accuracy. Their stronger claim, that the probes retain high
performance, is not what Section~\ref{sec:paper-baselines} finds here: both
probes sit below the sampling-based estimators in every slice where each was
measured.

\subsection{What does not}

\textbf{P(True) does not improve with model size.} Within the Llama family the
ordering is flat or inverted: 0.711 for the 70B model against 0.726 for the 8B
in the sentence-length condition, and 0.819 against 0.826 in the short-phrase
condition. Two sizes in one family is a weak test of a trend the original
observes across three families, so this is a failure to reproduce rather than a
refutation.

\textbf{DeBERTa is not the best paragraph entailment model here}, for a
procedural reason. The original's Supplementary Figure~1 uses the relaxed
entailment rule, while this study sets \texttt{strict\_entailment} everywhere. A
strict rule with a lower-recall model over-splits, which is the failure the
relaxation exists to avoid. The two are not the same algorithm, so this is not
a contradiction.

\textbf{The generation regime is not the original's.} Sentence-length answers
here average 255.2 characters against the 95.6 reported, and one generator
reaches a cell mean of 826.3. Part of that is the token-budget asymmetry
inherited from the released code: the estimators see samples capped at 50
tokens while correctness is judged on an answer allowed 256. Conclusions drawn
in that condition, including the strongest evidence for semantic entropy here,
are about a regime the original did not study.

\subsection{What the original's design cannot settle, and this one can}

Two of the original's own comparisons are not interrogated there. It gives
AURAC beside AUROC without noting that the two can rank the methods
differently. And it validates its graders against humans without asking whether
the choice of grader changes the ranking, which on this data it does.

The substantive addition is the comparator. Every baseline the original
evaluates differs from semantic entropy in more than one way at once, so
however large the reported margin, the design cannot attribute it to any single
step. A four-step decomposition can, and it attributes the reliable gain to the
normalization rather than the grouping.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

Ranked, most serious first. The first two bear on every number in this thesis,
and a reader should meet them before the conclusions rather than after.

\paragraph{The correctness judges were never validated against human labels.}
Two LLM judges agreeing on 93.7\,\% of records in the sentence-length condition
bounds their mutual \emph{consistency}, not their shared error. Two models of
similar construction, prompted alike, can agree closely and be wrong together.
Farquhar et al.\ perform exactly this check, on 100 questions in their
Supplementary Note~6; nothing here does. Every accuracy figure reported here,
and so every AUROC, rests on an unvalidated judge. This is not a caveat on one
table. It conditions the whole of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\paragraph{The entailment model and the grader answer the same question.} Both
are asked, in effect, whether two texts mean the same thing, and both are
models of the same kind. A method that groups answers by meaning will look good
exactly when correctness is scored by meaning, for reasons that have nothing to
do with detecting confabulation.

The data carry the fingerprint. Semantic entropy's margin over surface entropy
is $+0.020$ under LLM grading and $-0.051$ under token-F1 in the same
condition, and the Qwen backend gains $+0.065$ under LLM grading and $-0.015$
under token-F1. Each estimator does best where its notion of sameness matches
the grader's.

Nothing in this dataset breaks the tie, because it has no human labels. The
narrower circularity check of Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity} does not
cover it: re-grading with an independent judge asks whether \emph{one
particular model} in two roles inflates its own result, and finds that it does
not. This threat survives that check, because the problem is the class of judge
and not the identity of the model. The honest position is that the thesis
cannot say whether the LLM-graded numbers or the token-F1 numbers are closer to
the truth, and it treats neither as the true one.

\paragraph{The sentence-length condition is not the original's.} 255.2
characters mean against 95.6, with one generator at 667, for the token-budget
reason above. The estimators see paper-length answers while correctness is
judged on longer ones.

\paragraph{Nine independent units.} The design cannot resolve small
semantic-versus-surface margins, and the record-level pairing that could have
rescued them does not. What stays untestable is the other direction: a real
effect below roughly 0.01 AUROC would not be detected here.

\paragraph{The clustering step is order-dependent by construction.} The
released algorithm scans answers in generation order and reassigns, so
permuting the samples can change the entropy without changing a single
entailment judgment (Section~\ref{sec:order_dependence_example}). The effect is
invisible in aggregate: the partition is well defined and the entropy is
finite, so nothing downstream signals that another ordering would have given
another number.

\paragraph{One long-form task}, with degenerate string baselines and a single
grader that is also one of its clustering backends.

\paragraph{Coverage gaps.} The probes exist on one backend and P(True) on
another, so the two are never directly comparable; the temperature sweep omits
both, and covers only the original's two conditions on two of the three
backends; and the two added prompting conditions exist on one backend, so the
$2\times2$ can only be estimated on the canonical model and not on the backend
most favorable to semantic entropy.
