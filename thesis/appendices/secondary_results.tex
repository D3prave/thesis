\chapter{Supporting and Secondary Results}
\label{app:secondary-results}

This appendix gives supporting results for the secondary
analyses summarized in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The same aggregation script
used for the main results assembles the tables. Point estimates come from
\texttt{measurements.csv}. The hierarchical confidence intervals in
Table~\ref{tab:all-slices} are imported from the record-level paired-bootstrap
analysis because they cannot be recomputed from the exported measurement table
alone.

As in the main Results chapter, a comparison is one reported combination of
prompting condition, entailment model, correctness rule, and pair of methods.
An AUROC difference is the first method's AUROC minus the second's, so a
positive value favors the first method.

\section{All semantic-versus-surface comparisons}
\label{app:all-comparisons}

Table~\ref{tab:all-slices} and Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-forest} report all 24
comparisons. Eleven hierarchical intervals exclude zero. Four favor semantic
entropy, and all four use the Qwen entailment model. Seven favor surface
entropy, and all seven use token-F1 correctness labels.

The two smaller Qwen-entailment-model differences that favor semantic entropy are below
the 0.0245 seed-variation reference. This is not a
contradiction. The seed range describes how much one method's AUROC changes
across seeds. A paired test compares two methods on the same generated answers,
so variation shared by both methods cancels when their scores are subtracted.
The seed range is therefore descriptive context for one AUROC value, while the
paired interval assesses a difference under the stated resampling assumptions.

\section{Probes in and out of distribution}
\label{sec:probe-results}

Tables~\ref{tab:probes} and~\ref{tab:probes-ood} report the two hidden-state
probes in and out of distribution. The semantic entropy probe learns targets
from \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-large}; the accuracy probe learns correctness labels.

In distribution, on sentence-length answers, the accuracy probe leads the
semantic entropy probe by 0.046 and 0.042 under the two judges, though under
token-F1 the ordering reverses. On short-phrase answers the semantic entropy
probe leads under both judges.

\input{includes/t_probes.tex}

Out of distribution, meaning trained on the pooled prompts of the other two
short-answer tasks and scored on the held-out one, the accuracy probe reaches
0.625 on sentence-length answers and the semantic entropy probe 0.615.
On short-phrase answers the ordering reverses, at 0.642 against 0.668.
Comparing each probe against its own in-distribution value under the same
grader, the four losses are 0.094 and 0.061 on sentence-length answers and
0.105 and 0.077 on short phrases. The mean is 0.084 and the range 0.061 to
0.105. Under the grader the out-of-distribution pass used, the accuracy
probe's advantage of 0.042 in the first condition falls to 0.010.

Two limits apply. Both probes are trained and evaluated on disjoint question
sets in both regimes, so these are held-out figures throughout. But the
out-of-distribution pass ran under one grader per condition. It used the Llama
judge for sentence-length answers and token-F1 for short phrases. So the two
out-of-distribution rows are not graded alike and should not be compared with
each other directly.

Every interval includes zero, and the largest difference is below the

The two zero-shot conditions isolate the instruction because both omit worked
examples. Changing only the instruction changes mean answer length by a factor
of 3.7, while the AUROC difference falls from $+0.021$ to $+0.006$. In the two
five-shot conditions, changing only the instruction changes mean answer length

Two limits from Section~\ref{sec:conditions} make this a secondary analysis.
Both conditions that produce short phrases are five-shot, so the design separates length from
example count only among the longer answers. The added conditions also exist
only on the default entailment model, so the factorial cannot test the entailment model with
the largest AUROC differences in Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity}.

\section{Secondary comparison of AUROC and AURAC}
\label{sec:aurac-disagreement}

The framework is \emph{selective prediction}. The uncertainty score is a
ranking signal, and the records ranked most uncertain are the ones a deployment
would reject or check more expensively.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{AUROC}. Treating ``incorrect'' as the positive class, AUROC is
        the probability that a randomly chosen incorrect record gets a higher
        uncertainty score than a randomly chosen correct one, with tied scores
        counting as one half. Here $0.5$ is
        random and $1.0$ is perfect. It depends only on the ranking, so the
        unit of the entropy does not matter. This is the metric that isolates
        the detector, and the one this thesis leads with.
  \item \emph{Rejection-accuracy curve}. For each rejection fraction $f$, the
        accuracy on the remaining $1-f$ of records after discarding the $f$
        most uncertain. A useful signal makes the curve rise above the flat
        raw-accuracy line.
  \item \emph{AURAC}, the area under that curve, reported in \textbf{two
        definitions} in a secondary analysis. They are not directly comparable,
        and the released code uses the first:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \texttt{aurac\_paper}: twenty retention quantiles from $0.1$,
            with $dx = 0.9/19$, unnormalized. Maximum attainable about $0.947$.
          \item \texttt{aurac\_mean\_retained}: mean retained accuracy averaged
            over all retention levels.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Unlike AUROC, both AURAC definitions depend on how accurate the generator was
to begin with, so their values are comparable only within one task and model.

\paragraph{A worked example.} Suppose a detector scores five answers, three
correct (C) and two wrong (W):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Answer & \#1 & \#2 & \#3 & \#4 & \#5 \\
\midrule
Detector score & 0.10 & 0.20 & 0.25 & 0.30 & 0.90 \\
Actually & C & C & W & C & W \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{AUROC by hand.} Form every (wrong, correct) pair; there are $2 \times 3 =
6$. The wrong answer scores higher in five of them; the exception is
(\#3, \#4), where the wrong answer \#3 ($0.25$) scores below the correct answer
\#4 ($0.30$). So AUROC $= 5/6 \approx 0.83$.

\emph{Rejection accuracy by hand.} Start from the raw accuracy, $3/5 = 0.60$.
Reject the highest-scoring answer (\#5, wrong) and accuracy rises to
$3/4 = 0.75$. Reject the next (\#4, correct) and it dips to $2/3 \approx 0.67$, because a
good answer was thrown away. Reject the next (\#3, wrong) and it
reaches $2/2 = 1.00$; rejecting \#2 leaves the correct answer \#1, so the last
non-empty set is also $1.00$. The curve is $0.60, 0.75, 0.67, 1.00, 1.00$, and
averaging over those five levels gives $0.803$. For both metrics, higher is
better.

Raw answer accuracy is reported as context only. It is not a measure of the
detector.

AUROC is the primary metric in this thesis. Table~\ref{tab:aurac} provides a
secondary sensitivity analysis using the two rejection-accuracy definitions.
With AUROC, discrete semantic entropy leads surface entropy in 14 of 24
comparisons, with a mean difference of $+0.0016$. With the released
\texttt{aurac\_paper} definition, it leads in 8 of 24 and the mean difference
is $-0.0015$. With \texttt{aurac\_mean\_retained}, it leads in 6 of 24 and the
mean difference is $-0.0033$.

\input{includes/t_aurac.tex}

Across the 648 discrete-semantic-entropy cells at $T=1.0$,
\texttt{aurac\_paper} correlates with the generating model's accuracy at
$r = 0.971$, and \texttt{aurac\_mean\_retained} correlates at $r = 0.965$.
The corresponding correlation for AUROC is $r = 0.502$. The two AURAC measures
therefore reflect the model's accuracy much more strongly than AUROC does.
