\section{Exact order of random selections}
\label{app:random-selection}

The question selection follows the released
\texttt{generate\_answers.py}~\cite{kossen_semantic_uncertainty_code_2024}.
Three selections share one random sequence: worked examples, P(True)
examples, and evaluation questions. Their order matters because each draw
advances that sequence. The excerpt below shows the selection order using this
study's values; the surrounding loader supplies the eligible indices for each
split.

\begin{quote}\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
random.seed(10)
prompt_indices = random.sample(answerable_indices, 5)
remaining = list(set(answerable_indices)
                 - set(prompt_indices))
p_true_indices = random.sample(answerable_indices, 20)
remaining = list(set(remaining) - set(p_true_indices))
for split in ("train", "validation"):
    indices = random.sample(list(possible_indices),
                            min(400, len(dataset)))
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}

This selects 400 questions per split, except for SVAMP's validation split,
which contains only 300. Selection is fixed across generation seeds. Every
model and seed therefore answers the same questions, allowing paired
comparisons. The duplicate-removal step for TriviaQA is described in
Section~\ref{sec:datasets}.

\section{A worked example of order-dependent clustering}
\label{app:order-dependence}
\label{sec:order_dependence_example}

The main worked example in Section~\ref{sec:worked_example} has a transitive
entailment relation, so the released anchor scan and connected-components
grouping return the same clusters. This constructed example shows why that
agreement is not guaranteed.

Take four sampled answers to the same question:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\hline
Index & Sampled answer \\
\hline
$a$ & \emph{Canberra} \\
$b$ & \emph{Canberra, the capital} \\
$c$ & \emph{The capital city} \\
$d$ & \emph{Sydney} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Suppose the entailment model labels $a \leftrightarrow b$ and
$b \leftrightarrow c$ as mutual entailment, but not
$a \leftrightarrow c$. These are hypothetical model judgments, not a claim
that the answers truly have this relation. An entailment model can produce
inconsistent judgments even when the question is supplied, because the pairwise
test does not enforce transitivity.

Connected-components grouping joins $a$, $b$ and $c$ through $b$, producing
$\{a,b,c\},\{d\}$ in every order. Algorithm~\ref{alg:clustering} instead gives:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Order presented & Resulting clusters & Entropy \\
\hline
$a,b,c,d$ & $\{a,b\},\{c\},\{d\}$ & $1.04$ nats \\
$b,a,c,d$ & $\{a,b,c\},\{d\}$ & $0.56$ nats \\
$a,c,b,d$ & $\{a\},\{b,c\},\{d\}$ & $1.04$ nats \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The same four answers and the same entailment judgments produce two different
entropy values. The third row shows reassignment: $a$ first assigns $b$ to its
cluster. The scan then reaches the unassigned answer $c$, which becomes an
anchor and assigns $b$ again. Answer $a$ is left alone, even though it matches
$b$.

The reported score therefore depends on answer order as well as entailment
judgments. A normal-looking score does not reveal whether another order would
have produced a different value. This example establishes the possibility, not
its frequency or size in the experiments. The entailment-model comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:clustering-sensitivity} changes a different factor and does
not bound the effect of sample order.

\section{Additional collection checks}
\label{app:collection-checks}

\paragraph{Answer length and token budgets.}
The most-likely answer has a 256-token budget, while each of the ten entropy
samples has a 50-token budget. Sentence-length answers average 255.2 characters
here, compared with 95.6 in the original study. The largest cell mean, 826.3
characters, occurs for Llama-3.1-8B. These observations describe a difference in
the collected answers; the study does not isolate the token budget's causal
effect on their length.

\paragraph{Temperature-sweep label checks.}
The LLM correctness labels are joined by \texttt{prompt\_id} from the main
collection. They are bit-identical across all 108 checked cells, so all sweep
temperatures are judged against the same temperature-$0.1$ answer. Token-F1
labels instead use the modal sampled answer because the sweep did not save a
separate most-likely answer. Their accuracy differs from the main run in all
54 checked cells, by 0.015 on average in absolute terms and at most 0.262.
These checks support the restriction in Section~\ref{sec:temperature-sweep}:
only LLM correctness labels are used to interpret the sweep, and its
answer-length fields are not compared across temperatures.

\section{Implementation and HPC execution}
\label{app:hpc-details}

The experiments used the NHR@FAU computing center. Its GPU cluster
\emph{Alex}, with NVIDIA A100 nodes, handled generation and GPU-heavy steps.
Its CPU cluster \emph{Fritz} handled later cross-encoder clustering.

\paragraph{Code and libraries.}
The Python package targets Python~3.11+ and is installed with \texttt{uv}.
Generation uses Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} so that hidden states for
the probes can be saved in the same pass. The study's vLLM path is used for
LLM entailment and correctness judgments, where only generated text is needed.
The probes use scikit-learn.

The processing steps read a prepared dataset, sample $M$ answers per prompt,
group them with the chosen entailment model, compute the entropy scores,
attach correctness labels and write one JSONL record per prompt.
Appendix~\ref{app:schema} describes the exported measurement format.

\paragraph{Resources.}
Generation for the 7B and 8B models used one A100 GPU. The 70B model used four
A100-80GB GPUs with its weights divided between them; the cross-encoder ran on
CPU to leave GPU memory available. Later cross-encoder clustering ran on
Fritz. The Qwen2.5-72B entailment model and correctness grader ran on Alex.
GPU jobs were submitted through Slurm.

\paragraph{Job metadata and checks.}
Collection jobs require a pinned code commit, check that the repository HEAD
matches it, and refuse to start with changes to tracked files. Their
\texttt{job\_meta.json} records the code commit as
\texttt{analysis\_code\_commit}, together with the Slurm job identifier, host,
model, dataset, decoding parameters, seed and input path. Later processing
steps can record their own revision while retaining the upstream generation
revision. These records should not be read as a claim that every processing
step ran at one commit.

Code, configuration, result tables and figures are version-controlled. Model
caches, raw generations and hidden states are stored separately.

\paragraph{Analysis and validation.}
Clustering jobs check five output fields against the requested settings: the
clustering rule, closure rule, clustering input, entailment input format and
question-conditioning flag. A mismatch fails the job. These checks catch
configuration errors but do not validate the entailment model's judgments.

Analysis is separated from generation so saved outputs can be analyzed again.
Most Results tables are generated from \texttt{measurements.csv}; imported
record-level analyses and the hand-written reproduction scorecard have
separate provenance, as stated at the start of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.
Rebuilding a table from the measurement package is therefore not the same as
recomputing every value from raw answers.

\section{Bootstrap assumptions and saved-run provenance}
\label{sec:bootstrap-assumptions}

The recorded paired analysis uses 2,000 resamples, as specified explicitly in
the saved run description. This overrides the script's default of 10,000.
Within each selected dataset--generator pair, the three seed-level AUROC
differences are averaged; the statistic then averages over pairs. Record
resampling keeps the two compared methods paired, but draws question indices
separately for each seed cell. It does not synchronize the same question's
resampling across seeds or generators, or resample datasets and generators
as separate factors.

A record resample containing only one correctness class has undefined AUROC
and is omitted from that draw's average. This can matter when very few answers
are labeled correct. The exported intervals do not record how often such
omissions occurred. The reported intervals and bootstrap tail proportions
therefore remain conditional on these implementation choices. They are not
adjusted for the many comparisons reported here.

The local measurement package can verify the paired point estimates, but
cannot reconstruct the record-level intervals without the original scored
answers. Tests of the bootstrap implementation and checks of the exported
values are distinct from an independent rerun of those intervals.

\section{Probe training and saved features}
\label{app:probe-training}

For each training question, the most-likely answer supplies a hidden state,
read at the last generated token as fixed by the stop sequence. Separately, ten
sampled responses are grouped by meaning to compute discrete semantic entropy.
The high/low threshold is chosen from the training entropies to minimize the
sum of squared deviations within the two groups, following Equation~(5) of
Kossen et al.~\cite{kossen_semantic_entropy_probes_2024}. A logistic-regression
classifier then predicts that label from the hidden state. Correctness labels
are not used; the accuracy probe is the variant trained on those. At test
time, generate one answer, read the same hidden state, apply the classifier,
and use its output as the uncertainty score.

The probe is trained separately per dataset, model and seed on its own prompts
and scored on a disjoint set, so its numbers are a genuine held-out test. The
feature choices are fixed in advance rather than tuned on the evaluation set.
An audit of the saved probe inputs later found one missing training hidden state
in the NQ-Open Llama-3.1-70B short-phrase run with seed 42. The reported fits
predate that finding; this audit does not establish how that record was handled
during fitting. All retained evaluation records have saved features, but this
training-input gap limits exact reproduction of the existing fits.

One detail follows the code rather than the natural reading: the hidden state is
taken at position $n_{\mathrm{generated}}-1$, where $n_{\mathrm{generated}}$
comes from where the stop sequence cut the answer, and no end-of-sequence token
has to be present. The probe is far cheaper than full semantic entropy, but
limited to what is linearly readable from one hidden state.
