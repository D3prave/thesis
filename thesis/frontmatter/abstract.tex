\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often give fluent answers that are wrong or unsupported.
They also rarely warn the reader when this happens. Semantic entropy can flag
some of these failures without retraining the model. The model answers the same
question several times, and answers that mean the same thing are placed in one
group.
Shannon entropy then measures how widely the samples are spread across the
groups. Agreement in meaning suggests lower risk, while conflicting meanings
suggest higher risk.

This thesis does not propose a new method. It reimplements the published method
by following the authors' released code. The experiments use three open
language models, three short-answer benchmarks, and a long-form biography
evaluation. The main goal is to
evaluate how well semantic entropy detects incorrect answers, how it compares
with other uncertainty estimators, and under which conditions it performs
better. The comparison includes Farquhar et al.'s baselines, semantic entropy
probes that predict correctness from hidden states, and entropy over normalized
answer strings.

Semantic entropy ranks incorrect answers above correct ones better than
chance in the main comparisons graded by large language models. On sentence-length answers, both
semantic estimators outperform the measured Farquhar baselines and achieve
nearly identical performance. Their advantage over naive predictive entropy
is larger for sentence-length answers than for short phrases. On short
phrases, several leading methods are nearly tied.

On long-form biography answers, semantic entropy records a higher area under
the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) than the measured string
baselines with the larger entailment models, and this advantage appears across
the three generators for those models. Exact matching shows no advantage at all, and the base
entailment model shows one for only one of the three generators.

The conditions matter. With the tested Qwen entailment model, semantic entropy
also improves on normalized\nobreakdash-string counting under both large-language-model
correctness judges.
No such additional benefit is detected with the released entailment models.
In the sentence-length temperature comparison, naive predictive entropy
overtakes the sample-diversity estimators at low temperature. Changing the correctness rule can also reverse
the semantic-versus-surface ranking.

A supporting decomposition examines which steps explain these comparisons.
Normalizing answer strings before counting them gives the most consistent
improvement; the added value of grouping by meaning depends on the entailment
model. An undetected benefit does not establish equivalence. The results
remain conditional on the correctness rules, the limited dataset--model grid,
and possible shared biases between semantic grouping and grading.

Semantic entropy is therefore a useful detector in the tested settings, but
it is not consistently the best method. Its advantage must be stated together
with the comparator, prompting condition, entailment model, and evaluation rule.
