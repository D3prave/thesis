\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often produce fluent answers that are wrong or
unsupported by the input, and they rarely warn the reader when this
happens. A useful question-answering system should also be able to say
``I am not sure'' when its answer is unreliable.

This thesis builds on the work of Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024},
who proposed \emph{semantic entropy} as a training-free method for
detecting such unreliable answers. The idea is to sample several answers
from the model for the same question, group answers that mean the same
thing, and compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting group sizes. A
high entropy then signals that the model is internally inconsistent
about the meaning of its answer. Grouping is done in one of two ways:
exact-match clustering on a lightly normalized string, or bidirectional
Natural Language Inference (NLI) entailment using a fine-tuned
DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder. The first version is referred to throughout
the thesis as a \emph{surface-form} baseline and not as true semantic
entropy.

The contribution of this thesis is not to invent semantic entropy but
to re-implement and evaluate it reproducibly at full grid scale,
including a controlled comparison between short-answer and long-form
generation and a claim-level long-form extension. A reproducible
pipeline is built on the NHR@FAU high-performance computing cluster.
The final evaluation covers three open generators (Mistral-7B,
Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B) on four tasks (TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP,
and a FactScore-style biography task graded by an LLM judge against
Wikipedia), each decoded under three seeds with the original paper's
sampling and accuracy-labelling protocol, alongside P(True),
embedding-regression and semantic-entropy probes, Kernel Language
Entropy, multiple entailment backends, and a sampling-temperature
sweep. AUROC and
rejection-accuracy curves are the main quality metrics; cross-seed
stability and record-level paired-bootstrap significance are reported
separately.

The findings are regime-dependent. On short-answer tasks all
sampling-based estimators work (AUROC 0.74--0.88), and the aggregates
are quantitatively consistent with Farquhar et al.\ under the matched
protocol, but entropy over normalized strings is the strongest single
score in six of nine cells and no method beats it significantly: a
record-level analysis shows that answer normalization, not semantic
clustering, performs almost all of the grouping on short answers. On
long-form biography the surface baselines collapse to chance while
meaning-aware and representation-based detectors recover a significant
signal for every generator; the strongest long-form scores come from
probability-weighted naive entropy on the two smaller models and from a
single-pass self-evaluation, P(True), on the largest. The clustering backend is result-determining on long-form
inputs --- spanning chance-level to clearly useful behavior on the same
samples --- and at claim level, semantic
entropy's discriminative power decreases monotonically with generator
scale (AUROC 0.69 $\rightarrow$ 0.64 $\rightarrow$ 0.52 from 7B to
70B): larger models repeat their confabulations too consistently for
consistency-based detection. The value of moving beyond surface-only
uncertainty is therefore confined to --- and decisive in --- the
long-form regime, where it depends critically on the clustering
backend and erodes with model scale. A sampling-temperature sweep adds
a deployment-facing qualification: sampling-based entropy and the
hidden-state probe vary with decoding temperature in opposite
directions and cross, so which detector is stronger is set by the
decoding regime rather than fixed.
