\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often produce fluent answers that are wrong or
unsupported by the input, and they rarely warn the reader when this
happens. A useful question-answering system should also be able to say
``I am not sure'' when its answer is unreliable.

This thesis builds on the work of Farquhar et al.~\cite{semantic_entropy_nature_2024},
who proposed \emph{semantic entropy} as a training-free method for
detecting such unreliable answers. The idea is to sample several answers
from the model for the same question, group answers that mean the same
thing, and compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting group sizes. A
high entropy then signals that the model is internally inconsistent
about the meaning of its answer. Grouping is done in one of two ways:
exact-match clustering on a lightly normalized string, or bidirectional
Natural Language Inference (NLI) entailment using a fine-tuned
DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder. The first version is referred to throughout
the thesis as a \emph{surface-form} baseline and not as true semantic
entropy.

The contribution of this thesis is not to invent semantic entropy but to
re-implement and evaluate it on a smaller, locally reproducible setup.
A two-phase pipeline is built on the NHR@FAU high-performance computing
cluster. Phase~1 uses exact-match clustering; Phase~2 replaces it with
NLI clustering. The evaluation covers Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-70B on
three datasets: the short-answer benchmarks TriviaQA and SVAMP, and a
FactScore-style long-form biography task graded by an LLM judge against
Wikipedia. AUROC and rejection-accuracy curves are the main quality
metrics.

The final reproducibility audit found that the DeBERTa adapter used for
the original NLI runs supplied premise--hypothesis pairs through the
wrong input interface. The adapter has been corrected and the
sentence-pair reruns have since landed; they reproduce the original NLI
values to within rounding, so those values are confirmed. The main
finding comes from the
long-form biography task, where the sampled paragraphs are almost always
surface-distinct, exact-match clustering, naive sample entropy, and
surface entropy all drop to chance, and only meaning-level methods
recover a usable signal: open-judge equivalence clustering lifts both
generators well above chance (AUROC up to 0.81), and a held-out Semantic
Entropy Probe is the single strongest method for the 70B model
(AUROC~0.76), the regime where pairwise clustering is weakest. The
long-form task, not the short-answer benchmarks, is where the value of
meaning-level uncertainty is clearest. The short-answer numbers
should not be read as a close replication of Farquhar et al.; the
differences from that setup and the remaining gap are discussed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
