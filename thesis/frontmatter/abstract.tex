\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often give fluent answers that are wrong or unsupported.
They also rarely warn the reader when this happens. Semantic entropy can flag
some of these failures without retraining the model. The model answers the same
question several times, and answers that mean the same thing are placed in one
group.
Shannon entropy then measures how widely the samples are spread across the
groups. Agreement in meaning suggests lower risk, while conflicting meanings
suggest higher risk.

This thesis does not propose a new method. It reimplements the published method
by following the authors' released code. The experiments use three open
language models and three short-answer benchmarks. They ask whether semantic
entropy performs better than the methods in the original study, which step
explains any improvement, and how strongly the result depends on evaluation
choices.

On sentence-length answers, semantic entropy performs better than the original
comparison methods, so the main result reproduces. A step-by-step comparison,
however, shows that the consistent gain comes from normalizing answer strings
before counting them. With the entailment model loaded by the released code,
grouping by meaning gives no clear benefit over counting normalized strings. A
simpler method without this grouping also performs better than the original
comparison methods wherever both were tested here.

The finding depends on the entailment model. A much larger model does show a
clear benefit from grouping by meaning. The absence of a clear benefit with the
released-code model is not evidence that the methods are equivalent.

Evaluation choices change the result more than the choice between the two
methods. Changing the entailment model on the same generated answers changes the
reported score substantially. Sampling temperature changes the ranking of the
methods, and the correctness rule changes which method performs better. These
results have two further limits. The correctness judges were not checked against
human labels. In addition, the entailment model and the correctness judge both
compare meaning, which may favor a method that also groups answers by meaning.

Overall, the original comparison reproduces, but its size depends strongly on
the evaluation setup. For short answers, normalization rather than
meaning-based grouping produces the consistent gain.
