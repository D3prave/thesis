\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often produce fluent answers that are wrong or
unsupported, and they rarely warn the reader when this happens. Semantic
entropy, introduced by Farquhar et al.\ in \emph{Nature} in 2024, is a
training-free signal for that failure. The model is asked the same question
several times, the sampled answers are grouped so that answers meaning the same
thing count as one, and the spread over those meaning-groups is measured with
Shannon entropy. If the samples agree in meaning the answer is probably safe;
if they scatter across incompatible meanings it is probably not.

This thesis does not propose a new method. It reimplements the published one
against the authors' released code, on three open generators, three
short-answer benchmarks and a long-form biography task. That setup is used to
ask three questions. Does semantic entropy detect confabulations better than
the baselines the original paper evaluates? Is its advantage due to the
clustering step in particular? And how far does the answer move when
measurement choices the literature treats as settings are varied instead of
fixed?

Semantic entropy does detect confabulations better than the baselines the
original evaluates. On sentence-length answers it reaches AUROC 0.758, against
0.714 for self-evaluation and 0.694 for entropy over sequence log-likelihoods,
and the advantage over that last baseline, which is the comparison the original
leads with, reproduces.

Attributing that advantage to a single step is the thesis's contribution.
Every baseline the original
study uses differs from semantic entropy in more than one respect at once, so
that design cannot attribute the margin to any one component, although it is
routinely read as crediting one in particular. Breaking the method into four steps,
each differing from the one below it in essentially one respect, does attribute
it. The step that carries the reliable gain is not the clustering. Normalizing the answer strings before counting them is worth
$+0.040$ AUROC and is positive and resolved in all 24 slices of the evaluation
grid, the only step above the run-to-run noise. Grouping those normalized
strings by meaning adds nothing measurable on the entailment model the released
code loads. Across four prompting conditions and two independent LLM judges,
eight paired comparisons give margins between $-0.002$ and $+0.022$ AUROC. A
bootstrap that resamples both the nine independent task units and the records
within them resolves none of them. The clustering-free comparator also passes
every comparison the original study runs, with no slice anywhere going the other
way. The result is conditional in
two ways that are stated rather than hedged. Substituting a
72-billion-parameter entailment model produces a resolved margin of $+0.087$.
And on paragraph-length output no two samples are ever identical, so the
string-based scores are constant and clustering is not an improvement but a
precondition.

Measurement choices move the result by more than the choice of method does. On one fixed set of generations the
entailment model spans 0.751 to 0.823, which is three times the margin between
the methods. The sampling temperature reorders the families of detector, and
the usual setting is the most favorable point in the range. And the standard
token-overlap correctness rule reverses the sign of the headline contrast.
Two limitations bound all of this: the correctness judges were never validated
against human labels, and the entailment model and the judge answer versions of
the same question, so a method that groups by meaning is favored exactly when
correctness is scored by meaning.

The method reproduces. The number attached to it is a measurement of the
apparatus as much as of the method, and the component the margin is usually
credited to is not the component that produces it.
