\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models often produce fluent answers that are wrong or
unsupported by the input, and they rarely warn the reader when this
happens. A useful question-answering system should also be able to say
``I am not sure'' when its answer is unreliable. This thesis studies
one signal for detecting such unreliable answers: semantic entropy,
introduced by Farquhar et al.\ in \emph{Nature} (2024). The idea is simple. The
model is asked the same question several times; the sampled answers are
grouped so that answers meaning the same thing count as one; and the
spread over these meaning groups is measured with Shannon entropy. If
the samples agree in meaning, the answer is probably safe; if they
scatter across incompatible meanings, it is probably not.

The thesis does not propose a new method. It re-implements the method
of Farquhar et al.\ in a fully local, reproducible setup and asks when
the meaning-level grouping actually helps, compared with a much cheaper
baseline that only counts distinct answer strings. The evaluation runs
three open generator models (Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B)
on three short-answer tasks (TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP) and one
long-form biography task graded by an LLM judge against Wikipedia, each
decoded under three random seeds with the original paper's sampling and
labeling protocol on the NHR@FAU computing cluster. Alongside the
entropy scores it evaluates P(True), embedding regression,
semantic-entropy probes, Kernel Language Entropy, several entailment
backends, and a sampling-temperature sweep. Ranking quality is measured
with AUROC and rejection-accuracy curves; seed stability and
record-level paired-bootstrap significance are reported separately.

The answer depends on the length of the output. On short answers all
sampling-based estimators work (AUROC between 0.70 and 0.90) and reproduce the
original results under the matched protocol, but entropy over
normalized strings is the strongest single score in most cells, and no
method beats it by a practically relevant margin: a record-level
analysis shows that simple answer normalization, not semantic
clustering, performs almost all of the grouping. On long-form biography
the picture reverses. The surface baselines collapse to chance, the
meaning-aware and representation-based detectors recover a
statistically significant signal for every generator, and the choice of
clustering backend becomes result-determining, spanning chance-level to
clearly useful behavior on the same samples.

Two further effects qualify practical use. Detection power falls as the
generator scales, because larger models repeat their confabulations
more consistently, leaving less inconsistency to measure. And
sampling-based entropy and a single-pass hidden-state probe respond to
decoding temperature in opposite directions, so which detector is
stronger depends on how the model is decoded rather than being fixed.
